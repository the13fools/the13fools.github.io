What is… a blowup? · Part III of IV

Blowups in problems I know: Collisions, Tensors, and Fields

Rigid-body collisions in configuration space, diverging border-rank tensor fitting, and symmetric frame fields—taking blowups back to simulation and graphics.

Part I built the tools on curves in the plane, and Part II showed how replacing points with directions unpinches cones into smooth cylinders (and where Nash blowups fall short). Now I want to bring these tools back to problems I actually worked on in physics simulation and computer graphics.

In each domain, a classical formula breaks down: three rigid bodies collide at the same millisecond, low-rank tensor factors blow up to infinity trying to fit a target, or a smooth vector field on a disk is topologically forced to vanish. Standard engineering practice smooths over these breakdowns with penalty forces or arbitrary solver heuristics. But blowing up does something much cleaner: it excavates the singular point and tracks incoming directions.

9. Rigid-body collisions: the opening demo, unpacked

The three balls from the opening page provide our first mechanical blowup. In an elastic collision between two balls of masses $m_1, m_2$, conservation of momentum and kinetic energy uniquely determines the outgoing velocities:

m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2', \qquad m_1 v_1^2 + m_2 v_2^2 = m_1 v_1'^2 + m_2 v_2'^2.

When three balls touch simultaneously, however, there are three unknown velocities and only two conservation laws: rigid-body mechanics alone cannot pick an outcome!

Configuration space in a wedge: Write the gaps between neighbors as $g_1 = x_2 - x_1 - 2r \ge 0$ and $g_2 = x_3 - x_2 - 2r \ge 0$. In mass-weighted coordinates $q_i = \sqrt{m_i} x_i$, kinetic energy is standard Euclidean $\frac{1}{2}|\dot{q}|^2$. Removing uniform translation reduces the three-ball system to a single point bouncing inside a planar wedge between the constraint walls $g_1 = 0$ and $g_2 = 0$, with wedge angle:

\cos\beta = \sqrt{\frac{m_1 m_3}{(m_1 + m_2)(m_2 + m_3)}}.

A sequence of pairwise collisions is a billiard path bouncing between the walls. Simultaneous impact is the path striking the sharp corner $g_1 \cdot g_2 = 0$.

The blown-up bar: The color bar replaces the singular corner with all possible incoming directions—the microscopic gap ratio $g_2 / g_1$ immediately before impact. Every direction yields a deterministic sequence of bounces. The bar decomposes into chambers of shared outcomes. When masses are equal ($\beta = 60^\circ$), all incoming directions yield the exact same outcome, explaining why real cradles are predictable. With a light middle ball, different directions produce completely different orders of departure.

Inelastic collapse: When collisions have restitution $e < 1$, energy is dissipated. Below the critical threshold $e = 7 - 4\sqrt{3} \approx 0.072$ (McNamara & Young), three balls bounce infinitely many times in a finite time window! A standard pairwise impulse simulator traps itself in an infinite loop. Vouga, Smith, Kaufman, Tamstorf, and Grinspun's All's Well That Ends Well guarantees termination by detecting implied velocity equalities and blending elastic and inelastic limits.

10. Low-rank tensor factorization: collisions and border rank

In physical simulation, singularities live in configuration space: pencil strokes crossing on paper, billiard balls colliding in a corner, or the sharp tip of a cone. In each case, blowing up replaced the singular geometric point with incoming directions.

In machine learning and numerical optimization, however, the geometry lives in parameter space. What produces a singularity in parameter space? Not an external physical boundary, but internal model redundancy.

Whenever an overparameterized model has more internal capacity than the data strictly requires, different parameter configurations compute the exact same function. In low-rank matrix and tensor factorizations, factors can collapse into identical modes. When two components in a model collide, something extraordinary happens to the loss landscape: ordinary curvature goes blind.

10.1 The finite two-component collision

Here is the simplest, most explicit setting where this occurs. Our “data” consist of eight numbers: a $2\times2\times2$ symmetric tensor. The target tensor has entry $T^*_{000}=1$ and every other entry zero. We approximate it by summing two rank-one components, each defined by the vector $v(t)=(1,t)$ with tensor product $(v(t)^{\otimes3})_{ijk}=v_i(t)v_j(t)v_k(t)$:

T(a,b)=\tfrac12 v(a)^{\otimes3}+\tfrac12 v(b)^{\otimes3}.

The adjustable parameters are $a$ and $b$. At $(a,b)=(0,0)$, both components equal the target: the fit is exact ($K=0$). The loss $K$ is the sum of eight squared prediction errors, scaled by $1/3$ for convenience.

To see why pulling apart produces so little error, change coordinates to the mean $s=(a+b)/2$ and separation $d=(a-b)/2$. Moving together varies $s$; pulling apart varies $d$. Expanding the tensor entries reveals a fundamental cancellation:

  • Entries with one index equal to 1: $\tfrac{1}{2}(a+b) = s$.
  • Entries with two indices equal to 1: $\tfrac{1}{2}(a^2+b^2) = s^2+d^2$.
  • The entry with three indices equal to 1: $\tfrac{1}{2}(a^3+b^3) = s^3+3sd^2$.

Look at what happens along the separation direction ($s=0$): the linear terms $+d$ and $-d$ cancel out identically to first order! The prediction shifts only at second order ($d^2$). And because loss is squared prediction error, the resulting loss grows only as $(d^2)^2 = d^4$!

K(s,d)=s^2+(s^2+d^2)^2+\tfrac13(s^3+3sd^2)^2 \asymp s^2 + d^4.

Now evaluate the Hessian matrix at $(s, d) = (0, 0)$:

H = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial s^2} & \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial s \partial d} \\ \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial d \partial s} & \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial d^2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.

The second derivative along the separation axis is zero. The Hessian is degenerate. To quadratic order, classical calculus sees a flat valley and asserts that $d$ has zero curvature. It is completely blind to the fact that loss grows as $d^4$.

10.2 A different pathology: border rank and the fit that never finishes

Key terms: rank-one tensor, border rank, Veronese variety, tangent limit.

The collision above occurred at a finite point in parameter space. A completely different pathology occurs when a target tensor is approachable only by factors that diverge to infinity. Fitting a target $W$ with two rank-one components minimizes:

\text{minimize}\;\; E = \sum_{ijk}\big(a_{1i}b_{1j}c_{1k} + a_{2i}b_{2j}c_{2k} - W_{ijk}\big)^2.

Consider the target $W$ with $W_{001} = W_{010} = W_{100} = 1$ and all other entries 0. In the gradient-descent trajectory below, the two rank-one pieces diverge to $\pm \infty$ while nearly canceling, and the error creeps asymptotically toward zero without ever arriving!

10.3 Symmetric tensors as binary cubics: Hironaka vs. Nash

Key terms: binary cubic, twisted cubic, tangent developable, cuspidal edge.

A symmetric $2 \times 2 \times 2$ tensor corresponds to a homogeneous binary cubic with four coefficients. On the chart where the leading coefficient is nonzero, normalize it to one and write $t^3 + a t^2 + b t + c$. For distinct roots, its real symmetric rank is determined by the cubic discriminant $\Delta$:

  • $\Delta > 0$: three distinct real roots, real symmetric rank 3;
  • $\Delta < 0$: one real root, real symmetric rank 2;
  • $\Delta = 0$: repeated root (tangent developable boundary);
  • Triple root $(t - r)^3$: rank one (the twisted cubic curve $(a, b, c) = (-3r, 3r^2, -r^3)$).

The boundary $\Delta = 0$ is swept out by tangent lines to the twisted cubic—a tangent developable creased along the rank-one curve into a sharp cuspidal edge $(e^2, 2e^3)$. Hironaka's algorithm blows up the twisted cubic curve in three steps. Nash's lift records the root $r$, smoothing the crease into a flat sheet in a single step!

11. Symmetric frame fields on a disk: placing singularities

Key terms: cross field, 4-RoSy field, Poincaré–Hopf, Ginzburg–Landau vortex, Burer–Monteiro.

In computer graphics and geometry processing, quad meshing requires designing a cross field (a 4-RoSy field): at each point on a surface, we align a cross of four orthogonal directions to guide grid lines. But topology imposes strict constraints. By the Poincaré–Hopf index theorem, a disk with tangent boundary forces a total winding of $+1$. Because a simple vortex in a cross field carries winding $+1/4$, at least four singular points must exist inside the disk!

Figure {fig:crossdrag}. Drag singular points on a disk. The bars display Ginzburg–Landau energy. "Merge" forces points together, causing energy to spike quadratically.

Core structure and energy: Standard numerical optimizers minimize the Ginzburg–Landau energy:

E_\varepsilon(u) = \int_D \left( \frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^2 + \frac{1}{4\varepsilon^2}(1 - |u|^2)^2 \right) dA.

Outside the core, $|u| \approx 1$ and the field is smooth. Near the center, $|u| \to 0$ to prevent infinite Dirichlet energy $|\nabla u|^2 \sim 1/r^2$. The core cost scales as $d^2 \log(1/\varepsilon)$. Because a degree-4 vortex costs $4^2 = 16$ while four simple degree-1 vortices cost only $4 \times 1^2 = 4$, the energy violently repels merged cores, forcing them to split into four isolated $+1/4$ singularities!

Three strategies for frame fields:

  • Ginzburg–Landau Penalty: Allows the field amplitude $|u|$ to soften to zero at the core. The penalty parameter $\varepsilon$ controls the core size.
  • Drill a hole (Blowup): Punctures the disk at the core, replacing the singular point with a circular boundary $S^1$ where directions can freely rotate.
  • Lift to a sphere (Burer–Monteiro): Represents the 2D frame by a 3D unit vector $v \in S^2$. The singularity at the origin of $\mathbb{R}^2$ lifts to smooth trajectories across the equator!

What is… a blowup? · Part IV of IV

Singular learning theory

The local learning coefficient, two perfect fits, and how tensor collisions create normal-crossing divisors and logarithmic evidence.

The idea in one paragraph. Nudge a trained model's weights at random, retaining only perturbations that barely alter the loss. How much parameter room is available? In classical statistics, every minimum is assumed to be an isolated quadratic bowl, where room contracts strictly with raw parameter count ($d/2$). But neural network loss landscapes are dominated by degenerate, flat valleys of equivalent functions. The local learning coefficient $\lambda$ measures this room directly: $\lambda = d/2$ for a regular bowl, but $\lambda < d/2$ for degenerate singular minima. Singular Learning Theory proves that $\lambda$, not parameter count, dictates Bayesian generalization. And it can be measured directly with what amounts to a statistical thermometer.

13. Why singular models learn differently: two perfect fits

Consider a toy model with two parameters and two settings that fit the training data with zero error: one regular quadratic bowl ($K \propto r^2$) and one degenerate flat basin ($K \propto r^4$). Classical empirical risk cannot distinguish them. Which one does Bayesian inference select?

Figure {fig:twofits}. Posterior samples as sample size $n$ increases (blue = regular, orange = degenerate). Both achieve 0 training error. Yet as $n$ grows, the degenerate fit captures nearly all posterior mass: volume, not depth, decides the winner.

The posterior 900 samples
Share of the posterior near each fit
Key terms: equipartition, Hessian, harmonic approximation, free energy, learning coefficient.

From physics to Bayesian learning: At temperature $T = 1/n$, a particle in an empirical potential $K(w)$ visits parameter states according to the Gibbs posterior $p(w) \propto e^{-n K(w)} \varphi(w)$. In a regular quadratic well, the harmonic approximation $K(w) \approx \frac{1}{2} w^\top H w$ integrates to the Laplace volume:

V(\varepsilon) \approx \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2 + 1)\sqrt{\det H}}\,\varepsilon^{d/2}

Where classical statistics fails: In deep networks, permutation symmetries, dead units, and matrix factorizations create valleys of zero loss where the Hessian has multiple zero eigenvalues ($\det H = 0$). The Laplace formula divides by zero ($1/\sqrt{\det H} = \infty$), rendering BIC and AIC undefined.

Figure {fig:well}. Flat-bottomed well $V = (y^2 - x^3)^2$. Left: top view. Right: Hessian eigenvalues along the floor; one is identically zero, and both vanish at the cusp.

The well, from above V = (y² − x³)²
Cross-sections and Hessian

Sumio Watanabe resolved this impasse by applying Hironaka's resolution of singularities. Pulling back the loss $K(w)$ along an embedded resolution map $w = g(u)$ monomializes both the loss and the volume element into normal-crossing divisors:

K(g(u)) = u_1^{2k_1} u_2^{2k_2} \cdots u_d^{2k_d}, \qquad |J(u)| = |u_1^{h_1} u_2^{h_2} \cdots u_d^{h_d}|

The resulting partition function integrates analytically, proving that free energy and generalization error scale via the Real Log Canonical Threshold (RLCT) $\lambda$:

\lambda = \min_{i} \left( \frac{h_i + 1}{2k_i} \right), \qquad F_n \approx n S_n + \lambda\log n - (m-1)\log\log n, \qquad \mathbb{E}[G_n] \approx \frac{\lambda}{n}
  • Volume Scaling: Near a singular minimum, parameter volume shrinks as $V(\varepsilon) \asymp \varepsilon^{\lambda} (\log \frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{m-1}$. A network with $d = 100{,}000$ parameters and $\lambda = 42$ contracts under noise as if it had only $2\lambda = 84$ parameters!
  • Bayesian Occam's Razor: In model selection, the posterior evidence scales as $n^{-\lambda}(\log n)^{m-1}$. Models with smaller $\lambda$ suffer a strictly smaller complexity penalty, concentrating posterior probability into simpler, more degenerate basins.
  • Generalized Equipartition: While a quadratic bowl stores an average thermal energy of $\frac{d}{2}T$, a singular potential stores an average excess energy of $\mathbf{\lambda \cdot T}$.

14. Where singularities come from in networks: one function, many weights

Neural networks are inherently singular because the parameter-to-function map $w \mapsto f_w$ is non-injective:

  • Rescaling Symmetry: A ReLU unit satisfies $a \cdot \operatorname{relu}(wx) = (a/c)\cdot\operatorname{relu}(cwx)$ for any $c > 0$, creating 1D curves of identical functions.
  • Dead Units: If an outgoing weight vanishes ($a = 0$), all incoming weights can vary freely without changing the network's output.
  • Unit Merging: When two hidden units share identical incoming weight vectors, they collapse into a single effective unit.
  • Matrix Factorization Bottlenecks: Any product $W = BA$ exhibits a continuous $\operatorname{GL}(r)$ gauge symmetry $A \to GA, B \to BG^{-1}$.

Model problems

Linear networks are matrix factorizations: A two-layer network $y = BAx$ with hidden width $H$ factors $W = BA$. Adding hidden units increases parameter count $d = H(M+N)$ linearly. But Aoyagi & Watanabe proved that $\lambda$ plateaus at $MN/2$, the effective dimension of the rank-$r$ matrix variety itself!

15. Measuring it: the local learning coefficient

For modern neural networks, computing Hironaka blowups by hand is analytically impossible. But the Local Learning Coefficient $\lambda(w^*)$ can be measured directly using the Watanabe–Lau SGLD Estimator.

Define the localized, tempered posterior centered at trained weights $w^*$ with quadratic spring $\gamma > 0$:

p_\beta(w) = \frac{1}{Z(\beta)} \exp\left( -n\beta L_n(w) - \frac{\gamma}{2}\|w - w^*\|^2 \right)

By Hironaka's monomialization, the local partition function scales as $Z(\beta) \asymp (n\beta)^{-\lambda(w^*)} (\log n\beta)^{m-1}$. Differentiating the free energy $-\log Z(\beta)$ with respect to inverse temperature $\beta$ yields:

\frac{\partial}{\partial \beta}\big[-\log Z(\beta)\big] = n \cdot \mathbb{E}_\beta\big[L_n(w) - L_n(w^*)\big] \approx \frac{\lambda(w^*)}{\beta}

Multiplying by $\beta$ extracts the generalized equipartition relation:

\hat{\lambda}(w^*) = n\beta \cdot \mathbb{E}_\beta\big[L_n(w) - L_n(w^*)\big]

By simulating Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD) at inverse temperature $\beta^* = 1/\log n$ (Watanabe's Widely Applicable Bayesian Information Criterion, WBIC), the average thermal excess loss directly measures the local algebraic dimension $\lambda(w^*)$!

16. Tensor collisions, free weights, and the log N multiplicity

Key terms: component collision, component emptying, normal-crossing divisors, double pole.

What happens when components have free mixture weights and can collide? Consider the symmetric rank-2 cubic tensor model in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

T(a, b, \theta) = \theta\,v(a)^{\otimes 3} + (1-\theta)\,v(b)^{\otimes 3}, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 1

learning the rank-one target $T_* = e_1^{\otimes 3}$. The true tensor can be achieved in two distinct geometric ways: component collision ($a = b = 0$) or component emptying ($\theta = 0$ or $\theta = 1$).

In center and separation coordinates $s = \theta a + (1-\theta)b$ and $u = \|a - b\|^2$, the boundary where a component empties ($\theta \to 0$) becomes an exact normal-crossing product:

K_{\mathrm{sing}}(\theta, u) \sim (\theta\cdot u)^2

Integrating the evidence $Z_N = \int e^{-N K} dw$ across the intersecting divisors $\theta = 0$ and $u = 0$ yields:

Z_N \asymp N^{-1} \cdot N^{-1/2}\log N = \mathbf{N^{-3/2}\log N}

The RLCT is $\lambda = 3/2$, and the multiplicity jumps to $\mathbf{m = 2}$: the double pole of Watanabe's zeta function is the geometric intersection of component collision and component emptying!

The Rousseau–Mengersen boundary

For $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^q$ with a Dirichlet prior $\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\alpha-1}$, the RLCT is:

\lambda_{\mathrm{RLCT}} = \frac{q}{2} + \min\left(\frac{q}{4}, \; \frac{\alpha}{2}\right), \qquad m = \begin{cases} 2, & \alpha = q/2 \\ 1, & \alpha \neq q/2 \end{cases}

At the critical boundary $\mathbf{\alpha = q/2}$ (Rousseau & Mengersen 2011), component merging and emptying balance, creating the logarithmic evidence factor $N^{-\lambda}\log N$.

17. Where I'd like to take this

Hironaka's resolution theorem provides the geometric foundation for Singular Learning Theory, explaining why overparameterized networks generalize: gradient descent drives models into degenerate, highly redundant basins of low $\lambda$, which possess exponentially larger parameter volume and lower generalization error.

With resolution of singularities now formally verified in Lean 4 without a single sorry, the deep algebraic machinery that underpins learning theory has become machine-checked bedrock—a powerful milestone for mathematical physics, geometry processing, and AI safety.

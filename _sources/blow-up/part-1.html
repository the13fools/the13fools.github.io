What is… a blowup? · Part I of IV

What is a blowup?

A first example from billiards, a ladder of three singular points, the algorithm that resolves them all, and the choice-free alternative that sometimes loops forever.

Start with a demo. Two pen strokes cross. At the intersection, spatial position alone cannot tell you which stroke a point belongs to. But if you lift every point into three dimensions by its direction, the strokes separate. There are two natural directions to record: Nash's (each point's intrinsic tangent direction along its stroke, the algorithmic core of Petrov, Nabizadeh, Dodik, and Solomon's Tangent Blow-Ups for Processing Non-Manifold Geometry [2026]) and Hironaka's (the direction from a chosen center). Lifting a geometric space by directions to disentangle its singular points is a blowup.

1. A first example: the corner of a billiard table

Consider three balls on a line with gaps $g_1 = x_2 - x_1 - 2r \ge 0$ and $g_2 = x_3 - x_2 - 2r \ge 0$. In mass-weighted coordinates, their elastic motion corresponds to a single point bouncing inside a planar wedge bounded by the walls $g_1 = 0$ and $g_2 = 0$. When the point strikes a wall, rigid-body mechanics prescribes a unique specular reflection. But at the corner where $g_1 = g_2 = 0$, all three balls collide simultaneously.

Classical collision laws break down at the corner because "which ball hit first" is undefined. The resolution introduced on the opening page is to record the trajectory's direction of approach: the microscopic gap ratio $g_2 / g_1$ immediately preceding impact. Every direction yields a deterministic sequence of bounces. Replacing the singular corner by the space of directions of approach is a blowup. Geometrically, the boundary is the crossing $g_1 \cdot g_2 = 0$, an ordinary nodal singularity algebraically identical to $y^2 - x^2 = 0$.

Figure {fig:corner}. The three-ball system as a point in a planar wedge. Each bounce is a pairwise collision; the corner is all three balls touching. Around the corner, the blowup replaces the singular vertex with an arc of incoming directions, colored by the resulting sequence of bounces.

2. Singular points: crossing, touching, cusp

Key terms: zero set, normal field, smooth point, singular point.

For an algebraic curve defined by $f(x, y) = 0$, points where $\nabla f \neq 0$ are smooth: the implicit function theorem guarantees a well-defined tangent line orthogonal to the normal field $n = \nabla f / \|\nabla f\|$. Points where $\nabla f = 0$ are singular points. There the normalized gradient is undefined. A singularity can have several tangent directions, or a unique tangent with a singular parameterization, as at a cusp.

Three prototypical singularities form a natural ladder of difficulty:

  • Crossing: $f = y^2 - x^2 = 0$ (the lines $y = \pm x$). Two smooth branches crossing with distinct tangents.
  • Touching: $f = y^2 - x^4 = 0$ (the parabolas $y = \pm x^2$). Two smooth branches sharing the same tangent: order of contact 2.
  • Cusp: $f = y^2 - x^3 = 0$ (the curve $y = \pm x^{3/2}$). A single branch with non-integer scaling, coming to a sharp point.

3. Blowing up, and how it resolves each kind

3.1 The idea: record the direction

Definition: blowup of the plane, exceptional divisor, strict transform, total transform.

What is an exceptional curve? Blow up a point of a smooth surface. Above that one point, keep a whole projective line of directions. This new curve is called exceptional because the projection collapses all of it back to the old point. It belongs to the blown-up ambient surface; it is not another branch of the curve we are trying to resolve.

In the chart $y=xt$, the new coordinates are $(x,t)$ and the projection is $\pi(x,t)=(x,xt)$. For $x\ne0$, the old point determines its slope $t=y/x$. For $x=0$, every value of $t$ projects to $(0,0)$. Thus $E=\{x=0\}$ is a line of different directions above the same old point.

Figure {fig:exceptional}. Leave $x=0$ and move $t$: the point moves along the violet exceptional line at right, while its projection stays at the origin at left. Then increase $x$ to leave $E$. The orange lines are the transformed crossing $y=\pm x$; they meet $E$ at only two points, $t=\pm1$.

The dot explores the ambient surface, so it need not lie on the orange curve. The missing vertical direction is in the second chart, s = x/y. Together the two exceptional lines form ℝP¹ (a circle); over ℂ they form ℂP¹ (a sphere).

At a singular point, spatial coordinates lose information: crossing branches share $(0, 0)$. But a particle entering the origin remembers its slope $t = y/x$. The blowup of $\mathbb{R}^2$ at the origin replaces $(0, 0)$ with the circle of all directions passing through it:

\operatorname{Bl}_0(\mathbb{R}^2) = \{(p, [\ell]) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{RP}^1 : p \in \ell\}

with canonical projection $\pi(p, [\ell]) = p$. Away from the origin, $\pi$ is a diffeomorphism. Over the origin, the fiber $\pi^{-1}(0) = \{0\} \times \mathbb{RP}^1$ is the exceptional divisor $E \cong \mathbb{RP}^1$.

When a curve $C = \{f = 0\}$ is pulled back under $\pi$, the total transform $\pi^* C$ contains the entire exceptional curve $E$. Factoring out the maximal power of $E$ ($x^m$, where $m$ is the multiplicity) leaves the strict transform:

\widetilde{C} = \overline{\pi^{-1}(C \setminus \{(0, 0)\})}

which separates the branches according to their limiting slopes on $E$.

3.2 By hand: reading off Taylor coefficients

Key terms: affine charts, strict transform, simple normal crossings (SNC).

We cover $\operatorname{Bl}_0(\mathbb{R}^2)$ with two affine charts: the $x$-chart $U_x$ with map $(x, t) \mapsto (x, xt)$ (slope $t = y/x$, $E = \{x = 0\}$) and the $y$-chart $U_y$ with map $(s, y) \mapsto (ys, y)$ ($s = x/y$, $E = \{y = 0\}$). In the $x$-chart, a branch $y(x) = a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots$ has slope:

t(x) = \frac{y(x)}{x} = a_1 + a_2 x + a_3 x^2 + \cdots

At $x = 0$, the branch intersects $E$ at $t = a_1$, its initial slope. Each subsequent blowup divides by $x$ again, reading off $a_2, a_3, \dots$. The three rungs resolve as follows:

  • Crossing ($y^2 - x^2 = 0$): Setting $y = xt$ gives $x^2(t^2 - 1) = 0$. The strict transform $t^2 - 1 = 0 \implies t = \pm 1$ intersects $E$ transversely at two distinct points. Resolved in 1 step.
  • Touching ($y^2 - x^4 = 0$): Setting $y = xt$ gives $x^2(t^2 - x^2) = 0$. On $E$ ($x = 0$) both branches meet at $t = 0$, but their order of contact drops from 2 to 1: $t^2 - x^2 = (t - x)(t + x) = 0$ is now a transverse crossing. A second blowup $t = xw$ gives $w^2 - 1 = 0$, fully separating them. Resolved in 2 steps.
  • Cusp ($y^2 - x^3 = 0$): Setting $y = xt$ gives $x^2(t^2 - x) = 0 \implies x = t^2$. The curve is smooth in 1 step! But its tangent $x = 0$ is $E$ itself (a tangency). Two further blowups separate the curve from $E$ to achieve simple normal crossings (SNC), where all components cross transversely with no tangencies. Resolved in 3 steps.

3.3 Run the resolution procedure, with the boundary kept in view

Key terms: multiplicity, maximal contact, lexicographic invariant.

For the cusp, the operational question is concrete: where does the curve, together with the exceptional boundary, still fail the required normal-crossings condition? Blow up that point, substitute in both charts, factor the total transform, and inspect again. A smooth strict transform is not enough: tangencies and three components through one surface point still need work.

Where the general algorithm enters

The Bierstone–Grigoriev–Milman–Włodarczyk paper organizes resolution through marked ideals $(X,I,E,\mu)$: a smooth ambient space, equations, an exceptional boundary, and an order threshold. Its support is $\{p:\operatorname{ord}_p I\ge\mu\}$. A permissible center is smooth, lies in this support, and meets the boundary normally. After blowing up, the controlled ideal is $I'=I(E_{\rm new})^{-\mu}\pi^*I$. It may differ from the strict-transform ideal.

For our cusp we can calculate the first recursive reduction by hand:

  1. Find the highest order. For $f=y^2-x^3$, it is 2 at the origin. Use the companion marked ideal $(f,2)$ for this order-reduction task.
  2. Reduce the dimension. Since $\partial f/\partial y=2y$, the line $H=\{y=0\}$ is a hypersurface of maximal contact here. Expanding $f$ in $y$ leaves the coefficient $-x^3$ with threshold 2, represented on $H$ by $(x^3,2)$. Its support is $x=0$, so the center in the plane is $(0,0)$.
  3. Transform the marked ideal. Substitute $y=xt$: $f=x^2(t^2-x)$. Divide by $x^2$ because the mark is 2. The transformed marked ideal $(t^2-x,2)$ has empty support: its generator has order 1 everywhere. That recursive order-reduction task is finished.
  4. Return to the embedded problem. The strict curve $x=t^2$ is tangent to $E_1=\{x=0\}$. Blow up their common point with $x=ts$. The total transform becomes $t^3s^2(t-s)$. Three smooth components meet there, so blow up once more, $s=tw$, giving $t^6w^2(1-w)$. Now $w=1$ meets $E_3=\{t=0\}$ transversely, away from $E_1=\{w=0\}$.

In the last complementary chart $t=sv$, the total transform is $s^6v^3(v-1)$: the older divisor $v=0$ and the curve $v=1$ meet the new divisor $s=0$ at different points too. Both charts must be checked. The general procedure also separates exceptional monomial factors from the residual ideal, forms companion ideals, recurses on coefficient ideals, and handles boundary intersections. The scalar $3/2$ above is coefficient data in this example, not the complete invariant for all these tasks. See §3 of the 2012 paper.

What Bravo, Villamayor, Bierstone, Milman, and Włodarczyk improved

Bravo, Encinas, and Villamayor give a constructive route through principalization of ideals. Bravo and Villamayor's strengthening additionally controls the factorization of the total ideal into an exceptional factor and the strict-transform ideal.

Bierstone and Milman formulate resolution functorially for equivalent marked ideals. Jarosław Włodarczyk uses homogenized ideals to make the reduction independent of the chosen maximal-contact hypersurface. These are ways to choose compatible centers globally, beyond the substitutions in our small example.

The IAS lecture by Michael Temkin (December 8, 2025) also discusses a later approach: stack-theoretic weighted blowups. This changes the permitted operation. The Abramovich–Temkin–Włodarczyk algorithm avoids the classical exceptional-boundary history as input; it does not claim that its blowups have no exceptional fibers. 3.4 A shortcut: weighted blowups and Newton polygons

Key terms: quasihomogeneous weights, Newton polygon.

Standard blowups assign equal weight $(1, 1)$ to $x$ and $y$. But the cusp $y^2 = x^3$ has anisotropic scaling $y \sim x^{3/2}$. A weighted blowup assigns integer weights $(a, b)$ via $x = u^a, y = u^b v$. For $(a, b) = (2, 3)$, both terms match at $u^6$:

y^2 - x^3 = u^6 (v^2 - 1) = 0

For $(2,3)$, the strict equation on this chart cover is $v^2-1=0$, transverse to $u=0$. The map $x=u^2$, $y=u^3v$ has the identification $(u,v)\sim(-u,-v)$: the two displayed lifts do not mean that the cusp has two algebraic branches. A weighted blowup includes these quotient identifications and its other charts. This substitution alone is not an ordinary birational chart of a smooth surface.

The balancing weights come from the compact edge joining $(3,0)$ and $(0,2)$ of the Newton polygon: $3a=2b$. This explains the shortcut in this example; general resolution requires more than choosing weights from one picture.

3.5 What was formalized in Lean, and why

In 1964, Heisuke Hironaka proved that any algebraic or analytic variety in characteristic zero can be resolved into a smooth manifold with simple normal crossings via finitely many blowups. In Michael Atiyah's formulation required by Singular Learning Theory, any real-analytic loss $K(w) \ge 0$ can be pulled back via $\pi: M \to W$ so that loss and Jacobian determinant are simultaneously monomialized:

K(\pi(u)) = u^{2\mathbf{k}} \cdot g(u), \qquad |\det D\pi(u)| = u^{\mathbf{h}} \cdot b(u)

The exponential of a product of monomials is still coupled. Resolution makes its asymptotics tractable; it does not turn it into a product of independent Gaussian integrals:

Z_N=\int e^{-NK(w)}\varphi(w)\,dw\asymp N^{-\lambda}(\log N)^{m-1}.

With the analytic and prior assumptions in Part IV, $\lambda$ is the minimum of $(h_j+1)/(2k_j)$ over contributing charts and indices with $k_j>0$; multiplicity $m$ records the leading logarithmic factor. In October 2026, the Resolution team (Snikkers, Newgas, Hoogland, and Elliott) formalized a resolution theorem in Lean 4 without a single sorry (~500,000 lines).

4. How Nash's works, and how it fails

Key terms: Nash blowup, Gauss map, toric counterexample, recurring affine chart.

Hironaka's algorithm requires choosing a center of maximal invariant. John Nash proposed a radical, choice-free alternative: the Nash blowup $\operatorname{Nash}(X)$, defined as the algebraic closure of the Gauss map graph $\gamma: p \mapsto (p, T_p X)$ inside $X \times \operatorname{Gr}(d, N)$. On a curve $(x(\tau), y(\tau))$, it simply records the tangent slope $dy/dx$.

On the cusp $x = \tau^2, y = \tau^3$, the slope is $dy/dx = \frac{3}{2}\tau$. The Nash lift is $(\tau^2, \tau^3, \frac{3}{2}\tau)$, which is completely smooth! Nash resolves the cusp in 1 step without choosing a center. On meshes and point clouds, Petrov et al. (2026) exploit this automatic property to resolve self-intersections without needing feature detection.

Where Nash fails: In characteristic zero, iterated Nash blowups resolve curves; normalized Nash blowups resolve surfaces. These statements do not give a general termination theorem for ordinary Nash blowups on surfaces. Castillo, Duarte, Leyton-Álvarez, and Liendo construct a normal four-dimensional affine toric variety for which a chart of its Nash blowup is isomorphic to the original singular variety. The same obstruction occurs for the normalized Nash blowup:

U\subset\operatorname{Nash}(X),\qquad U\cong X.

A singular chart can therefore recur after every iteration; this is not an isomorphism between the entire Nash blowup and $X$. Their three-dimensional counterexample concerns non-normalized Nash blowups. In Part II, an exact cone blowup makes the direction coordinates concrete, before we return to these higher-dimensional obstructions.

5. Back to the opening demo: blowups on noisy data

In geometric data processing, scans touch themselves, meshes self-intersect, and point clouds cross. Petrov, Nabizadeh, Dodik and Solomon (2026) use Nash-style tangent lifts to disentangle non-manifold geometry without needing manual feature selection.

Figure {fig:noisy} tests both methods on noisy point clouds. Nash estimates tangents via local neighborhood PCA; Hironaka fits branches to locate a center. As noise increases or crossing angles narrow, Nash blurs where neighborhoods overlap both branches, while Hironaka breaks if the fitted center is unstable. Yet on clean geometry, both lifts disentangle the singularity into separated manifold sheets.

Research proposal · planted clique

Can geometry make finding a hidden clique more sample-efficient?

Summary. Below √n, finding a planted clique comes down to finding a seed: a few vertices that are surely in the clique. I propose to study whether a nonconvex energy over points on the 2-sphere can find seeds more cheaply than graph-only rankings, and to settle the question with a theorem in one direction or the other. Preliminary work, on degree-matched graphs where spectral methods recover 2 of 36 benchmark cliques and plain message passing none, shows that the energy gathers the clique well above chance but not yet strongly enough to beat random partitions or codegree. The same triangle couplings, used inside message passing, solve 35 of 36 graphs.

The plan has three aims: a Goemans–Williamson-style rounding theorem that says exactly what the geometry would have to achieve (Aim 1); rigorous state evolution for the triangle-coupled message passing that already works (Aim 2); and targeted repairs of the energy, each tested against matched controls with predictions fixed in advance (Aim 3).

1 · Specific aims

Can a low-dimensional nonlinear energy see what the analysed algorithm classes cannot?

The lower bounds for planted clique cover sum-of-squares, statistical-query and low-degree algorithms. A nonconvex flow of points on S², run with transport and rounding, is not obviously in any of these classes. The conjecture says it should still fail below √n, but no existing lower bound covers it. The long-term goal is to understand what geometric, nonconvex methods can and cannot do on average-case problems, using planted clique as the test case. The central hypothesis is that a sphere energy concentrates the clique by a measurable, analysable amount, and that this amount either decays with n in a provable way or yields a real gain in seed cost.

  • Aim 1 (theory). A rounding theorem for geometric seeds. Reduce “geometry beats √n” to a single angle condition (Lemma 4: average clique angle below 52.7°), then prove a lower bound on the flow’s concentration or show the flow is close to a low-degree function of the graph.
  • Aim 2 (theory). State evolution for dependent motif message passing. Turn the measured thresholds of (2+3)-body AMP (κ ≈ 0.647 unseeded, 0.371 with one debiased vertex) into theorems, although the triangle tensor shares edges across triples.
  • Aim 3 (experiment). Stronger concentration, judged against the right controls. Repair the three identified weaknesses of the energy and test each against random partitions and codegree at matched budget, with a go/no-go criterion that decides which direction Aim 1 pursues.

Every outcome is informative. A positive answer would give a new kind of seed-finding algorithm. A negative one would extend the low-degree picture to a class of nonconvex flows, and Aim 2 stands on its own.

2 · Background and significance · 2.1 The problem

Planted clique

Take a random graph G(n, ½): n vertices, with each pair joined independently with probability ½. Pick k of the vertices and join every pair of them. Given only the graph, find those k vertices.

The largest clique in G(n, ½) has about 2 log₂ n vertices (about 21 at n = 2,000). A planted clique larger than that is unique, and it certifies itself: any clique found that is larger than a random graph could contain must be the planted one. Every success reported here is checked this way.

Difficulty is measured by κ = k/√n. At n = 2,000, κ = 1 means a clique of about 45 vertices and κ = 0.5 means about 22.

The harder variant used here: degree-matched cliques

Planting a clique gives each of its vertices about k/2 extra neighbours, and most algorithms exploit that degree bump. Here every added clique edge is paid for by a degree-preserving swap outside the clique, so every vertex keeps exactly its original degree. Degree counts carry no signal, and spectral methods and plain message passing need κ ≈ 1, that is k ≈ √n. On the 36-graph benchmark at κ = 0.63–0.89, spectral methods recover 2 cliques and plain AMP none. Where spectral methods work on the standard problem, they fail here outright. The remaining signal is in higher-order structure, such as how triangles sit together, which is the structure this work targets. Every success reported below is at k < √n on these harder graphs.

2.2 · What is known

Spectral methods, seeds, and the √n barrier

  • Degrees (Kučera 1995). Clique vertices have about k/2 extra neighbours, which separates them once k ≳ √(n log n).
  • Spectral (Alon, Krivelevich & Sudakov 1998). The clique adds a rank-one bump of size about k/2 to a random matrix whose spectral norm is about √n. Once k ≥ C√n, the top eigenvector points at the clique. Message passing lowers the constant: AMP works for κ > 1/√e ≈ 0.61 (Deshpande & Montanari 2015).
  • Seeds (also AKS). Suppose s clique vertices were known. Restricting to their common neighbourhood shrinks the graph by 2s but the clique only by s, so κ grows by about 2s/2. Guessing the seeds by brute force costs about ns tries, which makes any constant κ solvable in time nO(log 1/κ).
  • Lower bounds. For k = n1/2−ε, sum-of-squares relaxations (Barak, Hopkins, Kelner, Kothari, Moitra & Potechin 2016, via pseudo-calibration), statistical-query algorithms (Feldman et al. 2013) and low-degree polynomial tests (Hopkins 2018) all fail. The problem is conjectured hard for every polynomial-time algorithm there.

So at constant κ, the practical question is: how cheaply can you find a seed?

Lemma 1 (restricting to a seed). Let \(G\) be \(G(n,\tfrac12)\) with a planted clique \(K\), \(|K| = k\). Fix \(S \subseteq K\) with \(|S| = s\), and let \(N(S)\) be the set of vertices outside \(S\) that are adjacent to every vertex of \(S\). Conditional on \(K\), the graph \(G[N(S)]\) is again a planted-clique instance: \[ N(S) = (K \setminus S) \,\cup\, U, \qquad \Pr[v \in U] = 2^{-s} \text{ independently for each } v \notin K, \] every edge of \(G[N(S)]\) not inside \(K \setminus S\) is an independent fair coin, and \(K \setminus S\) is a clique of size \(k-s\).

Proof. Whether \(v \notin K\) lies in \(U\) depends only on the \(s\) edges between \(v\) and \(S\). These are disjoint from the edges among \(V \setminus S\), and in \(G(n,\tfrac12)\) disjoint edge sets are independent. Hence the membership events are independent, each with probability \(2^{-s}\), and independent of every edge inside \(N(S)\). \(\square\)

Corollary 2 (the seed raises κ). \(|U| \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n-k,\,2^{-s})\). If \(2^{s} \le n^{1-\Omega(1)}\) and \(s = o(k)\), a Chernoff bound gives \(n' := |N(S)| = 2^{-s} n\,(1+o(1))\) with high probability, so \[ \kappa' \;=\; \frac{k-s}{\sqrt{n'}} \;=\; 2^{s/2}\,\kappa\,(1+o(1)). \]

Corollary 3 (a pure triangle is enough). Take \(s=3\). AMP recovers a planted clique with high probability when \(\kappa' > (1+\varepsilon)/\sqrt e\) (Deshpande & Montanari 2015). So if \[ \kappa \;>\; \frac{1+\varepsilon}{\sqrt{8e}} \;\approx\; 0.214\,(1+\varepsilon), \] AMP run on \(G[N(S)]\) for a pure triangle \(S\) recovers \(K \setminus S\). Checking each output for being a \(k\)-clique rules out false answers. Trying proposed triangles in order costs \(T \cdot \mathrm{poly}(n)\), where \(T\) is the rank of the first pure triangle. In a uniformly random order, \[ \Pr[\text{a random triangle of } G \text{ is pure}] \;\approx\; \frac{\binom{k}{3}}{\binom{n}{3}/8} \;\approx\; 8\kappa^{3} n^{-3/2}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[T] \approx \frac{n^{3/2}}{8\kappa^{3}}. \] At \(n = 2000\) and \(k = 30\), that is about 1 in 37,000. With \(s\) seeds instead of 3, \(s = \lceil 2\log_2\!\big(1/(\kappa\sqrt e)\big)\rceil + 1\) suffices for any \(\kappa>0\), which recovers the Alon–Krivelevich–Sudakov bound \(n^{O(\log 1/\kappa)}\).

So at constant κ every method here runs in polynomial time. What differs is \(T\): a good proposal source puts a pure triangle near the top of its list. Whole-graph codegree ranking (common neighbours of each pair) reaches \(T\) in the hundreds at n = 2,000. The question is whether geometry lowers \(T\) further. Lemma 1 uses the independence of the standard model. For the degree-matched graphs used in the experiments, where swaps correlate edges, Tern’s adaptive-completion lemma (appendix 4) plays the same role.

2.3 · The investigator’s approach

A solver-builder’s approach to an average-case problem

My background is in writing nonlinear solvers for low-dimensional problems. In that world, when a discrete structure is hidden in data, the usual move is to place the objects in a small continuous space. You then write an energy whose low states display the structure, and engineer a solver that reaches those states. This proposal applies that habit to planted clique.

From the standpoint of average-case complexity, this is a non-traditional approach. The lower bounds for planted clique are proved for particular classes of algorithms: sum-of-squares, statistical queries and low-degree polynomials (section 2.2). A nonconvex flow of points on a 2-sphere, run for many steps with transport and rounding, is not obviously in any of these classes. That is not a loophole, since the conjecture says no polynomial-time algorithm works below √n. But it leaves a question open rather than settled: how much can a low-dimensional nonlinear energy see that the analysed classes cannot? Aim 1 (section 5) states that question as a precise open problem.

How I arrived at the 2-sphere

I tried several formulations before settling on this one:

  • Phase codes. Each vertex carried a few complex numbers whose angles spell a binary colour code. The clique becomes one colour class of the complement graph, and a motif term rewards pairs with many shared neighbours. It beat a budget-matched null, but not message passing, and the solver kept exploiting slack in the encoding.
  • Outer-product motif energies, and clustering on higher-dimensional spheres. These included a mass-constrained “cell” that behaves like kernel k-means with a single cluster. Once the clique was gathered, these energies held it together, but from a cold start they did not find it.
  • Points on S², with triangle-area terms. This is the current formulation (section 3). Three dimensions force vertices to compete for room, and every intermediate state can be watched (replay). Here the clique gathers from a cold start, measurably above chance (section 4.1).

Since then I have been exploring energies within this formulation: springs, signed and squared triangle areas, and optimal-transport moves. The question has been which energy concentrates the clique most.

3 · Approach · 3.1 The key idea

Minimize an energy on the sphere that pulls the clique together

Place every vertex at a point on the unit sphere S². Each clique vertex lies in roughly k²/2 more edge-complete triangles than a typical vertex. An energy that shrinks complete triangles and spreads out empty ones should therefore pull the clique’s vertices toward each other. The energy has two terms: an angular spring on every edge, and a weighted squared area for every triple of points, where the weight depends on how many of the triple’s three edges exist.

E(X) = ½ ks Σij ∈ E (θij − θ0)² + (μ / n) Σa<b<c f(mabc) · area(xa, xb, xc)²,   xv ∈ S²,   mabc = Aab + Abc + Aca

Across the n = 500–4,000 benchmarks, each restart starts uniformly on S² and runs 15 cycles of the quench (f = (−16, 0.1, 0, 1), ks = 5, θ0 = 10°, μ = 10; 200 max-move gradient steps alternating with Sinkhorn transport β = 0.2 and a 0.3 swirl), splitting the cloud into four balanced clusters at tetrahedral angles (109.47°). The recorded movies in Replay prefix 6 gather cycles (f = (−8, 0.1, 0.1, 4) with non-edge repulsion) so the initial cohesion is visible before the split. The schematic below illustrates one recorded gather-then-quench run (n = 2,000, k = 35, κ = 0.78).

What a geometric corner buys—and what it costs

Landing 20 of 35 clique vertices inside a 504-vertex corner raises effective density from κ = 35/√2,000 = 0.78 to κcorner = 20/√504 = 0.89, making the subproblem substantially easier. The structural cost is hard truncation: any high-scoring triangle whose vertices straddle two corners is discarded. Section 4 measures both effects.

Two energies, used one after the other

The recorded runs minimize two different energies in sequence. Both share the edge springs and a squared-area term whose weight f(m) depends on how many of a triple’s three edges exist (m = 0, 1, 2, 3).

Stage 1 · gather (6 cycles). Pulls connected structure together.

\[ E_G = \tfrac52\!\sum_{ij\in E}(\theta_{ij}-10^\circ)^2 + 0.5\!\!\sum_{ij\notin E}\! e^{-\theta_{ij}^2/2(30^\circ)^2} + \tfrac{10}{n}\!\!\sum_{a<b<c}\! f_G(m_{abc})\,\mathrm{area}^2 + E_1 + E_2 \]

fG = (−8, 0.1, 0.1, 4), so f(3)/|f(0)| = ½. E1 pushes a vertex adjacent to neither endpoint away from an edge’s midpoint; E2 penalizes a non-edge whose endpoints share many nearby neighbours (both 15° kernels). Transport targets a sharp density (σ 5°) with half the mass uniform.

Stage 2 · quench (15 cycles). Splits the cloud into corners.

\[ E_Q = \tfrac52\!\sum_{ij\in E}(\theta_{ij}-10^\circ)^2 + \tfrac{10}{n}\!\!\sum_{a<b<c}\! f_Q(m_{abc})\,\mathrm{area}^2 \]

fQ = (−16, 0.1, 0, 1), so f(3)/|f(0)| = 1/16: the push on empty triples dominates. No repulsion, E1 or E2. Transport is smoother (σ 15°) with a 0.2 uniform share. The benchmark runs use this stage alone, from a uniform start.

How the solver runs

The state is n unit vectors in ℝ³. One cycle has two moves, and a run is 15 cycles from a uniform start:

  1. Relax. 200 projected gradient steps on the energy. In each step the vertex with the largest force moves 1°, and every other vertex moves in proportion. Because the step length is fixed, only the direction of the force matters. Rescaling all the weights together leaves a run unchanged to every digit. The real knobs are ratios: f(3)/|f(0)| and spring stiffness to μ.
  2. Transport. Estimate where small edge-complete triangles are dense: triangles within 45° of their centroid, counted on an icosphere grid and smoothed at 15°. Mix in a uniform share β = 0.2, and solve entropic optimal transport (Sinkhorn) from equal mass on the points to that density. Each point moves to its barycentric target, with a small sideways “swirl” component (weight 0.3).

At the end, spherical k-means with four clusters gives the corners that the read-outs search.

Why the gather forms two circles and the quench a tetrahedron

Both shapes follow from one identity. Let \(m = \tfrac1n\sum_v x_v\) be the cloud’s mean and \(M = \tfrac1n\sum_v x_v x_v^{\top}\) its second-moment matrix, so \(\operatorname{tr} M = 1\). For any \(n\) unit vectors, \[ \sum_{a<b<c} \mathrm{area}(x_a,x_b,x_c)^2 \;=\; \frac{n^3}{8}\Big[\,1-\operatorname{tr}(M^2)-2\,m^{\top}(I-M)\,m\,\Big]. \] (Expand \((x_b-x_a)\times(x_c-x_a) = x_a\times x_b + x_b\times x_c + x_c\times x_a\) and sum.) The bracket is at most \(2/3\), with equality exactly when \(m = 0\) and \(M = I/3\): the cloud is centred and looks the same in every direction.

In a random graph the empty triples are spread evenly over the vertices, so the large negative weight f(0) acts, to first order, like this whole sum with a minus sign. It pins the cloud at isotropy, and every recorded frame stays within 0.4% of the maximum. Within that constraint the springs, and the positive weight on complete triangles, pull points together. So the flow shrinks the cloud onto the smallest support that is still isotropic:

  • Gather: two circles at polar angles \(\arccos(\pm 1/\sqrt3) \approx 54.7^\circ\) and \(125.3^\circ\), with points spread evenly around each. This is a one-dimensional set with \(M = I/3\). The recorded runs land on 54–55° and 125–126°.
  • Quench: the four vertices of a regular tetrahedron, the smallest point set on the sphere with \(m = 0\) and \(M = I/3\). The recorded corners sit at 109–110° apart, the tetrahedral angle.

A check (K466) rules out a numerical artifact. The circles form the same way when the transport grid is rotated and when transport is switched off. Their axis is set by the random start, not by the coordinate frame, and they form on graphs with no clique too. Why the gather stops at circles rather than collapsing to four points is not yet tested. A plausible cause is its extra repulsion between non-adjacent pairs, which penalizes tight clumps. The quench drops that term.

What this means for the clique. Both shapes are set by the empty triples, which are global, and they appear on null graphs too. The clique enters only as a perturbation: which circle, or which corner, its vertices land in. That is why the corner concentration is real but modest. The isotropy constraint, not the clique, decides the overall shape.

What we understand, and what we don’t

  • The area² family is a global spread-or-collapse knob, with a sharp transition between r = 1.5 and 2. It does not single out the clique by itself.
  • The gathered clique is not a ground state. With every vertex free to move (K385), a state with the clique gathered does not have lower energy than ordinary restarts. Total energies differ by about 0.5%, and dense decoy sets sometimes end lower. So the concentration we measure is a property of the dynamics (relax, transport and the tetrahedral split) rather than of the minimizer. Any theory has to analyse the flow, not the energy landscape alone.
  • A corner assignment behaves like a degree vote. Tern’s analysis treats one exact best response with four fixed, balanced corners. Each vertex then joins the corner where it has the most neighbours. As n grows, the clique’s κ inside its best corner tends to half its whole-graph value, which is exactly the value an even split gives. One such step provably adds nothing in the limit. The concentration we observe must come from iterating the flow, or from finite size, and we cannot yet tell which.
  • Why four corners: the tetrahedron is the smallest isotropic support (box above). What sets the corner sizes, and whether a different energy would favour a corner holding most of the clique, is open.
  • The next change to the energy follows from the θ⁴ problem. A bounded reward for small complete triangles, for example a Gaussian in triangle size, would not vanish as the clique tightens.

Why a sphere?

Three dimensions and unit norm force competition for space: not every set can be close at once, so a tightly connected set has to win room. By Lagrange’s identity, a chord triangle’s squared area is quadratic in the Gram matrix G = XXᵀ, so the method is a rank-3, Burer–Monteiro-type relaxation. And every intermediate state can be looked at (replay).

Why optimal transport?

Gradient steps move a point about a degree at a time. In each cycle, entropic optimal transport (Sinkhorn) also moves every point toward the smoothed density of small complete triangles, mixed with a uniform share so the cloud does not collapse. This is the one move that relocates mass across the sphere in a single step. Its contribution to recovery has not yet been isolated by an ablation.

3.2 · Rationale: why it could save samples

Concentration multiplies the rate of pure triangles, by the cube

Suppose the quench puts a fraction \(\sigma\) of the clique into one corner that holds a quarter of the vertices. A random triangle inside that corner is then pure with probability \[ \approx \frac{8(\sigma k)^3}{(n/4)^3} \;=\; 64\,\sigma^{3}\cdot 8\Big(\frac{k}{n}\Big)^{3}, \] which is \(64\sigma^3\) times the whole-graph rate, so \(T\) shrinks by the same factor. Equivalently, inside the corner \(\kappa_{\text{corner}} = \sigma k/\sqrt{n/4} = 2\sigma\kappa\), so the corner is an easier instance whenever \(\sigma > \tfrac12\).

4 · Preliminary results

Partly: the energy gathers the clique, but not enough to give better seeds yet

Each experiment had its predictions written down before it ran, and each was paired with null G(n, ½) graphs. A success is an exact clique larger than any a random graph of that size contains (false-certificate probability below 2−45). No experiment produced a false certificate. All graphs are degree-matched, and every success is at k < √n (κ between 0.49 and 0.89), where spectral methods and plain AMP recover almost nothing. That is a constant factor below √n, not yet the n1/2−ε regime of the lower bounds. The four results below follow the argument above, step by step.

The concentration is real but usually modest. The median best-corner share corresponds to a predicted gain in pure triangles of only 1.24× over a random partition. Measured directly over 42 graphs, quench corners and random four-way partitions supplied the same number of all-clique triangle proposals: 48 each. The 8–20× gains of section 3.2 occur only on the few graphs where one corner holds half the clique or more.

4.1 The spherical quench concentrates the clique well above chance

Without labels and without a degree signal, the quench puts more of the clique into one corner than chance puts of a random k-set. Across 42 planted vs 6 null graphs (n = 1,000 and 2,000), planted enrichment has median 1.63× and reaches 2.99× (15 of 20 vertices in a 251-vertex corner at n = 1,000; and 27 of 40 in 504, 2.68×, at n = 2,000), while nulls stay at median 1.30× and never exceed 1.62× (Mann–Whitney p = 0.008; 22 of 42 planted graphs exceed the null maximum).

When decoded by an exact maximum-clique search per corner, this concentration yields direct recovery where naive baselines fail: 7 of 24 in K456 at n = 1,000 (κ 0.63–0.76) against 0 of 24 for random four-way partitions searched identically (sign test p = 0.016; 9 of 27 vs 1 of 27 when pooled with the earlier K450 pilot in the baseline table below, p = 0.022), and 10 of 12 at n = 2,000 (κ 0.67–0.89) where baseline AMP scores 0/12.

4.2 Matched controls separate partitioning from geometry

Do quench corners give triangle-proposal decoders better proposals? Not yet, once the decoder’s budget and the graph-only ranker are matched. Two controls show this.

  • The 4× call-count confound (K458h vs K464): Running a pool-based low-rank AMP decoder separately on each of the four corners initially beat a single whole-graph centered-triangle pool 34/42 vs 25/42. Auditing the budgets revealed that each corner call ran 512 AMP completions (4 × 512 total vs 1 × 512). Substituting random four-way partitions with the same four calls immediately matched the corners at 34/42 = 34/42 (4 vs 4 discordant, p = 1.0); corners also generated fewer all-clique proposals overall (48 vs 142).
  • Corner truncation vs whole-graph codegree (K458h3 & K460): With a stronger decoder that ranks triangles by pairwise codegree and completes the top 1,024, keeping only intra-corner triangles tied whole-graph codegree at 12/14 = 12/14 on the hardest graphs. When the clique gathered inside one corner, recovery arrived earlier (e.g., proposal 548 → 184); when the top planted triangle straddled two corners, filtering discarded it (proposal 18 → 403, even on a graph with 17/26 clique vertices in one corner). At budget 4,096, whole-graph codegree alone reached κ = 0.49 (5/6 at n = 2,000).

4.3 The same triangle couplings, as multi-body AMP

Asking why a triangle energy sees past pairwise spectra led to a faster algebraic method. The triangle energy splits into two orthogonal channels—centered edges and signed 3-cliques—whose state evolution explains the exact unseeded zero-gap vertex-AMP barrier: κ* = 1.000 for 2-body edges alone, 0.779 for 3-body triangles alone, 0.647 for hybrid (2+3)-body AMP, and 0.625 across all clique orders p ≥ 2.

To cross below 1/√e = 0.607 in low polynomial time, we combine channel-split (2+3)-body AMP with seed conditioning and global-degree debiasing. Inside the neighborhood N(v) of a candidate vertex (m ≈ n/2, raising local κv ≈ √2 κ), subtracting the configuration-model baseline S̃(v)uw = (2Auw − 1) − 2/(n−2)·(Δu + Δw − 1), where Δu = degG(u) − (n−1)/2, cancels global degree noise while preserving the positive internal clique shift—unlocking a non-negative exclusion-tempered denoiser on both 2- and 3-body channels (asymptotic global threshold κ* = 0.5246/√2 = 0.371). Extending the same debiasing to a candidate edge’s common neighborhood N(u) ∩ N(v) (m ≈ n/4, κuv ≈ 2κ) ranked by debiased codegree (S̃²)uv runs pure 2-body AMP in milliseconds per edge.

4.5 · Limitations of the preliminary data

What these results do—and do not—establish

  • Geometry does not yet beat the best graph-only search. Quench corners cluster the clique (p = 0.008) and beat random partitions under exact per-corner search (9/27 vs 1/27, p = 0.022), but under strong decoders they tie random 4-way partitions on equal-call AMP pools (34/42 = 34/42) and tie whole-graph codegree on the 14 hardest graphs (12/14 = 12/14).
  • Dense spherical concentration dilutes with n. At fixed κ, the clique’s share of global triangle density decays like n−3/2; at n = 4,000, κ = 0.79, single-solve quench concentration fell back to null, and at n = 4,000, κ = 0.60, codegree recovered 2/4 vs 1/4 for corners (while 1-seed (2+3)-body AMP recovered 3/4 in ~55 s).
  • Constant κ, not n1/2−ε. Every success—including 2-seed debiased AMP down to κ = 0.44 at n = 4,000 and κ = 0.51 at n = 8,000, and codegree down to κ = 0.49—lies at constant κ. Success rates line up on κ = k/√n across n = 1,000–8,000, as the planted-clique conjecture predicts.
  • Finite samples. Individual cells hold 4–11 graphs, so pooled tests (enrichment p = 0.008; exact corner search p = 0.022; K456 1-seed AMP 35/36 vs 17/36) carry the statistical weight rather than single points.

5 · Research plan

Three aims over two years

Each aim lists its hypothesis, approach, milestones, how success is judged, and what happens if it fails. Experimental milestones follow the discipline of the preliminary work: one small check first, predictions written before the run, matched null graphs, and refutations reported as such.

Aim 1 · A rounding theorem for geometric seeds

Hypothesis. Whether a sphere flow can beat √n reduces to one measurable quantity, the clique’s average pairwise angle θ̄ after the flow. Recursive hyperplane rounding gains a constant factor in κ per level whenever θ̄ < π(1 − 1/√2) ≈ 52.7° (Lemma 4, below).

Approach. (a) Measure θ̄ as a function of κ and n on the recorded flows, to see which side of 52.7° they sit and how that moves with n. (b) Positive direction: a rounding analysis lower-bounding the flow’s concentration in κ and n, starting from the fixed-background case. There, Tern’s analysis shows that one best response on four fixed corners gives no asymptotic gain (its κ gain tends to ½); the question is whether iterating the flow does better. (c) Negative direction: regress the corner map A ↦ corners(A) onto orthogonal graph polynomials of growing degree D, and, if the fit holds at D = O(log n), prove that the flow is close to a low-degree function, so the low-degree bounds apply. (d) Identify the convex lift: because area² is quadratic in G = XXᵀ, the energy is a rank-3 Burer–Monteiro relaxation of a degree-4 Gram program; compare its power with the rank-3 restriction.

Milestones. M1.1 θ̄(κ, n) on existing traces. M1.2 low-degree regression for D = 1…6 at n up to 4,000. M1.3 a proved statement in the direction the Aim 3 go/no-go selects.

Success. A theorem in either direction. Risk and fallback. The full adaptive flow may resist analysis; the fallback is a theorem for the one-step, fixed-background flow, which is already a well-posed problem.

Aim 2 · Rigorous state evolution for dependent motif AMP

Hypothesis. The heuristic state evolution of channel-split (2+3)-body AMP is asymptotically exact, even though the triangle tensor Tijk = SijSjkSki shares edges across triples and so violates the i.i.d. assumptions of tensor AMP. Preliminary support: the step-3 null standard deviation measures 0.993 against a predicted 1, and the method solves 35 of 36 benchmark graphs.

Approach. Derive the Onsager correction and field-covariance laws for tensors built from one random sign matrix, first for the triangle channel and then for non-backtracking cycle channels, checking each step numerically against the benchmark and larger n.

Milestones. M2.1 numerical state-evolution checks up to n = 16,000. M2.2 a proof for the triangle channel. M2.3 extension to cycle channels and to degree debiasing.

Success. Proved thresholds matching the measured 0.647 (unseeded) and 0.371 (one debiased vertex). Risk and fallback. If edge sharing changes the recursion, quantify the correction terms; a corrected recursion that matches experiment is itself the result.

Aim 3 · Stronger concentration, judged against the right controls

Hypothesis. The concentration is limited by three identified mechanisms: the area² pull on a tightening triangle vanishes as θ⁴; raw triangle density is 97.5% shared edge noise (appendix 2); and Sinkhorn transport amplifies within-corner splitting 18.89× (appendix 3). Repairing them gives corners that beat random partitions and codegree.

Approach. One change at a time: a bounded small-triangle reward; the Walsh-clean (1, 0, −1, 2) triangle field; anchored transport. Ablations first: transport off (K466 found the gather’s two circles form without it), and why the gather stops at circles rather than corners. Soft reweighting of triangle scores has been tested (K463) and gave no net gain over codegree, so the concentration itself has to get stronger.

Go/no-go. Over at least 40 graphs, a repaired energy must supply more all-clique triangle proposals than random four-way partitions (p < 0.05), and recover the clique earlier than codegree at κ ≤ 0.45. The bar is concrete: at n = 2,000 and κ = 0.45, codegree triangles with a branching decoder recover 4 of 6 cliques where random triangles at the same budget recover none (K462). A pass sends Aim 1 toward the positive direction; a fail sends it toward the negative one.

Risk and fallback. If no repair passes, the negative result is reported as such and becomes the empirical input to Aim 1’s low-degree argument.

Aim 1, the target statement: a rounding theorem for corners, in the style of Goemans–Williamson

Goemans and Williamson analysed MAX-CUT by embedding the vertices as unit vectors, cutting with a random hyperplane, and bounding the probability that each pair is separated. The same template gives a precise statement of what a geometric method would have to do to beat √n.

Lemma 4 (hyperplane rounding keeps the clique together). Let \(x: V \to S^{d-1}\) be any embedding and let \(\bar\theta\) be the average angle \(\angle(x_u, x_v)\) over ordered pairs of distinct clique vertices. Cut with a uniformly random hyperplane through the origin, and let \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2\) be the fractions of the clique on the two sides. Then \[ \mathbb{E}\big[\max(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)\big] \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}\big[\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2\big] \;=\; \frac1k + \Big(1-\frac1k\Big)\Big(1-\frac{\bar\theta}{\pi}\Big). \]

Proof. Because \(\sigma_1+\sigma_2 = 1\), \(\max_i \sigma_i \ge \sum_i \sigma_i^2\). The sum \(\sum_i \sigma_i^2\) is the probability that two clique vertices drawn independently and uniformly, with replacement, land on the same side. They are the same vertex with probability \(1/k\). Otherwise, the Goemans–Williamson lemma says a random hyperplane separates \(x_u\) and \(x_v\) with probability \(\angle(x_u,x_v)/\pi\). Averaging over pairs gives the identity. \(\square\)

Consequence for recursion. If the side holding more of the clique has at most \((\tfrac12+\eta)n\) vertices, the clique’s κ on that side is \(\kappa' \ge \sqrt{2/(1+2\eta)}\;\sigma_{\max}\,\kappa\). In expectation this is a fixed gain \(\kappa' \ge (1+\delta)\kappa\) per level whenever \[ \bar\theta \;<\; \pi\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big) \;\approx\; 52.7^\circ \quad(\text{with } \eta \text{ small}). \] Recursing on both sides for \(L = \lceil \varepsilon \log_2 n / \log_2(1+\delta) \rceil\) levels raises \(\kappa = n^{-\varepsilon}\) to a constant, at a cost of \(2^{L} = n^{\varepsilon/\log_2(1+\delta)}\) branches. That is polynomial. Corollary 3 then finishes.

The open problem. Is there a polynomial-time embedding of \(G\) that, at \(k = n^{1/2-\varepsilon}\), keeps the clique’s average pairwise angle below about 52.7° while splitting the other vertices evenly, and keeps doing so on the subgraphs it produces (each level selects its subgraph from the graph, so adaptivity has to be controlled)? Such an embedding would refute the planted-clique conjecture. Convex embeddings cannot do it: the Lovász θ function (Feige & Krauthgamer) and sum-of-squares (Barak et al.) do not see the clique at that size. A theorem is within reach in either direction:

  • Positive: a rounding analysis of the nonconvex sphere flow that lower-bounds its clique concentration as a function of κ and n. The experiments measure that quantity, and at constant κ it is real but modest.
  • Negative: a proof that the flow’s embedding is close to a low-degree polynomial in \(A\). Then the low-degree bounds apply, and no motif flow of this kind can reach \(n^{1/2-\varepsilon}\).

Appendix · analysis

Four analyses of the dynamics

Four analyses address why the flow concentrates at n ≤ 2,000, why the concentration dilutes at n = 4,000, and what limits triangle-based decoders. Analyses 2–4 are derivations written up in the agents’ notes. Analysis 1 is a heuristic scaling argument, not a proof.

1. Free-vertex 3-body spin-glass scaling (Θ(√n), not n2/5)

Holding background vertices fixed on S² suggests a gathered clique beats noise once k³ ≫ k√n + √k·n, i.e. k ≫ n2/5. Letting all 2n spherical coordinates relax changes this: dense mean-zero 3-body couplings act as a 3-spin glass whose noise ground state reaches Enoise ≈ −c n² (~c n per vertex). Pinning k clique vertices forfeits ~c k n of noise-lowering freedom, giving the global-minimum balance k³/48 ≳ c k n, i.e. k ≳ C√n, consistent with the n = 600 and n = 4,000 runs.

2. Walsh chaos of triangle density (97.5% edge noise)

Expanding raw local triangle excess in edge signs Xe = 2Ae − 1 gives 8R = Σe de Xe + Σt ht(XaXb + XbXc + XcXa + XaXbXc). Shared single-edge fluctuations account for (m−2)/(m−2+4/3) = 97.5% of local variance in a 54-vertex patch. Projecting out the edge term yields the unique Cauchy–Schwarz-optimal pure 3-clique field with triple weights (1, 0, −1, 2)—and its exact edge-null sphere potential f = (−1, −3, −5, 1).

3. Exact Sinkhorn splitting instability (18.89×)

On an ideal 4-partite tetrahedral configuration (109.47° corner spacing), both the graph energy and Sinkhorn substeps have an exact fixed point, yet zero-mean within-corner perturbations amplify under the shared barycentric transport Jacobian DF(x) = [2/(ε‖m‖)](I − FFᵀ) Covp(g). A continuum bound gives a splitting multiplier g ≥ (11/96)·(2/ε) ≈ 7.64, and it measures 18.89 on the icosphere mesh, which explains why unanchored masked transport destabilizes clean corners once local triangles disappear.

4. Uniform adaptive completion & codegree barriers

Using ε-net spectral bounds uniform over all graph-dependent subsets, any corner P of size m admits deterministic spectral + two-threshold completion on the same graph (even under degree-preserving switches) whenever s ≥ C√(m log(en/m)), and r = ⌈log₂(n/k²)⌉ + O(1) pure seed vertices suffice uniformly. Conversely, at k = n1/2−ε, centered codegree places zero planted pairs inside the top n1+δ edges (δ < 2ε) with high probability.

This program was conducted as an experiment in human-directed, multi-agent mathematical research inside one shared workspace.

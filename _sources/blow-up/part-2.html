What is… a blowup? · Part II of IV

3-Manifolds, Hironaka's radial excavation, and why Nash loops in 4D

Visualizing zero level sets in four dimensions as 3-manifolds, how Hironaka's blowup 1 excavates a pinched throat, and why Nash gets trapped in a 4-step periodic orbit.

Part I built our intuition on plane curves: lifting points by their direction untangles crossing lines, reduces the contact between touching parabolas, and smooths out cusps. But Hironaka's 1964 resolution theorem is monumental precisely because it works in every dimension. In machine learning, the parameter spaces of neural networks live in hundreds or thousands of dimensions, and even the simplest non-trivial geometric failures appear only when we reach four dimensions.

At the close of Part I, we mentioned that John Nash's choice-free blowup—which requires no manual selection of a center—fails in dimension four and higher (Castillo, Duarte, Leyton-Álvarez, and Liendo, Annals of Mathematics 2026). In Part I that counterexample was presented only as an algebra table, because the toric variety lives in seven dimensions. But how can we actually see what goes wrong? How does a higher-dimensional singularity look, why does Nash get stuck, and what exactly does Hironaka do in his first blowup?

The answer is to view the zero level set as a 3-manifold. By taking 3D spatial slices through 4D space, we can watch both algorithms operate side by side. 6. Zero level sets in 4D as 3-manifolds

New terms: 3-manifold, hypersurface, regular point, singular locus, slice.

A single polynomial equation in four variables, $F(x, y, z, w) = 0$, defines a three-dimensional geometric space sitting inside $\mathbb{R}^4$: a hypersurface, or 3-manifold. At any point where the gradient vector

\nabla F = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial z}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial w}\right) \neq (0, 0, 0, 0)

is non-zero, the implicit function theorem guarantees that the solution set is locally smooth. If you zoom in close enough to a regular point, the 3-manifold looks completely indistinguishable from flat three-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^3$. It possesses a unique, well-defined tangent 3-plane perpendicular to $\nabla F$.

The trouble begins at points where the gradient vanishes entirely: $\nabla F = 0$. There, the implicit function theorem provides no guidance. The tangent space collapses, and the geometry develops a singularity. Depending on the equation, this can be an isolated pinch point (like the apex of a double cone), two sheets kissing with high-order tangency (a tacnode), or whole lines and planes of singular points.

To visualize a 3-manifold, we do what magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) does to a three-dimensional brain: we slice it along coordinate planes. Fix $w = 0$ (or a constant time-slice $w = c$) and examine the resulting two-dimensional surface in the remaining three coordinates $(x, y, z)$. For instance:

  • The A₁ Conifold: $x^2 + y^2 - z^2 - w^2 = 0$. At $w = 0$, this is the cone $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$. Two conical nappes meet at an isolated sharp apex $(0, 0, 0, 0)$. The neck radius at the waist is strictly zero.
  • The A₃ Tacnode: $x^2 + y^2 = z^4 + w^4$. At $w = 0$, the radius in the $(x, y)$-plane is $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2} = z^2$. Instead of straight conical rays, two parabolic sheets kiss with second-order contact at the origin.
  • The Fermat Cubic: $x^3 + y^3 + z^3 + w^3 = 0$. Slicing at $w = 0$ gives a fluted cone with 3-fold symmetry, radiating 27 lines from a triple-point singularity.
  • The Brieskorn Cusp: $x^2 + y^2 = z^3 + w^2$. The cross-section has a fractional scaling exponent $r \propto |z|^{3/2}$, creating a 3D needle tip.

7. What Hironaka actually does in Blow-Up 1: Radial excavation

New terms: blowup center, exceptional divisor, radial excavation, strict transform, normal crossing.

When algebraic geometers say "blow up the singular point", what is the actual mathematical surgery being performed? Why does it turn a pinched cone into an open, smooth surface?

Consider the conical double point $X^2 + Y^2 - Z^2 = 0$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. At the origin, the gradient is $(2X, 2Y, -2Z) = (0, 0, 0)$. The origin has lost its direction: rays approach it from every angle in the cone $X^2 + Y^2 = Z^2$.

Step 1: Write in direction coordinates. Choose the origin as the center. In spherical coordinates, every point is written as a radius $R$ and a direction $(\xi, \eta, \zeta)$ on the unit sphere $S^2$ with $\xi^2 + \eta^2 + \zeta^2 = 1$. In standard affine charts, we set $X = u$, $Y = uv$, $Z = uw$, where $u$ plays the role of the radial distance from the center, and $(v, w) = (Y/X, Z/X)$ record the directions of approach.

Step 2: Substitute and factor out the center. Substitute these coordinates into the cone's equation:

F(u, uv, uw) = u^2 + (uv)^2 - (uw)^2 = u^2\big(1 + v^2 - w^2\big) = 0

Notice what happened: a common factor of $u^2$ factored out cleanly from every term! That factor $u^2 = 0$ is the exceptional divisor $E_1$. It is the algebraic memory of the origin: the origin has been replaced by the entire projective sphere of directions $\mathbb{RP}^2$.

Step 3: The strict transform is smooth. What remains after factoring out $u^2$ is the strict transform:

1 + v^2 - w^2 = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w^2 - v^2 = 1

This is no longer a cone! It is a smooth one-sheeted hyperboloid. Its gradient with respect to $(u, v, w)$ is $(0, -2v, 2w)$, which never vanishes on the surface because $w^2 - v^2 = 1$ ensures $w \neq 0$.

The physical intuition: radial excavation. Hironaka's blowup 1 does not merely redraw the coordinates; it physically excavates the singular apex. It drills out the point $R = 0$ where the equations broke down and glues in a smooth sphere of directions. In Figure {fig:manifold3d}, watch the waist radius jump from $R = 0.00$ to $R = 0.92$: traffic can now flow smoothly through the throat! The exceptional divisor $E_1$ (the cyan ring) sits right at the neck, marking where the point was removed.

For higher-order singularities like the $A_3$ Tacnode ($x^2 + y^2 = z^4$), two parabolic sheets kissed with fourth-degree contact. The first blowup factors out $\rho^2$, widening the throat and reducing the tangency from degree 4 to degree 2 (kissing parabolas become crossing cones). A second blowup then factors out the remaining quadratic factor, opening the crossing into two smooth, non-intersecting necks. That is why Hironaka's loop is guaranteed to terminate: every step strictly reduces the order of contact.

8. The 4D failure of Nash: Why choice-free blowups loop forever

New terms: Nash blowup, Gauss map, Grassmannian fiber, limit cycle, toric counterexample.

In the 1950s, John Nash proposed a wonderfully egalitarian alternative to Hironaka. Why should a mathematician have to make arbitrary choices about where to place the centers of blowups? If an algorithm is to run automatically inside a geometry engine, on a 3D scan, or during neural network training, it should not require a human to inspect the geometry and choose centers.

Nash's idea: Take a $d$-dimensional shape $X \subset \mathbb{R}^N$. At every smooth point $p$, the tangent space is a $d$-dimensional plane $T_p X$. That plane is a single point in the Grassmannian $\operatorname{Gr}(d, N)$, the space of all $d$-dimensional planes in $N$-dimensional space. This gives the Gauss map $\gamma: p \mapsto T_p X$. At singular points, $\gamma$ is undefined. Nash proposed taking the closure of the graph of $\gamma$:

\widetilde{X}_{\mathrm{Nash}} = \overline{\big\{(p, T_p X) : p \in X_{\mathrm{smooth}}\big\}} \subset X \times \operatorname{Gr}(d, N)

Over each singular point, Nash attaches all possible limiting tangent spaces. It requires no center, no chart choices, and no human intervention.

For curves (dimension 1), Nash's method works miracles. In Part I we saw that for the cusp $y^2 = x^3$, one Nash step immediately produces the smooth space $(\tau^2, \tau^3, 3\tau/2)$. In 1990, Mark Spivakovsky proved that for surfaces (dimension 2), alternating Nash blowups with normalization always terminates in a smooth surface. It was widely conjectured that Nash blowups would resolve singularities in all dimensions.

The 2026 counterexample. In 2024 and published in the Annals of Mathematics in 2026, Ana Belén de Felipe, Daniel Duarte, Pedro Fortuny Ayuso, and Antonio Liendo discovered that Nash's dream breaks down starting in dimension four. They constructed a 4-dimensional toric variety $X \subset \mathbb{R}^7$ whose Nash blowup fails to make any progress.

Why Nash gets trapped: the geometry of the cycle. Unlike Hironaka, Nash does not excavate the singular point. It retains the original positions and merely appends the limiting tangent hyperplanes. When the singularity is a higher-order pinch—such as our $A_3$ Tacnode or the toric cone of Castillo et al.—the Gauss map $\gamma$ folds over itself in the Grassmannian $\operatorname{Gr}(3, 4)$.

Watch the right-hand panel of Figure {fig:manifold3d}:

  • Step 0 ($M^3_0$): The initial pinched cone sits at the origin with waist $r = 0$.
  • Step 1 ($M^3_1$): Nash records tangent planes. Because the normal field folds along the crease, the blown-up chart tilts the cone along its first axis. But the apex is not excavated; it remains pinched at $r = 0$.
  • Step 2 ($M^3_2$): The second Nash blowup shears the cone along its transverse coordinates, rotating the crease in $\operatorname{Gr}(3, 4)$.
  • Step 3 ($M^3_3$): A unimodular integer coordinate transformation $U \in \operatorname{GL}(4, \mathbb{Z})$ maps the exponent fan of $M^3_3$ identically back to $M^3_0$!

The sequence has closed into an infinite periodic limit cycle of length 4:

M^3_0 \;\xrightarrow{\text{Nash}}\; M^3_1 \;\xrightarrow{\text{Nash}}\; M^3_2 \;\xrightarrow{\text{Nash}}\; M^3_3 \;\cong\; M^3_0 \;\xrightarrow{\text{Nash}}\; \cdots

Because Nash makes no choices, it cannot break symmetry: on the next step it repeats the exact same sequence. It is trapped forever, like a traveler on a four-dimensional Penrose staircase. No measure of complexity drops, because the fourth chart is literally isomorphic to the first.

The takeaway for practitioners: When designing algorithms for non-manifold meshes, neural network loss surfaces, or physical simulators, Nash's choice-free approach is tempting because it requires no heuristics. But in dimensions four and above, choice-free methods can march in circles. Hironaka's algorithm succeeds because it insists on picking a center where the invariant is strictly worst, excavates the singularity radially, and proves that its invariant drops at every step.

With this geometric understanding of level sets, radial excavations, and 4D tangent failures in hand, we are ready to bring these tools back to problems where I have hands-on intuition: colliding physical bodies, low-rank tensor factorizations, and symmetric cross fields.

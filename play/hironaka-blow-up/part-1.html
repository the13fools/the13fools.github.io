What is… a blowup? · Part I of IV

What is a blowup?

A first example from billiards, then a ladder of three singular points, the algorithm that resolves them all, and the choice-free alternative that sometimes loops forever.

Start with a demo. Two pen strokes cross. At the crossing, a point's position no longer tells you which stroke it is on. So give every point one more number, a direction, and lift it to that height. There are two natural choices of direction. Nash's: each point's own direction along its stroke, estimated from its neighbors (the computational core of Petrov, Nabizadeh, Dodik, and Solomon's Tangent Blow-Ups for Processing Non-Manifold Geometry [2026]). Hironaka's: the direction from one chosen center, here found by fitting the strokes. Either way the two strokes have different directions at the crossing, so in the lift they no longer meet. Lifting a shape by directions so that its singular points come apart is a blowup. This part is about why that works, when it has to be repeated, and when it can fail.

1. A first example: the corner of a billiard table

Start with the demo on the opening page. Three balls on a line with positions $x_1 \le x_2 \le x_3$ can be described by a single point moving in a plane. Every elastic collision between two adjacent balls is that point bouncing off a planar boundary. There are two non-penetration gap constraints: $g_1 = x_2 - x_1 - 2r \ge 0$ (balls 1 and 2 touch when $g_1 = 0$) and $g_2 = x_3 - x_2 - 2r \ge 0$ (balls 2 and 3 touch when $g_2 = 0$). In mass-weighted coordinates where the kinetic energy metric is flat Euclidean, these two walls meet at a sharp wedge corner where all three balls touch simultaneously (section 9 does the full mechanics).

Away from the corner the physical rules are unambiguous: a ball pair collides, and the point reflects specularly off the boundary hyperplane. At the corner the classical rules go completely silent: which wall did the point "hit first"? Simulators confront this as the notoriously ill-posed problem of simultaneous rigid-body impact; Vouga, Smith, Kaufman, Tamstorf, and Grinspun's All's Well That Ends Well is the reference I know best for resolving it robustly.

The resolution in that demo was to ask how the trajectory approached the corner: what was the ratio of the two microscopic gaps $g_2 / g_1$ immediately before impact? That ratio is a direction of approach in the plane. Every direction gives a unique, deterministic sequence of infinitesimal bounces. Replacing a singular point by the space of directions of approach to it is a blowup, and this billiard corner is the simplest possible case. Geometrically, the boundary walls form the zero set of the polynomial $g_1 \cdot g_2 = 0$: two straight lines crossing like the letter X. After an affine change of coordinates, this is algebraically identical to the nodal crossing $y^2 - x^2 = 0$. The billiard feels non-smooth because of the velocity reflections, but the underlying geometry is an ordinary crossing singularity: two smooth branches meeting with distinct tangents. It is the first rung of our ladder.

So the natural next question: what other kinds of singular points are there, and does recording directions fix them all? The rest of Part I climbs a ladder of three examples: lines that cross, curves that touch, and a cusp.

2. Singular points: crossing, touching, cusp

New terms: zero set, normal field, smooth point, singular point.

In graphics I mostly meet singularities of fields. Normalize a gradient into a direction field, n = ∇u/|∇u|, and n is undefined wherever ∇u = 0; a smooth cross field on a disk has to have such points, which is where irregular vertices come from. Hironaka's theorem is about something that looks different but is the same thing seen from another angle: singular points of shapes defined by equations.

Take a polynomial f(x, y). The points where f = 0, its zero set, usually form a curve. Near a point where ∇f ≠ 0, the implicit function theorem says the curve is smooth: zoom in and it looks like a straight line, perpendicular to ∇f. So the curve carries a normal field ∇f/|∇f|, and its singular points are exactly the points of the curve where that field has a singularity, ∇f = 0. There the curve has no tangent line, and every tool that leans on the implicit function theorem (parametrizing, integrating along the curve, Newton's method) breaks.

Three examples make up the ladder, each a little harder than the last:

  • Crossing: f = y² − x², the lines y = x and y = −x. Two branches through the origin, in different directions. (The billiard corner is this.)
  • Touching: f = y² − x⁴, the parabolas y = x² and y = −x². Two branches through the origin in the same direction: they are tangent.
  • Cusp: f = y² − x³, the curve y = ±x3/2. One branch that comes in, stops, and goes back out, with a fractional exponent.

3. Blowing up, and how it resolves each kind

Three kinds may look like a small zoo. It is: plane curves alone have infinitely many kinds of singular point (y² = xk for every k, three or more branches through one point, and so on), surfaces add cones, pinch points and whole curves of singular points, and nothing stops at codimension one; Hironaka's theorem covers shapes of any dimension cut out by any number of equations, including the set of perfect parameters of a neural network in Part IV. A classification is hopeless, which is why the algorithm of section 3.3 never asks what kind of point it is looking at. It measures how bad the point is and blows up the worst place. The three rungs here were chosen because each shows a different thing a single blowup can do: separate, reduce, or smooth.

3.1 The idea: record the direction

New terms: center, exceptional curve, strict transform, total transform, Hironaka's blowup, Nash's blowup.

The problem. At a singular point the equation $f(x, y) = 0$ has lost information. Two crossing lines share the same spatial coordinates at the origin $(0, 0)$, so position alone cannot distinguish them. Yet any particle traveling along a branch knows perfectly well which line it is on: it arrives from a specific direction. Spatial position forgot what the direction still remembers.

The geometric fix. Choose a point to serve as the center; here, the origin $(0, 0)$. Every other point $p = (x, y) \neq (0, 0)$ in the plane lies on a unique line $\ell$ passing through the origin, with slope $t = y/x$. The blowup replaces the plane with the set of all pairs $(p, [\ell])$ where $\ell$ is a line through the origin that contains $p$.

Formal definition. The blowup of the plane at the origin is the incidence manifold
\operatorname{Bl}_0(\mathbb{R}^2) = \{(p, [\ell]) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{RP}^1 : p \in \ell\},
equipped with the canonical projection (the blowdown map) $\pi: \operatorname{Bl}_0(\mathbb{R}^2) \to \mathbb{R}^2$ given by $\pi(p, [\ell]) = p$. Over any point $p \neq (0, 0)$, there is exactly one line through the origin and $p$, so $\pi^{-1}(p)$ is a single point: $\pi$ is a diffeomorphism everywhere away from the origin. But over the origin $p = (0, 0)$, every line through the origin qualifies. The fiber $\pi^{-1}(0) = \{0\} \times \mathbb{RP}^1 \cong \mathbb{RP}^1$ is an entire circle of directions: this is the exceptional curve (or exceptional divisor) $E$. In general, one blows up a smooth submanifold $Z$ inside a manifold $M$ by replacing each point of $Z$ with the projectivized normal space $\mathbb{P}(N_{Z/M})$.

Total vs. Strict Transform. When we pull an algebraic curve $C = \{f(x, y) = 0\}$ back under $\pi$, we obtain the total transform $\pi^* C = \{ (p, [\ell]) : f(\pi(p, [\ell])) = 0 \}$. Because $f(0, 0) = 0$, the total transform always contains the entire exceptional curve $E$. To recover the curve itself, we take the topological closure of the lifted points from outside the origin:

\widetilde{C} = \overline{\pi^{-1}(C \setminus \{(0, 0)\})}.
This $\widetilde{C}$ is the strict transform (or proper transform). In coordinates, this corresponds to factoring out the maximal power of the exceptional curve's local equation (dividing out $x^m$, where $m$ is the multiplicity of $f$ at the origin). What remains is the genuine geometric lift.

Nearby in $\operatorname{Bl}_0(\mathbb{R}^2)$ means nearby as pairs: two points are close when both their positions and their lines of direction are close. Two trajectories entering the origin along the branch of slope $+1$ converge to $(0, [\text{slope } +1])$; along the branch of slope $-1$, they converge to $(0, [\text{slope } -1])$. On the exceptional divisor $E$, these are two distinct, separated points. Plot the direction as a third spatial coordinate (height) and you get a lift of the curve into three dimensions.

There are two natural directions to record:

  • Hironaka's blowup: records the direction from a chosen center, $[\ell] = [x : y]$, as defined above.
  • Nash's blowup: records each point's own intrinsic tangent direction: pairs $(p, T_p C)$ at smooth points, plus all their limiting tangent spaces at singular ones. It requires no center at all.

How is this different from a covering space? In geometry processing, cross fields are commonly lifted to the quad cover (Kälberer, Nieser and Polthier's QuadCover), four copies of the surface glued so that the four directions of a cross become one continuous vector field (the cube cover of Nieser, Reitebuch and Polthier does the same for octahedral frames). A cover modifies the entire manifold at once, creating several sheets to resolve the field's discrete symmetry; the singularities remain fixed as branch points. A blowup operates locally: it takes a single copy, modifies only the chosen center, and replaces that single point with its projectivized tangent space. It resolves the shape, not the symmetry. The two combine naturally: the drilled boundary cylinder of section 11 is a blowup of a cross field's singular core, whose boundary circle is subsequently analyzed on the quad cover.

3.2 By hand: each blowup reads off one more coefficient

New terms: affine chart, strict transform, total transform, order of contact, simple normal crossings.

Because $\mathbb{RP}^1$ cannot be covered by a single coordinate chart (vertical lines have infinite slope $t = y/x = \infty$), we cover the blowup $\operatorname{Bl}_0(\mathbb{R}^2)$ using two overlapping coordinate charts:

  • The x-chart $U_x$ (non-vertical directions): coordinates $(x, t)$ with the map $\pi(x, t) = (x, xt)$. Here $t = y/x$ is the slope, and the exceptional divisor $E$ is given by $x = 0$.
  • The y-chart $U_y$ (non-horizontal directions): coordinates $(s, y)$ with the map $\pi(s, y) = (ys, y)$. Here $s = x/y$ is the reciprocal slope, and $E$ is given by $y = 0$.

On the overlap where $x \neq 0$ and $y \neq 0$, the transition is simply $s = 1/t$. To compute the blowup of an algebraic curve $f(x, y) = 0$, we substitute the chart map into the polynomial. If the curve has multiplicity $m$ at the origin (its lowest non-zero terms have total degree $m$), then $x^m$ factors out completely:

f(x, xt) = x^m \cdot \widetilde{f}(x, t) = 0.

The equation $x^m \cdot \widetilde{f}(x, t) = 0$ is the total transform: the factor $x^m$ represents the exceptional divisor $E = \{x = 0\}$ appearing with multiplicity $m$. Dividing out $x^m$ leaves the strict transform $\widetilde{f}(x, t) = 0$. Where does the strict transform intersect the exceptional divisor? Set $x = 0$ in $\widetilde{f}(x, t) = 0$: the roots $t_i$ of $\widetilde{f}(0, t) = 0$ are precisely the slopes of the tangent lines to the branches at the origin! The ultimate goal of resolution is simple normal crossings (SNC): the strict transform and all exceptional divisors must be nonsingular, meeting only transversely like the coordinate axes, with no tangencies and no three components intersecting at a common point.

The core algebraic intuition: a blowup divides by $x$, so it reads off the next Taylor coefficient. Write a smooth branch as a series $y(x) = a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3 + \cdots$. In the $x$-chart, the slope variable is:

t(x) = \frac{y(x)}{x} = a_1 + a_2 x + a_3 x^2 + \cdots

At $x = 0$, the branch lands on $E$ at height $t = a_1$, its initial slope. A second blowup divides by $x$ again, reading off $a_2$, and a third reads off $a_3$. Two branches that share initial terms separate at the very first blowup where their Taylor coefficients differ.

  • Crossing, $f = y^2 - x^2 = 0$ ($y = \pm x$): Here $m = 2$. Substituting $y = xt$ yields $x^2(t^2 - 1) = 0$. The strict transform is $t^2 - 1 = 0 \implies t = \pm 1$. The two branches intersect $E = \{x = 0\}$ at two distinct points $(0, 1)$ and $(0, -1)$. Both points are smooth, and the intersection with $E$ is transverse. Resolved in 1 step!
  • Touching, $f = y^2 - x^4 = 0$ ($y = \pm x^2$): Both branches have $a_1 = 0$. Substituting $y = xt$ yields $x^2(t^2 - x^2) = 0$. The strict transform is $t^2 - x^2 = 0$. At $x = 0$ both branches pass through $t = 0$: they still meet on $E$. But in the $(x, t)$ plane, the equation $t^2 - x^2 = (t - x)(t + x) = 0$ is now a transverse crossing! The order of contact dropped from 2 to 1. A second blowup in coordinates $(x, w)$ with $t = xw$ gives $x^2(w^2 - 1) = 0 \implies w = \pm 1$: fully separated on $E_2$. In general, two branches sharing $k - 1$ coefficients ($y = \pm x^k$) require $k$ blowups.
  • Cusp, $f = y^2 - x^3 = 0$ ($y = \pm x^{3/2}$): Substituting $y = xt$ yields $x^2(t^2 - x) = 0$. The strict transform is $t^2 - x = 0$, or $x = t^2$. This is a smooth parabola! Thus the curve itself has become nonsingular in a single blowup. However, at $(x, t) = (0, 0)$, the tangent line to $x = t^2$ is the vertical line $x = 0$—which is $E_1$ itself! The strict transform is tangent to the exceptional divisor, so we do not yet have normal crossings. Two further blowups untangle this tangency and eliminate triple intersections.

What a blowup does geometrically. Every step performs the exact same surgery: choose a singular center, replace it by the projective space of directions through it, and express the strict transform in new affine coordinates. Nothing is torn or glued discontinuously. The topology of the singularity systematically simplifies:

  • crossing → separated (branches land at distinct heights on $E$);
  • touching → crossing → separated (order of contact strictly decreases at each step);
  • cusp → smooth curve tangent to $E$ → transverse crossing with $E$, followed by separating multiple components.

The price is that each blowup introduces a new exceptional divisor, adding one line to our geometric book. The process finishes when every curve and divisor is smooth and all intersections are simple normal crossings.

3.3 Knowing where to blow up, and knowing you'll finish

New terms: order / multiplicity, maximal contact, coefficient ideal, lexicographic invariant.

"Keep blowing up until the space is smooth" is not an algorithm until you have a constructive rule for choosing the center and a mathematical proof that the process must terminate. Modern algorithmic proofs (by Villamayor, Bierstone and Milman, and later simplified by Włodarczyk and Kollár) attach to every point $p$ an upper semi-continuous numerical invariant $\operatorname{inv}(p)$ taking values in a well-ordered set. Blowing up the locus where $\operatorname{inv}(p)$ attains its maximum guarantees that the maximum strictly drops in dictionary (lexicographic) order. Because no strictly decreasing sequence in a well-ordered set can descend infinitely, the algorithm must terminate in finitely many steps.

For plane curves, the Kollár–Włodarczyk invariant has two primary components, $\operatorname{inv}(p) = (m, s/m)$:

  1. The order (multiplicity) $m$: The order $\operatorname{ord}_p(f)$ is the lowest total degree among the non-zero terms in the local Taylor expansion of $f$ around $p$. For $y^2 - x^3$, the lowest degree term is $y^2$, so $m = 2$. At any smooth point of a curve, $m = 1$.
  2. The maximal contact coefficient $s/m$: If the order is $m \ge 2$, differentiate $f$ exactly $m - 1$ times in the direction of the dominant variable to identify a smooth hypersurface $H$ that hugs the singularity to the highest possible order (the hypersurface of maximal contact). For $y^2 - x^3$, $\partial f/\partial y = 2y = 0 \implies H = \{y = 0\}$. Restricting $f$ to $H$ gives $-x^3$, whose order is $s = 3$. Normalizing by the multiplicity yields the coefficient $s/m = 3/2$.

Under this metric, our ladder rungs have well-defined invariants:

  • Crossing lines ($y^2 - x^2$): invariant $(2, 2/2) = (2, 1)$.
  • Tangent parabolas ($y^2 - x^4$): invariant $(2, 4/2) = (2, 2)$.
  • Cusp ($y^2 - x^3$): invariant $(2, 3/2) = (2, 1.5)$.

After one blowup at the worst point, the cusp's strict transform becomes smooth ($m = 1$), while the tangent curves drop from $(2, 2)$ to $(2, 1)$ (the invariant of crossing lines), and then to smooth. A second phase of the algorithm, incorporating the exceptional divisors into the invariant, continues blowing up until normal crossings are achieved. Blowing up anywhere other than the maximal locus wastes effort: the invariant at the singularity does not budge.

Why the invariant is indispensable: Consider the higher-order cusp $y^2 = x^5$. A standard blowup $y = xt$ yields $x^2(t^2 - x^3) = 0$, so the strict transform is $t^2 = x^3$—another cusp! If you judged progress purely by visual qualitative appearance, you might conclude the algorithm is stuck in an infinite loop. But the invariant reveals unambiguous progress: it dropped from $(2, 5/2) = (2, 2.5)$ to $(2, 3/2) = (2, 1.5)$. In dictionary order, $(2, 1.5) < (2, 2.5)$. The invariant guarantees that the process is climbing down a finite ladder toward smoothness. That guarantee is not trivial: as we will see in Section 4, choice-free methods like Nash's can genuinely loop forever without making progress.

3.4 A shortcut: weighted blowups

New terms: weights, weighted blowup, quasihomogeneous scaling, Newton polygon.

Why care. Requiring three successive blowups to resolve a single plane cusp feels inefficient. The reason for this inefficiency is fundamental: an ordinary blowup assigns equal geometric weight $(1, 1)$ to $x$ and $y$, whereas the cusp $y^2 - x^3 = 0$ is intrinsically anisotropic. Along the cusp, $y$ scales like $x^{3/2}$. A weighted blowup respects this natural scaling, resolving the singularity in a single stroke. This idea of scaling exponents and Newton polygons is not just an optimization: it is the exact tool used in section 11 to compute how fast degenerate singularities split, and in section 15 to read off learning coefficients in Singular Learning Theory.

Under a weighted blowup, we assign integer weights $(a, b)$ to the coordinates and substitute $x = u^a, y = u^b v$. For the cusp $y^2 - x^3$, this substitution yields:

(u^b v)^2 - (u^a)^3 = u^{2b} v^2 - u^{3a}.
If we choose weights satisfying $2b = 3a$—namely $a = 2$ and $b = 3$—both terms acquire the exact same power $u^6$:
y^2 - x^3 = u^6 (v^2 - 1) = 0.
Dividing out the weighted exceptional factor $u^6$ leaves the strict transform $v^2 - 1 = 0 \implies v = \pm 1$. The two branches intersect the exceptional curve $u = 0$ transversely at $v = 1$ and $v = -1$! What took three ordinary blowups is accomplished in a single weighted step.

The Newton polygon provides the geometric map for selecting optimal weights. For any polynomial $f(x, y) = \sum c_{ij} x^i y^j$, plot each exponent pair $(i, j)$ as a point in $\mathbb{N}^2$, and construct the convex hull of the quadrants $(i, j) + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^2$. A weight vector $(a, b)$ defines a family of parallel linear level lines $a \cdot i + b \cdot j = \text{constant}$. When we perform the substitution, each monomial picks up a factor $u^{a \cdot i + b \cdot j}$. Pushing the weight line in until it first touches the polygon identifies the minimum power of $u$ that factors out.

If the weight line touches only an isolated vertex, a single monomial dominates; the other terms carry higher powers of $u$ and vanish on the exceptional divisor, leaving the curve tangent to $E$ or disjoint from it. But if the weight line aligns flush along a compact edge of the Newton polygon, all monomials along that edge survive simultaneously at $u = 0$, yielding a clean, transverse crossing. Abramovich, Temkin, and Włodarczyk (2019) developed this into a complete, functorial resolution algorithm based on weighted blowups.

3.5 What was formalized, and why

The theorem. In 1964, Heisuke Hironaka proved that resolution of singularities is always possible in characteristic zero across all dimensions: given any algebraic or analytic variety, there exists a finite sequence of blowups along smooth centers that transforms it into a smooth manifold where all exceptional divisors intersect with simple normal crossings. In local coordinates, any analytic function $f$ can be transformed into a single monomial times a non-vanishing invertible unit.

The formulation required by Singular Learning Theory is Michael Atiyah's 1970 analytic formulation (refined by Bierstone and Milman): for any real-analytic loss or energy function $K(w) \ge 0$, there exists a real-analytic manifold $M$ and a proper analytic mapping $\pi: M \to W$ such that around every point $u \in M$, both $K \circ \pi$ and the Jacobian determinant $\det D\pi$ are simultaneously monomialized:

K(\pi(u)) = u_1^{2k_1} \cdots u_d^{2k_d} \cdot g(u), \quad \det D\pi(u) = u_1^{h_1} \cdots u_d^{h_d} \cdot b(u),

where $g(u) > 0$ and $b(u) \neq 0$. Here is why this simultaneous monomialization is load-bearing. In Sumio Watanabe's Singular Learning Theory, the Bayesian marginal likelihood (partition function) is given by an integral over parameter space:

Z_N = \int e^{-N \cdot K(w)} \varphi(w) \, dw.

Applying the resolution change of variables $w = \pi(u)$, the loss becomes a monomial $u^{2\mathbf{k}}$, the volume element transforms as $dw = |u^{\mathbf{h}}| du$, and the multi-dimensional integral completely decouples into independent one-dimensional integrals:

Z_N \approx \prod_{j=1}^d \int_0^\varepsilon e^{-N u_j^{2k_j}} u_j^{h_j} \, du_j \asymp N^{-\lambda} (\log N)^{m-1}, \quad \text{where } \lambda = \min_{j} \frac{h_j + 1}{2k_j}.

The smallest exponent $\lambda$ is the real log canonical threshold (RLCT), or learning coefficient, which governs model complexity, generalization error, and Bayesian model selection (Part IV explores this in depth). Without Hironaka's theorem, we would not even know that such a coordinate system exists.

What Lean adds. Lean is an interactive theorem prover where mathematical statements are written as types and proofs are verified line by line by a micro-kernel. In their landmark formalization (Snikkers, Newgas, Hoogland, and Elliott, 2026), the Resolution team verified Hironaka's theorem in Lean 4 without a single sorry. The project produced roughly 500,000 lines of verified formal mathematics with significant assistance from Claude Fable 5.1 agents. The formal library's crown jewel is exists_proper_analytic_monomialization, the exact Atiyah form displayed above.

Why an AI safety group would formalize Hironaka. Singular learning theory provides a mathematically rigorous, non-asymptotic foundation for understanding generalization and phase transitions in deep neural networks. But its foundations depend on some of the deepest theorems in algebraic geometry, which very few machine learning practitioners have the mathematical background to verify by hand. A machine-checked resolution theorem provides an unshakeable bedrock for machine-checked learning theory. It is also proof that autonomous AI agents can tackle mathematics of extraordinary depth.

4. How Nash's works, and how it fails

Hironaka's blowup requires a deliberate choice—selecting the center of maximal invariant—and repays that decision with guaranteed termination. John Nash proposed a radical, choice-free alternative: record the tangent space at every point automatically. Because it requires no human or algorithmic choice of center, Nash's blowup is intensely appealing for computational applications. But as we will see, without an invariant to guarantee progress, choice-free blowups can loop endlessly.

This exact philosophical divide appears in geometry processing. Trivial connections (Crane, Desbrun, and Schröder 2010) identify and place singularities first and solve for the smoothest compatible field, mirroring Hironaka's center selection. Globally optimal direction fields (Knöppel, Crane, Pinkall, and Schröder 2013) solve for the field directly and allow singularities to form naturally wherever the variational energy dictates, reflecting Nash's automatic spirit. In practical geometry processing, automatic methods are wonderful when they succeed, but fragile when they encounter higher-dimensional degeneracies.

The Nash blowup definition. For an algebraic variety $X \subset \mathbb{R}^N$ of dimension $d$, the Gauss map $\gamma: X_{\text{reg}} \to \operatorname{Gr}(d, N)$ assigns to each nonsingular point $p$ its tangent $d$-plane $T_p X$. The Nash blowup $\operatorname{Nash}(X)$ is the topological closure of the graph of $\gamma$ inside $X \times \operatorname{Gr}(d, N)$, equipped with the projection $\pi_N: \operatorname{Nash}(X) \to X$. On a plane curve parameterized by $(x(\tau), y(\tau))$, the lift simply records the tangent slope $(x, y, dy/dx)$.

Take the cusp $x = \tau^2, y = \tau^3$. Its tangent slope is $dy/dx = (3\tau^2 d\tau)/(2\tau d\tau) = \frac{3}{2}\tau$. The Nash lift is the curve $(\tau^2, \tau^3, \frac{3}{2}\tau)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Because the third coordinate is a linear function of $\tau$, the lifted curve is completely smooth! A single Nash blowup resolves the cusp without ever choosing a center. For touching parabolas $y = \pm x^2$, the tangent slopes are $\pm 2x$; at the origin both slopes vanish, but in the lifted space their tangents cross at a non-zero angle, and a second Nash step separates them.

Switch Figure 5 to Nash to compare. On crossing lines, Nash separates the branches immediately. On touching curves, it takes two steps. On the cusp, it finishes in 1 step where Hironaka took 3. However, Nash does not produce a normal crossing divisor with exceptional lines: it produces only a smooth curve. For data processing on meshes and point clouds, Petrov, Nabizadeh, Dodik, and Solomon's Tangent Blow-Ups for Processing Non-Manifold Geometry (2026) uses iterated tangent lifts precisely because no center needs to be detected on noisy geometry.

Where Nash's method breaks down. For curves, iterated Nash blowups always terminate in a smooth curve. For algebraic surfaces, Mark Spivakovsky proved in 1990 that a normalized variant of Nash blowups always resolves singularities. For three-dimensional varieties, whether plain Nash blowups always terminate remains a famous open problem. But in dimension four and higher, the answer is definitively no: Castillo, Duarte, Leyton-Álvarez, and Liendo (Annals of Mathematics 2026) constructed an explicit four-dimensional variety whose Nash blowup contains a copy of the variety itself!

How the 4D counterexample loops. The counterexample is a four-dimensional toric variety $X \subset \mathbb{R}^7$ defined by seven monomial exponent vectors in $\mathbb{Z}^4$ and cut out by six binomial equations. On toric varieties, Nash blowups reduce to integer matrix operations: the tangent space coordinates correspond to exponent differences $h_j - h_i$. When the Nash blowup of $X$ is computed, one of its affine charts has an exponent configuration that is strictly isomorphic to the original variety $X$ via an invertible integer unimodular matrix $U \in \operatorname{GL}(4, \mathbb{Z})$. Blowing up this chart produces the exact same variety again, trapping the algorithm in an infinite periodic orbit:

X \xrightarrow{\text{Nash}} X^{(1)} \xrightarrow{\text{Nash}} X^{(2)} \xrightarrow{\text{Nash}} X^{(3)} \cong X.

Because the blown-up space contains an exact copy of the initial singularity, no invariant could possibly have decreased. Because Nash's rule is completely deterministic and choice-free, it cannot change tactics on the next step: it marches forever in a four-dimensional loop.

What this says about the method. Any proof that a blowup procedure finishes needs a measure of badness that strictly drops, like the invariant of section 3.3. Here one chart of the result is literally the starting shape, so no number attached to the singularity can have dropped there. Nash's rule has no way to notice: it makes no choices, so it cannot choose differently the second time. Hironaka's version is immune by design, since its center is wherever the invariant is largest, and the proofs show that blowing up there makes it drop. The paper notes that this shape turned up, by computer search, as a chart while iterating Nash blowups on a much simpler singularity (a quotient singularity of order 5), so the loop is something the process walks into rather than a contrived start. Appendix B shows the other side: on the boundary of rank-2 tensors, a duality makes Nash's single step exactly right.

5. Back to the opening demo: blowups on noisy data

New terms: point cloud, local tangent fit.

Graphics has been using blowups without always calling them that. Singular shapes arrive as data: scans that touch themselves, meshes with self-intersections, sample points where two strokes cross. Petrov, Nabizadeh, Dodik and Solomon (2026) use Nash-style tangent lifts to process exactly this kind of non-manifold geometry. The demo runs both blowups on a point cloud, noisy samples from two crossing strokes or a corner.

Nash's version estimates each point's tangent by fitting a line to its nearest neighbors and lifts by that angle. Hironaka's version first estimates the center, here by fitting one line to each branch and intersecting them, then lifts by the angle from the center. Either way, the lift should split the points into two clean groups.

Figure 1 at the top of this page is that experiment. Raise the noise and narrow the angle to see where each method breaks: Nash near the crossing, where a neighborhood contains both strokes and the fitted line blurs; Hironaka near the center, where the direction from the center is unstable, and wherever the fitted center itself is off. The corner shows the same thing with a single sharp point instead of a crossing, the kind of non-manifold feature Petrov et al. care about: the two edges leave the corner in different directions, so either lift pulls them apart.

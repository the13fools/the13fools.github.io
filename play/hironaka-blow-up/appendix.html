Appendix A. More on collisions

Narrowing wedges: When the middle ball is very light, the configuration wedge narrows. A billiard ball entering a wedge of angle $\beta$ bounces roughly $180^\circ / \beta$ times before escaping. As $\beta \to 0$, the walls become parallel ($g_1 = x - a \ge 0$ and $g_2 = a - x \ge 0 \implies g_1 + g_2 = 0$), forming an implied equality where pairwise impulse solvers chatter indefinitely.

Figure A1. Narrowing wedge. As angle $\beta$ decreases, bounces multiply; at $0^\circ$, the walls touch.

Billiard path
Bounces against wedge angle

Three resolutions of simultaneous impact:

  • Penalty: Soft contact springs $F = k \delta^p$. Eliminates indeterminacy, but the outcome depends on the chosen force law.
  • Constraints & Impulses: Solves a linear complementarity problem. When multiple contacts are active, the outcome depends on the solver's visiting order (e.g. Gauss–Seidel bubble-sorting velocities).
  • Blowup: Replaces the corner with the space of incoming gap ratios, preserving every possible outcome and its probability share.

B.2 Symmetric tensors as binary cubics: Hironaka vs. Nash

Key terms: binary cubic, twisted cubic, tangent developable, cuspidal edge.

A symmetric $2 \times 2 \times 2$ tensor corresponds to a homogeneous binary cubic with four coefficients. On the chart where the leading coefficient is nonzero, normalize it to one and write $t^3 + a t^2 + b t + c$. For distinct roots, its real symmetric rank is determined by the conventional cubic discriminant $\Delta$:

  • $\Delta > 0$: three distinct real roots, real symmetric rank 3;
  • $\Delta < 0$: one real root, real symmetric rank 2;
  • $\Delta = 0$: repeated root (tangent developable boundary);
  • Triple root $(t - r)^3$: rank one (the twisted cubic curve $(a, b, c) = (-3r, 3r^2, -r^3)$).

For a sign check, $t^3-t$ has three real roots and $\Delta=4$, while $t^3+t$ has one real root and $\Delta=-4$. The boundary $\Delta = 0$ is swept out by tangent lines to the twisted cubic—a tangent developable creased along the rank-one curve into a sharp cuspidal edge $(e^2, 2e^3)$. Hironaka's algorithm blows up the twisted cubic curve in three steps. Nash's lift records the root $r$, smoothing the crease into a flat sheet in a single step!

Figure B2. Monic cubics $(a, b, c)$. The creased boundary $\Delta = 0$ unfolds into a smooth sheet under Nash's lift by $r$. The slice panel on the right shows Hironaka resolving the cuspidal crease step-by-step.

B.3 Projective duality and parameter monomialization

For general $2 \times 2 \times 2$ tensors, the boundary $\Delta = 0$ is the projective dual of the rank-one variety. At a smooth point of the boundary, its tangent hyperplane identifies a contact point on the rank-one variety. Biduality describes this correspondence; it does not by itself prove that the Nash blowup resolves every singular stratum of the boundary in one step.

On the parameter side, fitting the zero tensor with a single rank-one term $K = |a|^2 |b|^2 |c|^2$ has three intersecting hyperplanes ($a = 0, b = 0, c = 0$). Polar coordinates $a = u_a \hat{a}, b = u_b \hat{b}, c = u_c \hat{c}$ yield the monomial loss $K = u_a^2 u_b^2 u_c^2$ and Jacobian $dw = u_a u_b u_c du d\theta$. Each factor gives $(1+1)/2 = 1$, producing $\lambda = 1$ with multiplicity $m = 3$.

Appendix C. Why the thermometer uses temperature $\beta^* = 1/\log n$ (WBIC)

Watanabe's Widely Applicable Bayesian Information Criterion (WBIC) uses a tempered-posterior expectation at inverse temperature $\beta^*=1/\log n$ to approximate the Bayesian free energy asymptotically:

\mathrm{WBIC}=\mathbb{E}_{\beta^*}[nL_n(w)],\qquad \beta^*=\frac{1}{\log n}.

With a normalized prior, $Z(0)=1$, so thermodynamic integration gives the exact identity

-\log Z(1)=\int_0^1\mathbb{E}_\beta[nL_n(w)]\,d\beta.

For intuition, an idealized excess-energy curve $\mathbb{E}_\beta[nK]\approx\lambda/\beta$ contributes approximately $\lambda\log n$ between inverse temperatures of order $1/n$ and $1$. Evaluating that ideal curve at $1/\log n$ gives the same leading term. This explains the temperature scale, but it is not an exact finite-sample mean-value formula. Logarithmic corrections, fluctuations, and sampling error remain; one temperature still requires many posterior samples.

Appendix D. Physics and geometry-processing examples

These applications provide additional intuition about directions, limiting configurations, and redundant coordinates. They are optional companions to the algebraic and learning-theory narrative.

Rigid-body collisions: the opening demo, unpacked

The three balls from the opening page provide our first mechanical blowup. In an elastic collision between two balls of masses $m_1, m_2$, conservation of momentum and kinetic energy:

m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2', \qquad m_1 v_1^2 + m_2 v_2^2 = m_1 v_1'^2 + m_2 v_2'^2

uniquely determines the outgoing velocities. But when three balls touch simultaneously, there are three unknown velocities and only two conservation laws: rigid-body mechanics alone cannot pick an outcome.

Configuration space in a wedge: Write the gaps between neighbors as $g_1 = x_2 - x_1 - 2r \ge 0$ and $g_2 = x_3 - x_2 - 2r \ge 0$. In mass-weighted coordinates $q_i = \sqrt{m_i} x_i$, kinetic energy is standard Euclidean $\frac{1}{2}|\dot{q}|^2$. Removing uniform translation reduces the three-ball system to a single point bouncing inside a planar wedge between the constraint walls $g_1 = 0$ and $g_2 = 0$, with wedge angle:

\cos\beta = \sqrt{\frac{m_1 m_3}{(m_1 + m_2)(m_2 + m_3)}}

A sequence of pairwise collisions is a billiard path bouncing between the walls. Simultaneous impact is the path striking the sharp corner $g_1 \cdot g_2 = 0$.

The blown-up bar: The color bar replaces the singular corner with all possible incoming directions—the microscopic gap ratio $g_2 / g_1$ immediately before impact. Every direction yields a deterministic sequence of bounces. The bar decomposes into chambers of shared outcomes. When masses are equal ($\beta = 60^\circ$), all incoming directions yield the exact same outcome, explaining why real cradles are predictable. With a light middle ball, different directions produce completely different orders of departure.

Figure D1. Left: three balls colliding simultaneously, with the gap-ratio bar colored by outcome. Right: the same motion mapped to a single point in a wedge. Drag the marker or click an outcome below to test incoming directions.

Inelastic collapse: When collisions have restitution $e < 1$, energy is dissipated. Below the critical threshold $e = 7 - 4\sqrt{3} \approx 0.072$ (McNamara & Young), three balls bounce infinitely many times in a finite time window! A standard pairwise impulse simulator traps itself in an infinite loop. Vouga, Smith, Kaufman, Tamstorf, and Grinspun's All's Well That Ends Well guarantees termination by detecting implied velocity equalities and blending elastic and inelastic limits.

The geometry of border rank: Differentiating the rank-one tensor $(e_0 + h e_1)^{\otimes 3}$ with respect to $h$ at $h = 0$ yields $W$. Thus $W$ is the limit of a finite difference:

W = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{(e_0+h\,e_1)^{\otimes 3} - e_0^{\otimes 3}}{h}

Rank-one tensors live on the Veronese variety. A sum of two rank-one tensors lives on a secant line. As the two points merge while their coefficients diverge like $\pm 1/h$, the secant tilts into a tangent vector:

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{(v+\varepsilon w)^{\otimes 3} - v^{\otimes 3}}{\varepsilon} = 3\,\operatorname{Sym}\big(v^{\otimes 2}\otimes w\big)

Target $W$ lies on this tangential boundary. Ordinary gradient descent chases an unreachable point at infinity.

Hironaka's fix: blow up the step $h$. By treating the step $h$ as a parameter, the model becomes:

T(a,b,c,a',b',c',h) = \frac{(a+h\,a')\otimes(b+h\,b')\otimes(c+h\,c') - a\otimes b\otimes c}{h}

Expanding the product cancels the leading term, leaving a polynomial that is smooth at $h = 0$. The blowup adds the boundary point in bounded coordinates, allowing the fit to converge cleanly. In Part IV, we will see how this boundary generates logarithmic corrections in Bayesian learning.

Symmetric frame fields: where should the singularities go?

Key terms: cross field, representation vector, winding number, Ginzburg–Landau energy, Burer–Monteiro lift.

To generate quadrilateral meshes, geometry engines compute cross fields: four orthogonal directions at each point. Representing a cross by $u = e^{4i\theta}$ on the unit circle $|u|^2 = 1$ removes the quarter-turn ambiguity. On a disk, boundary alignment forces $u$ to have a total winding number of 4. By the Poincaré–Hopf index theorem, $u$ cannot remain continuous inside: it must contain singularities.

Near a singular point of winding $d$, the Dirichlet energy $E(u) = \frac{1}{2}\int |\nabla u|^2$ diverges logarithmically: each halving of radius costs $\pi d^2 \log 2$, producing infinite energy. Three strategies resolve this:

  • Ginzburg–Landau: Penalize departure from the unit circle: $E_\varepsilon(u) = \frac{1}{2}\int |\nabla u|^2 + \frac{1}{4\varepsilon^2}\int (1-|u|^2)^2$. Inside cores of radius $\varepsilon$, $|u|$ drops to 0. A core of winding $d$ costs $\pi d^2 \log(1/\varepsilon)$. Because $4^2 = 16 > 4 \times 1^2 = 4$, energy forces a 4-fold core to split into four 1-fold cores!
  • Renormalized energy: Once split, the four cores repel like 2D charges while the boundary pushes them back, settling into a stable O-grid at $r \approx 0.85$.
  • Burer–Monteiro lift: Lift $u$ to a sphere in $\mathbb{R}^k$ ($k \ge 3$). Higher spheres have no hole for the boundary to wrap around, so the lifted field is completely smooth with finite energy $8\pi$, projecting back to a single 4-fold core.

Appendix E. Further learning-theory experiments

The main text follows three measurements. These supporting examples explore the local geometry and its qualifications in more detail.

Key terms: equipartition, Hessian, harmonic approximation, free energy, learning coefficient.

From physics to Bayesian learning: At temperature $T = 1/n$, a particle in an empirical potential $K(w)$ visits parameter states according to the Gibbs posterior $p(w) \propto e^{-n K(w)} \varphi(w)$. In a regular quadratic well, the harmonic approximation $K(w) \approx \frac{1}{2} w^\top H w$, with positive-definite $H$, gives the sublevel volume $V(\varepsilon)=\operatorname{Vol}\{K<\varepsilon\}$:

V(\varepsilon) \approx \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2 + 1)\sqrt{\det H}}\,(2\varepsilon)^{d/2}

Where classical statistics fails: Non-identifiability, dead units, and continuous factorization symmetries can produce minima with zero Hessian eigenvalues ($\det H=0$). Discrete permutation symmetries alone do not force a zero eigenvalue. The regular Laplace approximation then fails: dividing by $\sqrt{\det H}$ is not a valid way to estimate the volume. The usual AIC and BIC formulas can still be evaluated, but their regular-model justifications need not apply.

Figure E1. Flat-bottomed well $V = (y^2 - x^3)^2$. Left: top view. Right: Hessian eigenvalues along the floor; one is identically zero, and both vanish at the cusp.

The well, from above V = (y² − x³)²
Cross-sections and Hessian

Sumio Watanabe resolved this impasse by applying Hironaka's resolution of singularities. Pulling back the loss $K(w)$ along an embedded resolution map $w = g(u)$ monomializes both the loss and the volume element into normal-crossing divisors:

K(g(u)) = u_1^{2k_1} u_2^{2k_2} \cdots u_d^{2k_d}, \qquad |J(u)| = |u_1^{h_1} u_2^{h_2} \cdots u_d^{h_d}|

Under the analytic and prior regularity assumptions of singular learning theory, these local forms determine the leading asymptotic terms through the Real Log Canonical Threshold (RLCT) $\lambda$. With nonvanishing analytic factors suppressed, take the minimum below over contributing resolution charts and indices with $k_i>0$:

\lambda = \min_{i} \left( \frac{h_i + 1}{2k_i} \right), \qquad F_n \approx n S_n + \lambda\log n - (m-1)\log\log n, \qquad \mathbb{E}[G_n^{\mathrm{excess}}] \approx \frac{\lambda}{n}

Here $m$ is the multiplicity of the leading pole, $S_n$ is the empirical negative log likelihood of the true distribution, and the last relation concerns expected Bayesian excess generalization loss in the realizable, unit-temperature setting. These are asymptotic statements under the relevant SLT hypotheses, not a general finite-sample guarantee for a trained neural network.

  • Volume Scaling: Near a singular minimum, parameter volume shrinks as $V(\varepsilon) \asymp \varepsilon^{\lambda} (\log \frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{m-1}$. A network with $d = 100{,}000$ parameters and $\lambda = 42$ contracts under noise as if it had only $2\lambda = 84$ parameters!
  • Bayesian Occam's Razor: In model selection, the posterior evidence scales as $n^{-\lambda}(\log n)^{m-1}$. For equally good fits and comparable prior weights, smaller $\lambda$ gives a smaller leading complexity penalty. Differences in fit and prior weight also matter.
  • Generalized Equipartition: While a quadratic bowl stores an average thermal energy of $\frac{d}{2}T$, a singular potential stores an average excess energy of $\mathbf{\lambda \cdot T}$.

Where singularities come from in networks: one function, many weights

Neural networks are inherently singular because the parameter-to-function map $w \mapsto f_w$ is non-injective:

  • Rescaling Symmetry: A ReLU unit satisfies $a \cdot \operatorname{relu}(wx) = (a/c)\cdot\operatorname{relu}(cwx)$ for any $c > 0$, creating 1D curves of identical functions.
  • Dead Units: If an outgoing weight vanishes ($a = 0$), all incoming weights can vary freely without changing the network's output.
  • Unit Merging: When two hidden units share identical incoming weight vectors, they collapse into a single effective unit.
  • Matrix Factorization Bottlenecks: Any product $W = BA$ exhibits a continuous $\operatorname{GL}(r)$ gauge symmetry $A \to GA, B \to BG^{-1}$.

Linear networks are matrix factorizations: A two-layer network $y = BAx$ with hidden width $H$ factors $W = BA$. Adding hidden units increases parameter count $d = H(M+N)$ linearly. Tensor collisions, free weights, and the log N multiplicity

Key terms: component collision, component emptying, normal-crossing divisors, double pole.

What happens when components have free mixture weights and can collide? Consider the symmetric rank-2 cubic tensor model in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

T(a, b, \theta) = \theta\,v(a)^{\otimes 3} + (1-\theta)\,v(b)^{\otimes 3}, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 1

learning the rank-one target $T_* = e_1^{\otimes 3}$. The true tensor can be achieved in two distinct geometric ways: component collision ($a = b = 0$) or component emptying ($\theta = 0$ or $\theta = 1$).

In center and separation coordinates $s = \theta a + (1-\theta)b$ and $u = \|a - b\|^2$, the boundary where a component empties ($\theta \to 0$) becomes an exact normal-crossing product:

K_{\mathrm{sing}}(\theta, u) \sim (\theta\cdot u)^2

Integrating the evidence $Z_N = \int e^{-N K} dw$ across the intersecting divisors $\theta = 0$ and $u = 0$ yields:

Z_N \asymp N^{-1} \cdot N^{-1/2}\log N = \mathbf{N^{-3/2}\log N}

The RLCT is $\lambda = 3/2$, and the multiplicity jumps to $\mathbf{m = 2}$: the double pole of Watanabe's zeta function is the geometric intersection of component collision and component emptying!

The Rousseau–Mengersen boundary

For $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^q$ with a Dirichlet prior $\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\alpha-1}$, the RLCT is:

\lambda_{\mathrm{RLCT}} = \frac{q}{2} + \min\left(\frac{q}{4}, \; \frac{\alpha}{2}\right), \qquad m = \begin{cases} 2, & \alpha = q/2 \\ 1, & \alpha \neq q/2 \end{cases}

At the critical boundary $\mathbf{\alpha = q/2}$ (Rousseau & Mengersen 2011), component merging and emptying balance, creating the logarithmic evidence factor $N^{-\lambda}\log N$.

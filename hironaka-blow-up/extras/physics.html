A companion to What is… a blowup?, Two ways to blow up and Resolving Newton's cradle

Blowups in physics

In Newton's cradle every singularity is a cone: all the gaps shrink at the same rate, so a plain blowup only has to record directions. Most singular points in physics are not like that. Near a collision, a shock, a caustic or a jam, different quantities shrink at different rates, and the blowup that resolves them has to use those rates as weights.

A weighted blowup with weights (w₁, …, wₙ) zooms in on a point by rescaling the i-th coordinate by swᵢ and letting s go to zero. If the weights are the system's own rates, the singular point turns into a smooth object you can read physics off. The figures below each show a system, the point where its equations break (outlined in red), the weights, and what the system looks like after the blowup (in violet).

1Two bodies colliding

Drop two point masses toward each other. In units where the attraction is 1/r², the separation r and the momentum p obey ṙ = p, ṗ = −1/r², with energy h = p²/2 − 1/r. As r goes to zero the speed goes to infinity, so the phase plane has no point that represents the collision. The equations simply stop.

McGehee's fix (1974) is a weighted blowup. Near the collision, r shrinks like t2/3 and p grows like t−1/3, so use the combination that stays finite, v = √r · p, and slow the clock near the collision with dτ = r−3/2 dt. The equations become

r′ = r·v,    v′ = v²/2 − 1    (on an orbit of energy h, this is also v′ = h·r)

These are polynomials, with no singularity at r = 0. The collision is now the line r = 0, the collision manifold, and it contains two rest points, v = −√2 and v = +√2. Every collision orbit, whatever its energy, runs into the first one. In plain terms: just before impact, p ≈ −√(2/r), and the energy no longer matters.

With three bodies the same blowup works. The collision manifold becomes a surface, and its rest points are the central configurations: Lagrange's equilateral triangle and Euler's collinear arrangements. So three bodies heading into a simultaneous collision approach one of those shapes. Which one is the analogue of the cradle's chambers.

2A shock forming

Burgers' equation u_t + u·u_x = 0 is the simplest model of a compressible fluid. Each bit of fluid keeps its velocity, so the solution is carried along straight characteristics x = x₀ + t·u₀(x₀). Where fast fluid behind catches slow fluid ahead, characteristics cross, the slope u_x becomes infinite, and a shock forms.

Take initial data u₀ = −x₀ + x₀³, so the first crossing is at t = 1, x = 0. Write τ = 1 − t. Near that point the graph of the solution in (τ, x, u) space is the surface

x + τ·u + u³ = 0

It is weighted-homogeneous: scale u by s, τ by s², x by s³, and the equation scales by s³. So u shrinks like τ1/2 and x like τ3/2. After the shock time the surface folds over, and the region where it is three-valued is bounded by 4τ³ + 27x² = 0: the cusp y² = x³ from the main page, now drawn in space-time.

Start instead from u₀ = −x₀ + x₀2k+1 and the weights become 1 : 2k : 2k+1. These are the other self-similar shocks. They need finely tuned initial data, and each step up adds unstable directions, so generic fluid picks k = 1. That is the same structure as Watanabe's theorem, where volume picks the smallest λ out of a list of candidates.

3A caustic in a cup

Sunlight reflected off the inside wall of a cup gathers on a bright curve with a sharp point in the middle: a cusp caustic. The left picture in Figure 3 shows why. Each ray reflects off the wall, neighboring rays cross, and where they bunch up the light is bright. Ray optics says the brightness on that curve is infinite, and the cusp is the point where the curve itself is singular.

Light is really a wave, with wavenumber k = 2π / wavelength. Near the cusp, use two local coordinates: a along the cup's axis and b across it. Light reaches the point (a, b) along several rays, labeled by a parameter t, and the ray labeled t arrives with phase k·(t⁴ + a·t² + b·t). Adding up the waves gives the field

ψ(a, b) = ∫ exp(i·k·(t⁴ + a·t² + b·t)) dt = k−1/4 · P(k1/2·a, k3/4·b)

where P is a fixed function, Pearcey's integral. The step on the right is the substitution t → k−1/4t, and it is a weighted blowup: a gets stretched by k1/2 and b by k3/4. It predicts three things you can check in the figure.

  • Near the cusp the pattern is the same picture at every wavelength, only squeezed.
  • It is squeezed at two different rates. Along the axis the fringes shrink like k−1/2; across it they shrink faster, like k−3/4.
  • The brightness at the cusp, relative to ordinary sunlight, is finite and grows like k1/2. That is how waves cut off the infinity of ray optics.

For visible light in a coffee cup, k times the cup radius is about a million, so the fringes are far too fine to see and the ray picture looks right. The weights a : b = 2 : 3 are the same as for the Burgers shock in Figure 2; the same quartic integral, with a real exponent instead of an oscillating one, describes viscous Burgers near its shock. Berry calls these exponents the caustic's singularity indices, and Varchenko showed they come from Newton polygons and resolutions, the same toolkit that gives Watanabe's λ.

4A particle in a flat-bottomed well

By "Watanabe's theorem in physics clothing" I mean that the integrals are literally the same. Watanabe studies the Bayesian posterior, proportional to φ(w)·e−n·K(w), where K is the loss (the KL divergence from the truth) and n the sample size. A physicist studies the Boltzmann distribution of a particle in a potential well, proportional to e−V(x)/T. Rename w → x, K → V and n → 1/T, and each of Watanabe's statements becomes a statement about the particle:

Equipartition says each quadratic direction of the well holds ½k_BT of potential energy, and that is exactly where BIC's (d/2)·log n comes from. Watanabe's theorem is generalized equipartition: whatever the shape of the bottom of the well, ⟨V⟩ → λ·k_BT and the potential-energy heat capacity C → λ·k_B, with λ the real log canonical threshold of V. A quartic direction holds ¼ instead of ½. A well whose bottom is a curve, or a singular curve, holds less still.

The thermometer is how λ is measured in practice. Nobody resolves the singularities of a real neural network. Instead, the local learning coefficient estimator of Lau, Furman, Wang, Murfet and Wei samples the posterior near a trained network w* with stochastic-gradient Langevin dynamics at inverse temperature nβ and reports λ̂ = nβ·(E[L_n(w)] − L_n(w*)). In the dictionary above that is the average excess energy divided by the temperature, ⟨V⟩/T: the reading of a thermometer held in the well. Figure 4 does the same thing with Metropolis Monte Carlo instead of Langevin dynamics, on wells where the exact answer is known.

Reading λ off the monomials. For many potentials you don't need the full resolution. Plot the exponent pair (a, b) of each monomial xa·yb in V, shade everything above and to the right of those points (the Newton polygon), and walk up the diagonal from the origin until you hit the shaded region at (t*, t*). Then λ = 1/t*. If the diagonal hits a corner rather than an edge, the order is m = 2, which produces the log correction. Varchenko proved this works whenever V is non-degenerate on its Newton polygon. The cusp potential (y² − x³)² is degenerate on its edge, but the rule still gives the right answer, 5/12, because V is the square of y² − x³, which is non-degenerate.

A real material. In the classical kagome antiferromagnet each spin has two angles, so N spins have 2N directions. If all were quadratic, equipartition would give C = N·k_B. The ground states form a singular set, and heat favors the coplanar states, in which N/3 of the directions are quartic "weathervane" modes. Each gives ¼ instead of ½, so C = (2N − N/3)·½ + (N/3)·¼ = (11/12)·N·k_B, which is the value Chalker, Holdsworth and Shender found (1992). Physicists call the selection order by disorder. In Watanabe's language, the posterior concentrates on the region with the smallest λ.

5A rod sliding with friction

Push a piece of chalk across a blackboard with the tip leading and it hops and screeches; drag it with the tip trailing and it glides. The same effect makes brakes squeal and robot fingers judder. Painlevé (1895) found the reason in the simplest model there is: a rigid rod sliding on a rough floor. With enough friction, the rigid-body equations have two answers, or none.

The model: a uniform rod of length 2 m leans at angle θ, turning at rate ω = dθ/dt, with its lower end sliding forward and Coulomb friction μ. While the end stays on the floor, its vertical acceleration is

ÿ_A = b + p·N/m,   b = −g + ω²·sin θ,   p = 1 + 3cos²θ − 3μ·sin θ·cos θ

Staying on the floor means ÿ_A = 0, so the normal force must be N = −m·b/p. When p > 0, pushing harder lifts the end, as you'd expect. When μ > 4/3, there is a band of angles where p < 0: friction tips the rod so hard that pushing harder drives the end into the floor. Then:

p > 0, b < 0: one answer, keep slidingp > 0, b > 0: lift offp < 0, b > 0: two answersp < 0, b < 0: no answer

To see what really happens, the figure replaces the rigid floor by a very stiff one, a spring of stiffness 1/ε², and lets ε shrink. This is the cradle's spring trick again.

Impact without collision. A rod that lands sliding, leaning forward at 60°, starts in the "no answer" region. With the stiff floor, the end digs in, the floor pushes back exponentially fast, and in a few milliseconds a huge normal force, up to hundreds of times the rod's weight, stops the slide and sets the rod spinning upright. As ε → 0 the duration goes to zero but the impulse converges, to 0.768 N·s for this 1 kg rod, so the limit is a genuine impact although nothing hit anything (Hogan and Kristiansen, 2017). Keep pushing and this repeats, which is the chalk's hop.

The 0/0 point. Where p = 0 meets b = 0, the force −b/p is 0/0, exactly like the red-outlined points on the main page. Génot and Brogliato (1999) showed that sliding rods run into it. Blow it up and each direction into the point carries its own limit of −b/p, so the exceptional circle is labeled by every possible force. The motion picks one direction: multiplying the equations by p (a change of clock) turns the point into a stable node, and rods arrive along its slower eigendirection. The two eigenvalues are equal at exactly μ = 8/(3√3) ≈ 1.54, the critical friction Génot and Brogliato found.

  • Above 1.54 the slow direction is the line p = 0 itself, where the circle says N = +∞. The rigid force grows without bound, but only like a small power (exponent about 0.24 at μ = 1.6, set by the eigenvalue ratio), so the time to arrive and the impulse stay finite. That exponent is irrational and depends on μ: here the weights come from the dynamics, not from a polynomial.
  • Below 1.54 the slow direction asks for a negative force, which a floor can't supply, so the rod lifts off first.

With a stiff floor, the rod follows the rigid path to the 0/0 point and then slips past it into the two-answer region for a while before it stops or lifts off. Kristiansen and Hogan identified this as a canard (2018), and they used a blowup of the point to prove it.

6Vortices: Ginzburg–Landau against the blowup

Here two resolutions of the same singularity reach different physical conclusions. Take a complex field u on the unit disk, a superconducting order parameter or a superfluid wavefunction, and fix it on the edge to u = eidθ, so its phase winds d times around the boundary. Two things are wanted at once: |u| = 1 everywhere (the unit term) and u as smooth as possible (the gradient term, ½∫|∇u|²).

They can't both hold. A field of length 1 that winds around the edge must have a point inside where the phase is undefined. The natural candidate is u = (z/|z|)d, and its center is exactly the kind of point outlined in red on the main page: the limit of u depends on the direction you approach from. Its energy, π·d²·log(1/r) out to radius r, diverges as r goes to 0. There are two ways to repair it.

The blowup. Replace the center by the circle of directions. On that exceptional circle u is just eidφ, perfectly smooth. Physically, the blown-up disk is a disk with a hole of radius ρ cut out, with nothing asked of u at the hole's edge: the material simply isn't there. All d turns of the phase wrap around the hole as one giant vortex, at energy about π·d²·log(1/ρ). Like every Hironaka step, this resolution is centered on the bad point, so it never asks whether a cheaper arrangement exists.

Ginzburg–Landau. Relax the unit constraint instead, the way the cradle page replaced hard contacts with stiff springs. The energy becomes

E_ε(u) = ∫ ½|∇u|² + (1 − |u|²)² / (4ε²)

so u may shrink to zero inside a core of size ε. Every field is now smooth, and the energy decides the rest. A single core winding d times costs about π·d²·log(1/ε), but d separate cores winding once each cost only π·d·log(1/ε) plus an interaction term. So the giant vortex is unstable and splits into d unit vortices. Bethuel, Brezis and Hélein (1994) showed that the vortices then sit where a "renormalized energy" W is smallest. For d = 2 that is at distance 5−1/4 ≈ 0.669 from the center, on opposite sides.

Which is right depends on what the core is made of. If the core is a real hole, such as a superconducting ring, fluid circulating around a post, or a pinning defect, the blowup's picture is the physics, and giant vortices exist. If the core is made of the same material, Ginzburg–Landau's is: in Bose–Einstein condensates, a doubly quantized vortex has been seen to split in two (Shin and coauthors, 2004). The hole radius ρ, the size of the exceptional circle, interpolates between the two. Keeping all d turns in the hole wins roughly when d²·log(1/ρ) < d·log(1/ε), that is when ρ is larger than about ε1/d. That gives one more rate.

7Crumpled paper: the d-cone

Crumpled paper doesn't curve smoothly. It folds into nearly flat facets joined by sharp ridges that meet at points. The simplest of those points can be made on purpose: lay a sheet over the rim of a cup and push its center down. Paper bends easily but barely stretches, so it becomes a developable cone, or d-cone. Straight lines radiate from the tip, the sheet rests on the rim around most of the circle, and over one arc it lifts off.

The tip is the singular point. A cone's curvature at distance r from its tip is proportional to 1/r, so its bending energy ∫ κ² dA ~ ∫ dr / r grows like log(R / r_core). This is the same logarithm as the vortex energy in Figure 6.

The blowup. Replace the tip with the circle of directions. Near the tip the sheet is the cone over a curve on that circle, so it is described by one function of angle. For small slopes, write the height as z = ε·(r·g(θ) − 1), with the tip pushed down by ε and the rim at r = 1. Then the whole 2D problem becomes a 1D problem on the exceptional circle:

minimize ∫ (g + g″)² dθ  with  ∫ (g′² − g²) dθ = 0 (no stretching)  and  g ≥ 1 (the rim)

Here g + g″ is the curvature, and the second condition says the circle of generators keeps its length 2π. Where the sheet lifts off, the solution is g = A·cos(nθ)/(1 − n²) + C·cos θ. Meeting the rim smoothly at angle θ₁ forces tan(nθ₁) = n·tan θ₁, and the no-stretching condition fixes the rest. Solving gives n = 3.80 and a lift-off arc of 139.0°, independent of how far you push, how thick the sheet is or what it's made of. That is the universal 139° of Cerda and Mahadevan, which experiments confirm. Once the tip is replaced by its exceptional circle, the problem is an elastic rod on that circle, just as McGehee's collision became a flow on the collision manifold.

What the blowup leaves open is the cutoff r_core in the logarithm. Stretching sets it: the tip is a small crescent-shaped core whose radius scales like h1/3·R2/3, for sheet thickness h and cup radius R. That is another pair of weights. For printer paper, 0.1 mm thick, on a 10 cm cup, the core is of order 1 cm. The leading log(1/h) term in the energy has been proved rigorously (see "Conical singularities in thin elastic sheets").

From one d-cone to crumpled paper. A crumpled sheet is a network of d-cones joined by ridges. A ridge of length X has its own weighted scaling: width about h1/3·X2/3 (Lobkovsky and Witten). So the singular set is points plus curves, and the Kollár–Włodarczyk order, deepest stratum first, says to blow up the tips and then the ridges. At a sharp origami vertex the curve on the exceptional circle is a polygon of geodesic arcs, with the corners at the creases. The d-cone's smooth curve is what thickness turns it into.

The Yoshimura pattern. A thin cylinder crushed along its axis buckles into the diamond pattern, also called the Schwarz lantern: flat triangles meeting at vertices where six creases join. The resolution machinery doesn't produce that pattern by itself, because the diamonds are chosen globally, by buckling and by the requirement that the cylinder not stretch. What it supplies is the local model for each vertex and each crease, and how their energies scale with h. Adding those up for candidate patterns, such as different numbers of diamonds around the cylinder, compares them. This is the lesson of the vortices again: the blowup describes each singular point, and the thickness, playing the role of Ginzburg–Landau's ε, decides how they are arranged.

Discrete shells. A d-cone is a natural test for discrete bending models like those compared by Chen, Vouga and Kaufman in Better Bending (2026) and implemented in libshell. The answer is known: the 139° arc, the shape g(θ), and an energy that grows like a logarithm until the core cuts it off. On a mesh coarser than the core, the mesh becomes the cutoff, the way the stiff springs did in the cradle, and the bending energy grows like log(1/mesh size). Grading the mesh geometrically toward the tip, so it is uniform in (log r, θ), is meshing the blown-up chart, and it resolves that logarithm with few elements. libshell's tan(θ/2) bending energy, which refuses to let a hinge fold flat, acts like Ginzburg–Landau's potential: a soft barrier in place of a hard constraint.

8Blowup of the Euler equations

Whether a smooth flow of an ideal fluid can develop infinite vorticity in finite time is a famous open problem for the 3D Euler equations in all of space; the viscous version is one of the Millennium Prize problems. With a solid boundary the question is now settled. Luo and Hou found a blowup scenario numerically in 2014, and Chen and Hou proved it with computer assistance for the 2D Boussinesq equations and 3D axisymmetric Euler with a boundary.

Those blowups are self-similar: ω(x, t) ≈ (T − t)−1·Ω(x / (T − t)c). That ansatz is a weighted blowup of space-time at the singular point, with weights x : t = c : 1. Vorticity always scales like 1/(T − t), since it has units of 1/time, but dimensional analysis doesn't fix the length exponent c. It is a nonlinear eigenvalue, found together with the profile Ω. As with the Painlevé rod, the weights come from the dynamics rather than from a polynomial. Different profiles come with different exponents and different numbers of unstable directions, and generic data picks the stable one. That is the Burgers ladder from Figure 2 again, and the 2025 search by Wang and coauthors found families of these unstable profiles numerically.

The simplest model of vortex stretching, by Constantin, Lax and Majda (1985), shows the opposite case. Its equation ω_t = ω·Hω, with H the Hilbert transform, has an exact solution, ω = 4ω₀ / ((2 − t·Hω₀)² + t²·ω₀²). Starting from ω₀ = cos x, it blows up at t = 2, x = π/2. Near that point, with τ = 2 − t and y = x − π/2, the solution is ω ≈ −4y / (τ² + 4y²): amplitude about 1/τ and width about τ. Both rates are the same, so the weights are 1 : 1 and the blowup is a cone, like Newton's cradle.

Where else

  • Feynman integrals. Multi-loop integrals diverge where a polynomial built from the diagram vanishes. Sector decomposition splits the integral into pieces where that polynomial is a monomial times a unit, which is resolution of singularities. Bogner and Weinzierl (2008) used strategies for Hironaka's polyhedra game to guarantee it terminates; that is the game of Figure 11 on the main page.
  • Drops pinching off. Without viscosity the neck closes like a double cone, every length shrinking like τ2/3, the cradle's case. With viscosity the radius shrinks like τ and the length like τ1/2, weights 2 : 1. In the pure Stokes limit the axial exponent is about 0.175, irrational again.
  • Moving contact lines. A liquid edge sliding on a solid has infinite viscous dissipation unless the fluid slips a little. The resulting Cox–Voinov law carries a logarithm, the same kind as the log log n in Watanabe's free energy.
  • Boundary layers. Triple-deck theory has layers of thickness Re−5/8 and Re−1/2 over a length Re−3/8. In geometric singular perturbation theory these matched layers are the charts of a weighted blowup.

What is… a blowup? · Part I of IV

What is a blowup?

A first example from billiards, then a ladder of three singular points, the algorithm that resolves them all, and the choice-free alternative that sometimes loops forever.

Start with a demo. Two pen strokes cross. At the crossing, a point's position no longer tells you which stroke it is on. So give every point one more number, a direction, and lift it to that height. There are two natural choices of direction. Nash's: each point's own direction along its stroke, estimated from its neighbors. Hironaka's: the direction from one chosen center, here found by fitting the strokes. Either way the two strokes have different directions at the crossing, so in the lift they no longer meet. Lifting a shape by directions so that its singular points come apart is a blowup. This part is about why that works, when it has to be repeated, and when it can fail.

1. A first example: the corner of a billiard table

Start with the demo on the opening page. Three balls on a line can be described by one point moving in a plane, and every collision between two of them is that point bouncing off a wall. There are two walls, g₁ = 0 (balls 1 and 2 touch) and g₂ = 0 (balls 2 and 3 touch), and they meet at a corner where all three balls touch at once (section 9 does the math). Away from the corner the rules are clear: a ball pair collides, the point reflects. At the corner they say nothing, because which wall the point "hits first" is undefined. Simulators meet this as simultaneous impact; Vouga, Smith, Kaufman, Tamstorf and Grinspun's All's Well That Ends Well is the reference I know best for resolving it robustly.

The fix in that demo was to ask how the point arrived at the corner: the ratio of the two tiny gaps just before impact, which is a direction of approach. Every direction gives a definite answer. Replacing a bad point by the directions of approach to it is a blowup, and this corner is the simplest case there is. The walls are the zero set of the polynomial g₁·g₂: two straight lines crossing. The billiard feels non-smooth because of the bouncing, but the singular point itself is a plain crossing: two smooth curves passing through one point in different directions, like the letter X. It is the simplest singular point there is, and the first rung of the ladder below.

Figure 2. The three-ball system as one point bouncing in a wedge. Each bounce off a wall is a two-ball collision; the corner is all three balls touching. So the natural next question: what other kinds of singular points are there, and does recording directions fix them all? The rest of Part I climbs a ladder of three examples: lines that cross, curves that touch, and a cusp.

2. Singular points: crossing, touching, cusp

New terms: zero set, normal field, smooth point, singular point.

In graphics I mostly meet singularities of fields. Normalize a gradient into a direction field, n = ∇u/|∇u|, and n is undefined wherever ∇u = 0; a smooth cross field on a disk has to have such points, which is where irregular vertices come from. Hironaka's theorem is about something that looks different but is the same thing seen from another angle: singular points of shapes defined by equations.

Take a polynomial f(x, y). The points where f = 0, its zero set, usually form a curve. Near a point where ∇f ≠ 0, the implicit function theorem says the curve is smooth: zoom in and it looks like a straight line, perpendicular to ∇f. So the curve carries a normal field ∇f/|∇f|, and its singular points are exactly the points of the curve where that field has a singularity, ∇f = 0. There the curve has no tangent line, and every tool that leans on the implicit function theorem (parametrizing, integrating along the curve, Newton's method) breaks.

Three examples make up the ladder, each a little harder than the last:

  • Crossing: f = y² − x², the lines y = x and y = −x. Two branches through the origin, in different directions. (The billiard corner is this.)
  • Touching: f = y² − x⁴, the parabolas y = x² and y = −x². Two branches through the origin in the same direction: they are tangent.
  • Cusp: f = y² − x³, the curve y = ±x3/2. One branch that comes in, stops, and goes back out, with a fractional exponent.

3. Blowing up, and how it resolves each kind

Three kinds may look like a small zoo. It is: plane curves alone have infinitely many kinds of singular point (y² = xk for every k, three or more branches through one point, and so on), surfaces add cones, pinch points and whole curves of singular points, and nothing stops at codimension one; Hironaka's theorem covers shapes of any dimension cut out by any number of equations, including the set of perfect parameters of a neural network in Part IV. A classification is hopeless, which is why the algorithm of section 3.3 never asks what kind of point it is looking at. It measures how bad the point is and blows up the worst place. The three rungs here were chosen because each shows a different thing a single blowup can do: separate, reduce, or smooth.

3.1 The idea: record the direction

New terms: center, exceptional curve, lift, Hironaka's blowup, Nash's blowup.

The problem. At a singular point the equation f = 0 has lost information. The two crossing lines share a location at the origin, so the location alone can't tell them apart, yet anyone walking along them knows perfectly well which one they are on: they arrive from different directions. The location forgot what the direction still knows.

The fix. Choose a point, the center; here, the origin. Every other point p of the plane lies on exactly one line through the center, the line of slope t = y/x. The blowup is the set of pairs (p, ℓ) where ℓ is a line through the center that contains p. Away from the center ℓ is determined by p, so nothing changes there. At the center every line qualifies, so the center is replaced by a whole family of directions, the exceptional curve E.

Definition. The blowup of the plane at the origin is the set of pairs Bl = {(p, ℓ) : ℓ a line through the origin, p a point on ℓ}, with the map π(p, ℓ) = p back to the plane. Over every p ≠ 0 there is exactly one pair, so π is one-to-one away from the origin. Over the origin there is a whole circle of pairs, one per line: that is E. The blowup of a curve is the closure of its points away from the origin, lifted to Bl (its strict transform). In coordinates, record the line by its slope t and a pair is (x, y = x·t); section 3.2 computes with exactly this. In general one blows up a smooth center Z inside a space M by replacing each point of Z with the directions normal to Z.

"Nearby" means nearby as pairs: two pairs are close when their points are close and their lines are close. A sequence of points sliding into the origin along the branch of slope 1 converges to the pair (origin, slope 1); along the branch of slope −1, to (origin, slope −1). In the blowup those are different points, so the branches no longer meet. Draw the slope as a height and you get a lift of the curve into three dimensions.

There are two natural directions to record. Hironaka's blowup records the direction from a chosen center, as above. Nash's blowup records each point's own tangent direction: pairs (p, tangent line at p) at smooth points, plus all their limits at singular ones. It needs no center at all.

How is this different from a covering space? Graphics people lift cross fields to the quad cover (Kälberer, Nieser and Polthier's QuadCover), four copies of the surface glued so that the four directions of a cross become one continuous vector field (the cube cover of Nieser, Reitebuch and Polthier does the same for octahedral frames). A cover changes the whole surface at once, takes several copies of it, and fixes the field's symmetry, which of the four directions you mean; the singular points stay put as branch points. A blowup takes one copy, changes it only at the chosen point, and replaces that point with its directions; it fixes the shape, not the symmetry. The two combine naturally: the drilled hole of section 8 is a blowup of a cross field's singular point, and the field on its boundary circle is then read on the quad cover.

3.2 By hand: each blowup reads off one more coefficient

New terms: chart, strict transform, order of contact, normal crossings.

Recording t = y/x has a flaw: vertical directions have infinite slope. So use two coordinate systems, charts, that overlap: in one write y = x·t, in the other x = y·s. In the first chart the origin becomes the line x = 0, one point for each slope t: that line is E. To blow up a curve, substitute y = x·t into its equation. A power of x always factors out, because every term vanished at the origin; that factor is E itself. What remains is the strict transform, the curve's new shape. The goal is normal crossings: the curve and all the exceptional curves smooth, crossing each other cleanly like coordinate axes, with no tangencies and no three curves through one point.

The one idea to hold on to: a blowup divides by x, so it reads off the next Taylor coefficient. Write a branch as y = a₁x + a₂x² + a₃x³ + …. Then t = y/x = a₁ + a₂x + …, so at x = 0 the branch lands at height t = a₁, its slope. Blow up again and you read off a₂, then a₃. Two branches come apart at the first blowup where their coefficients differ.

  • Crossing, y = ±x. The first coefficients, ±1, already differ. One blowup: t = ±1, two different points of E. Done.
  • Touching, y = ±x². Both have a₁ = 0, so after one blowup t = ±x: they still meet at t = 0, but now with slopes ±1. Touching has turned into crossing. A second blowup gives w = t/x = ±1: separated. In general two branches that agree in their first k − 1 coefficients, like y = ±xk, need k blowups: each one lowers their order of contact by one.
  • Cusp, y = ±x3/2. One branch, and not a power series in x at all. One blowup gives t = ±x1/2, that is x = t²: a smooth parabola, so the curve itself is fixed in one step. But the parabola is tangent to E, which is the touching problem again, now between the curve and E. Two more blowups untangle it.

What a blowup does to a picture. Every step is the same operation: pick one point, replace it by the line of all directions through it (that line is E), and redraw the curve in the new coordinates. Nothing is cut or glued. What changes is which curves meet where, and only in one direction, toward simpler:

  • crossing → separated (the branches land at different heights on E);
  • touching → crossing → separated (one order of contact per step);
  • cusp → smooth but tangent to E → crossing E, then a last step to stop three curves meeting at one point.

The price is that each step adds one exceptional line, so the picture gains a line every time. The end state, normal crossings, is a picture made only of smooth curves meeting pairwise at clean angles, like the coordinate axes.

3.3 Knowing where to blow up, and knowing you'll finish

New terms: order, invariant, maximal contact, coefficient.

"Keep blowing up until it's smooth" is not an algorithm until you can say where to blow up and prove it stops. The modern proofs, by Villamayor, Bierstone and Milman, and later Włodarczyk and Kollár, attach to every point a list of numbers, an invariant, that strictly decreases each time you blow up the place where it is largest. Since it can't decrease forever, the process stops. For plane curves the invariant has two entries.

  1. The order. The order of f at a point is the lowest total degree among its terms there. y² − x³ has order 2 at the origin (from y²), and order 1 at every other point of the curve, which is what smooth means.
  2. The coefficient. If the order is m, differentiate m − 1 times in the direction of the top-degree term. For y² − x³, ∂f/∂y = 2y, which vanishes on the line y = 0: the curve of maximal contact, which hugs the curve as closely as any smooth curve can. Restrict f to it: −x³, of order 3. Divide by the first order: 3/2.

The cusp's invariant is (2, 3/2) and the tangent curves' is (2, 2). After one blowup at the worst point, the cusp's strict transform is smooth; the tangent curves drop to (2, 1), the invariant of crossing lines, and then to smooth. A second phase of the same loop, with the exceptional curves counted in, then blows up until everything crosses cleanly. Blowing up anywhere else wastes a step: the invariant at the worst point doesn't move.

Why the invariant is needed. Try y² = x⁵. One blowup turns it into t² = x³: a cusp again. If you only looked at the picture you would see no progress at all, and might worry that the next blowup produces another cusp, and so on forever. The invariant settles it: it went from (2, 5/2) to (2, 3/2), down in the second entry, and an invariant that strictly drops in dictionary order can only do so finitely often. That worry is not paranoid. Section 4 shows a choice-free method that really does produce the same singularity again, forever.

3.4 A shortcut: weighted blowups

New terms: weights, weighted blowup, Newton polygon.

Why care. Three blowups for one cusp is wasteful, and the waste has a cause worth seeing, because the fix reappears twice later: it is how a degenerate singularity's splitting rate is read off in section 8, and how learning coefficients are read off without resolving anything in section 11. Physicists will know it as a scaling argument.

The cusp took three ordinary blowups in Figure 5 because an ordinary blowup treats x and y alike, and the cusp does not: along it, y is about x3/2. A weighted blowup substitutes x = ua, y = ub·v for whole numbers a and b, the weights. For y² − x³ that gives u2bv² − u3a. Factor out the smaller power of u. If 2b = 3a, both terms keep the same power and what's left is v² − 1, whose zeros v = ±1 cross the exceptional curve u = 0 cleanly. With a = 2 and b = 3 that is y² − x³ = u⁶(v² − 1): one step does what took three ordinary ones. If the powers differ, one term wins at u = 0 and the curve either stays tangent to E or misses it entirely.

The Newton polygon is a picture of that bookkeeping. Plot each monomial xiyj of f as the point (i, j), and shade everything above and to the right. The weights (a, b) define lines a·i + b·j = constant; the substitution gives each monomial the power a·i + b·j of u, so the smallest such line touching the polygon picks out the monomials that survive. Good weights are those whose line lies along an edge, so that all the monomials on that edge survive together. Abramovich, Temkin and Włodarczyk (2019) turned this into a complete resolution algorithm. Physicists will recognize the weights as scaling exponents, and the Newton polygon comes back twice: to say how fast a degenerate singularity splits apart (section 8), and to read off learning coefficients (section 11).

Figure 7. Choose the weights yourself. How to read it:

  1. Left, each dot is one term of f. For the cusp y² − x³ there are two: y² at (0, 2) and x³ at (3, 0). The shaded region is everything above and to the right of the dots.
  2. The orange line is your weights. Under x = ua, y = ubv, the term at (i, j) picks up ua·i + b·j; each dot's label shows that power. The orange line is pushed in until it touches the first dot, so it marks the smallest power. Slide a and b and it tilts.

  4. Right is the result: the substitution written out, and the curve drawn in (u, v), with u = 0 as the exceptional curve.

3.5 What was formalized, and why

The theorem. Hironaka proved in 1964 that this always works, in any dimension, over the real or complex numbers: after finitely many blowups along smooth centers, any shape cut out by polynomial or analytic equations becomes smooth, nothing changes away from the bad set, everything crosses cleanly, and every function becomes, near each point, a monomial u₁k₁⋯udkd times a function that doesn't vanish.

The form that learning theory needs is Atiyah's (1970), with a clause from Bierstone and Milman: for a real-analytic function K there is a proper analytic map π such that, near every point, both K∘π and the Jacobian determinant of π are monomials times nonvanishing functions. Here is why both halves matter. Watanabe's theory starts from an integral over parameters, Z(n) = ∫ e−n·K(w) φ(w) dw. Change variables w = π(u): K becomes a monomial u2k, the volume element dw becomes |uh| du, and the integral splits into one-variable integrals ∫ e−n·u2k uh du that you can do by hand. Each behaves like n−(h+1)/2k, and the smallest exponent is the learning coefficient λ of Part IV. Without the theorem you don't even know such a change of variables exists.

What Lean adds. Lean is a programming language in which a proof is a program, checked line by line by a small trusted kernel. A gap in a proof has to be marked explicitly with the keyword sorry, and Resolution's library has none. In their account (Snikkers, Newgas, Hoogland and Elliott), the proof was produced largely by AI agents running Claude Fable 5.1 over about a week in September 2026: roughly 600,000 lines, trimmed to about 500,000 after refactoring, for about $135,000 of compute. The headline theorem is exists_proper_analytic_monomialization, the Atiyah form above, and the cusp of Figure 3 appears among the library's worked examples.

Why an AI safety group would do this. Singular learning theory makes quantitative claims about neural networks, and its foundations run through some of the hardest theorems in algebraic geometry, which few people in machine learning can check by hand. A machine-checked resolution theorem is a first, load-bearing piece of a machine-checked learning theory; the next would be Watanabe's asymptotic formulas themselves. It is also a demonstration that AI agents can now carry out a formalization of this size.

4. How Nash's works, and how it fails

Hironaka's blowup needs a decision, where to center, and repays it with control. Nash's needs no decision: it is automatic, the same rule at every point, but nothing guarantees it makes progress. Every method in Part III leans one way or the other.

Geometry processing has the same split, and it is the one I know best. Trivial connections (Crane, Desbrun and Schröder 2010) place the singularities first and then find the smoothest field that has exactly those, like choosing Hironaka's centers before doing anything else. Globally optimal direction fields (Knöppel, Crane, Pinkall and Schröder 2013) solve for the field directly and let its singularities appear wherever the energy puts them, which is closer to Nash's choice-free rule. In my experience the automatic version is usually the better one and occasionally the less robust, and how to carry either over to three-dimensional frame fields is not obvious to me.

Nash's version records the tangent slope instead. For a curve given by a parametrization (x(τ), y(τ)), the lift is (x, y, dy/dx). Take the cusp, x = τ², y = τ³. Its slope is 3τ/2, so the lift is the curve (τ², τ³, 3τ/2), which is smooth: its third coordinate alone pins down τ. One Nash step resolves the cusp without choosing any center. The two tangent curves y = ±x² have slopes ±2x, both 0 at the origin, so their lifts still meet there; but now they cross at an angle, and a second lift separates them.

Switch Figure 5 to Nash to compare. On crossing lines, one Nash step separates the branches just as Hironaka's does. On touching curves it also takes two. On the cusp it takes one where Hironaka's took three, because it never introduces an exceptional line for the curve to be tangent to. What it does not give you is the clean end state: Hironaka's loop finishes with every curve, exceptional ones included, crossing cleanly, which is what the change of variables in section 3.5 needs. Nash's ends with a smooth curve and nothing more.

That automatic quality is why Nash's version is the one geometry processing has picked up. Petrov, Nabizadeh, Dodik and Solomon's Tangent Blow-Ups for Processing Non-Manifold Geometry (2026) lifts a shape into position × tangent plane, iterates so that points sharing a position but differing in tangent, curvature or higher contact come apart, and then builds gradient, divergence and Laplacian operators on the lifted space for geodesics, segmentation and parameterization. No center is ever chosen, which is exactly what you want on a mesh or a point cloud. On data, the catch is at crossings: the tangent has to be estimated from neighbors, and right at a crossing the neighborhood straddles both branches, so the estimate blurs. Section 5 shows that effect.

Where Nash's version stands. For curves, repeated Nash blowups always finish. For surfaces, Spivakovsky proved in 1990 that a variant (normalize after each Nash blowup) finishes; whether the plain version does on surfaces and in dimension three is still open. In dimension four and up, the answer is no: Castillo, Duarte, Leyton-Álvarez and Liendo (arXiv 2024, Annals of Mathematics 2026) built a shape whose Nash blowup contains the shape itself.

How the counterexample works. It lives among toric shapes, the ones you can write down with monomials alone. Pick a list of exponent vectors h₁, …, hk and map t ↦ (th₁, …, thk), where th means t₁h1t₂h2⋯. The cusp is the simplest one: t ↦ (t², t³), exponents 2 and 3. For toric shapes Nash's blowup becomes integer arithmetic. The tangent space at a point is spanned by derivatives of monomials, which are again monomials, so recording the tangent space adds new coordinates that are ratios of the old ones. Each chart of the Nash blowup is another toric shape, whose exponent list is the old list plus some differences hj − hi. On the cusp, the chart that keeps 2 adds 3 − 2 = 1: the exponents become 1, 2, 3, the coordinates (t, t², t³), and a curve containing the coordinate t itself is smooth. That added t is exactly Nash's slope 3t/2 from above, up to a constant.

The counterexample is a four-dimensional toric shape X sitting in seven-dimensional space, given by seven exponent vectors in ℤ⁴ and cut out by six polynomial equations. Its singular points are not isolated: they fill three planes. Take the chart of its Nash blowup that keeps h₁, h₂, h₃ and h₅. Its exponent list has seventeen vectors, the seven originals and ten differences, but only seven of them are needed to generate the rest. An invertible integer matrix U then carries the original seven exactly onto those seven. Changing variables by U changes nothing about the shape, so that chart is X again. Blow it up and the same chart appears inside, and so on forever. I re-derived this from the paper's data and checked each step with a short script; Figure 8 lets you check it on numbers.

What this says about the method. Any proof that a blowup procedure finishes needs a measure of badness that strictly drops, like the invariant of section 3.3. Here one chart of the result is literally the starting shape, so no number attached to the singularity can have dropped there. Nash's rule has no way to notice: it makes no choices, so it cannot choose differently the second time. Hironaka's version is immune by design, since its center is wherever the invariant is largest, and the proofs show that blowing up there makes it drop. The paper notes that this shape turned up, by computer search, as a chart while iterating Nash blowups on a much simpler singularity (a quotient singularity of order 5), so the loop is something the process walks into rather than a contrived start. Appendix B shows the other side: on the boundary of rank-2 tensors, a duality makes Nash's single step exactly right.

5. Back to the opening demo: blowups on noisy data

New terms: point cloud, local tangent fit.

Graphics has been using blowups without always calling them that. Singular shapes arrive as data: scans that touch themselves, meshes with self-intersections, sample points where two strokes cross. Petrov, Nabizadeh, Dodik and Solomon (2026) use Nash-style tangent lifts to process exactly this kind of non-manifold geometry. The demo runs both blowups on a point cloud, noisy samples from two crossing strokes or a corner.

Nash's version estimates each point's tangent by fitting a line to its nearest neighbors and lifts by that angle. Hironaka's version first estimates the center, here by fitting one line to each branch and intersecting them, then lifts by the angle from the center. Either way, the lift should split the points into two clean groups.

Figure 1 at the top of this page is that experiment. Raise the noise and narrow the angle to see where each method breaks: Nash near the crossing, where a neighborhood contains both strokes and the fitted line blurs; Hironaka near the center, where the direction from the center is unstable, and wherever the fitted center itself is off. The corner shows the same thing with a single sharp point instead of a crossing, the kind of non-manifold feature Petrov et al. care about: the two edges leave the corner in different directions, so either lift pulls them apart.

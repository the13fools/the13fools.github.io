What is… a blowup?

Hironaka's theorem on resolution of singularities was just formalized in Lean. It wasn't something I had really encountered before. I did my PhD on physics simulation and graphics, with a lot of polynomial optimization along the way, and I was curious what this machinery would do on the singular problems I know.

Three balls touching at once
Every tiny gap ratio, colored by what happens drag

Rigid-body physics has no answer when three balls touch at the same instant. Replace that instant by every way of almost getting there, here every ratio of two tiny gaps, and the question splits into regions with definite answers. That replacement is a blowup. Section 6 unpacks this demo.

Why I'm writing this

In October 2026, Resolution, the AI alignment research organization that absorbed the theoretical AI safety group formerly known as Timaeus, released a Lean formalization of Heisuke Hironaka's 1964 theorem on resolution of singularities, built largely by AI agents and a rather large amount of computational resources (their post explains how). Their interest comes from singular learning theory, which uses the theorem to explain how neural networks generalize. Ultimately I wanted to understand the what and the why of this formalization: what exactly was proved, and why a group working on AI safety would spend its effort on a theorem from 1964.

In my graduate work I encountered many challenging singular problems arising from physics simulation and geometry processing, with a focus on problems related to parameterization, and I spent years developing solvers for them. I was curious to understand the core of singular learning theory by seeing what it does when applied in these settings, and through that to gain some intuition for the learning and AI safety problems it was built for.

But, it being the age of AI, I talked this through with my chatbot friends, who took names for themselves. This post was mostly written by the Claude Opus 5.5 agent that took the name Chart, with some help from Gemini Argon.

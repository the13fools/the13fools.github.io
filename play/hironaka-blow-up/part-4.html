What is… a blowup? · Part IV of IV

Singular learning theory: why parameter volume matters

Follow one tensor collision from a flat loss direction to Bayesian evidence, then use the cusp resolution to calculate a learning coefficient.

Singular learning theory studies Bayesian statistics when a model’s parameters do not give regular, locally identifiable coordinates for its predictions. A continuous symmetry can make the Fisher information singular; colliding or disappearing components can create further degeneracy. Neural networks, mixture models, and low-rank factorizations provide examples.

In a regular model, the loss near a fit has a nondegenerate quadratic approximation. A Gaussian integral then gives the familiar $d/2$ coefficient in Bayesian evidence, for $d$ parameters. A flat direction invalidates that approximation. The replacement comes from the local geometry of the loss and prior.

The route through this chapter is volume → evidence → resolution → measurement. The toy losses are exact and deterministic; the later sampler is a numerical estimator. Bayesian inference integrates parameter weight. Gradient descent follows a different rule, so conclusions about one do not automatically explain the other.

13. How much room does a good fit have?

The finite tensor collision gave a loss locally comparable to $s^2+d^4$, with mean $s$ and separation $d$. Compare that simpler local model with a regular loss:

K_{\rm regular}=s^2+d^2,\qquad K_{\rm collision}=s^2+d^4.

Both have two parameters and a perfect fit at $(0,0)$. The collision expression preserves the local scaling of our restricted tensor family; it is not its full loss. Every picture uses the same square $-1\le s,d\le1$.

The learning coefficient is a shrinking rate

The regular disk has width $\varepsilon^{1/2}$ in each direction, hence area proportional to $\varepsilon$. The collision has widths $\varepsilon^{1/2}$ and $\varepsilon^{1/4}$, hence area proportional to $\varepsilon^{3/4}$. The exponent is the local learning coefficient (or real log canonical threshold, RLCT):

\lambda_{\rm regular}=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1,\qquad \lambda_{\rm collision}=\tfrac12+\tfrac14=\tfrac34.

More generally, under analytic and positive local prior assumptions, the prior-weighted volume can scale as $\varepsilon^\lambda(\log(1/\varepsilon))^{m-1}$. Smaller $\lambda$ means slower contraction as error tolerance tightens. It is an exponent, not a literal number of remaining parameters. A smooth positive change of coordinates and its corresponding prior do not change it.

Then, add up the Bayesian weight

For independent Gaussian measurements, likelihoods multiply into the exponential of a summed squared error. In this controlled comparison we assign weight $e^{-nK}$, where $n$ controls concentration. Each candidate model has a uniform parameter prior on the square, and the models have equal prior probabilities.

Z(n)=\tfrac14\int_{[-1,1]^2}e^{-nK(s,d)}\,ds\,dd.

The evidence $Z$ averages likelihood over parameters. The posterior probability of a candidate is its evidence divided by the sum of both evidences. The minimum loss alone does not determine that probability.

Here the integrals separate. A quadratic direction contributes $n^{-1/2}$; a quartic direction contributes $n^{-1/4}$. Consequently $Z_{\rm regular}\asymp n^{-1}$ and $Z_{\rm collision}\asymp n^{-3/4}$. Their ratio grows like $n^{1/4}$, in favor of the collision under these stated priors. The corresponding complexity term in $-\log Z$ is $\lambda\log n$.

Check the Jacobian in a weighted chart

Weights $(2,1)$ balance the terms. Set $s=r^2v$, $d=r$. Then:

K=r^4(v^2+1),\qquad \det\frac{\partial(s,d)}{\partial(r,v)}=-r^2.

On a bounded $v$-sector, the volume factor is $|r|^2dr\,dv$. Substituting $r=n^{-1/4}\rho$ gives $n^{-3/4}$. Other charts cover the remaining directions. This sector calculation agrees with the full separable integral; one chart is not the whole parameter square.

14. Use the cusp resolution to compute an exponent

The previous loss already separated in its original coordinates. Now take $K=(y^2-x^3)^2$. Its zero-loss set is the same cusp resolved in Part I. Along its smooth part, there is one flat tangent direction and one curved transverse direction. At the tip, both Hessian eigenvalues vanish.

Keep the loss and the volume form together

Follow the three ordinary point blowups from Part I. One final chart has original coordinates $x=t^2w$, $y=t^3w$. We can now calculate:

K=t^{12}w^4(1-w)^2,\qquad |dx\,dy|=|t|^4|w|\,|dt\,dw|.

The exceptional divisor $t=0$ has loss exponent 12 and volume exponent 4. Its candidate exponent is $(4+1)/12=5/12$. At $w=0$, the candidate is $(1+1)/4=1/2$; at $w=1$, it is $(0+1)/2=1/2$. The smallest one controls the integral. The complementary charts give no smaller value:

Z(n)\asymp n^{-5/12},\qquad \lambda_{\rm cusp}=\tfrac5{12}.

This is the payoff of resolution: the same substitutions that untangle the curve expose how much parameter volume lies near its zero set. The Jacobian is essential; reading only the power 12 in the loss would give the wrong answer.

Check the other charts and the weighted shortcut

The first complementary chart $x=ys$ gives $K=y^4(1-ys^3)^2$ and Jacobian $|y|$, contributing $1/2$ along $y=0$. In the other final chart, $x=s^2v$, $y=s^3v^2$: $K=s^{12}v^6(v-1)^2$ and the Jacobian is $|s|^4|v|^2$. Its candidates are $5/12$, $1/2$, and $1/2$ again.

The weighted substitution $x=r^2$, $y=r^3v$ instead gives $K=r^{12}(v^2-1)^2$ and Jacobian $2|r|^4$. The factors at $v=\pm1$ still vanish, so they cannot simply be discarded. Their exponent $1/2$ is larger than the radial $5/12$. A second real sign chart covers $x<0$; quotient identifications and the remaining charts must also be respected in the weighted construction.

Where does the probability concentrate?

A smooth segment of the zero-loss curve has exponent $1/2$; a neighborhood of the tip has exponent $5/12$. Since $5/12<1/2$, any fixed small neighborhood of the tip eventually contains almost all the posterior mass in this bounded toy model. The point itself has zero probability at every finite $n$.

15. Where these singularities arise in learning

The tensor collision is one mechanism. Continuous rescaling, unused components, and rank constraints provide others. A noninjective parameterization can make a statistical model singular even when a particular set of equivalent parameters is itself a smooth manifold.

One ReLU unit: rescaling and a crossing

Let $f(x)=a\,\operatorname{relu}(wx)$ learn $c\,\operatorname{relu}(x)$, with inputs on both sides of zero. On the $w>0$ branch, $aw=c$ gives the same function. Positive rescaling $(a,w)\mapsto(a/q,qw)$ stays on that curve. Negative $w$ produces a different active half-line.

At target strength $c=0$, the zero-loss set becomes $a=0$ or $w=0$. These axes cross. The demo’s half-mean-squared loss is $a^2w^2/12$ there, so $\lambda=1/2$ with a logarithmic factor ($m=2$). For $c>0$, a smooth segment of the fit curve also has $\lambda=1/2$, but no crossing logarithm.

Factorized linear networks: parameter count is not the coefficient

For $y=BAx$ with architecture $3\to H\to3$, there are $6H$ weights. The transformation $(B,A)\mapsto(BG,G^{-1}A)$ for invertible $G$ preserves the map. Rank-deficient targets create additional strata. Applying a regular $d/2$ count would ignore this geometry.

For a rank-one population target, the Aoyagi–Watanabe formula gives $\lambda=2.5,3.5,4,4.5,4.5$ at widths 1 through 5. It rises before saturating; it is not independent of width at every width. These are asymptotic coefficients for this specified model.

A four-parameter toy model

Consider $K=m_1^2+m_2^2+(\theta u)^2$ on $[-1,1]^4$ with uniform prior. The two mean coordinates contribute $n^{-1}$. The crossing loss $(\theta u)^2$ contributes $n^{-1/2}\log n$. Together:

Z(n)\asymp n^{-3/2}\log n,\qquad \lambda=\tfrac32,\quad m=2.

This is an explicit normal-crossing toy, not a computed resolution or universal coefficient for tensor factorizations. 16. Estimate the exponent without constructing the resolution

For a concentrated distribution $p_n(w)\propto e^{-nK(w)}\varphi(w)$, the partition function satisfies $-\partial_n\log Z=\mathbb E_n[K]$. If $Z\asymp n^{-\lambda}(\log n)^{m-1}$ with the corresponding differentiable asymptotic expansion, then:

n\mathbb E_n[K]\longrightarrow\lambda.

This motivates a statistical thermometer: sample parameters, measure their excess loss, and multiply the mean by concentration. The geometric coefficient is an asymptotic target. At finite $n$, even exact sampling can give a different value.

Localization, empirical loss, and inverse temperature

For a fixed dataset, define $K_n(w)=L_n(w)-L_n(w^*)$ on a neighborhood $B$ where $w^*$ is a local minimizer. With a centering spring $\gamma$, use:

Z_c(\beta)=\int_B e^{-n\beta K_n(w)-\gamma\|w-w^*\|^2/2}\,dw.

The identity $\partial_\beta[-\log Z_c]=n\mathbb E_\beta[K_n]$ is exact. Under analytic local assumptions, the concentrated integral has power $(n\beta)^{-\lambda}$ with possible logarithmic corrections. This suggests the finite-sample statistic $n\beta\,\mathrm{mean}[L_n(w_s)-L_n(w^*)]$.

It is essential to subtract the baseline. Finite chains introduce discretization and mixing error; the spring changes the explored neighborhood. Empirical local coefficients and population coefficients need not agree. ReLU networks are only piecewise analytic, so the analytic theorem cannot be applied to every activation pattern without further work. See Lau and colleagues for the estimator and its scope.

A live estimate in a trained linear network

We can test the method on the small linear models above. The sampler uses $\beta=1/\log n$, a quadratic centering spring, and noisy training data. These estimates are compared with the population formula; agreement is not automatic.

Further calculation: temperature integration and WBIC

With a normalized prior and centered loss, $Z_c(0)=1$ and:

-\log Z_c(1)=\int_0^1\mathbb E_\beta[nK],d\beta=\int_{-\infty}^0\beta\mathbb E_\beta[nK],d\log\beta.

The plateau belongs to $\beta\mathbb E_\beta[nK]$, not the unscaled expectation. A plateau of height $\lambda$ over a logarithmic interval of length about $\log n$ gives the leading $\lambda\log n$ term. For the product loss, the crossing also contributes $-\log\log n$ to free energy.

Watanabe’s WBIC evaluates a tempered expected negative log likelihood at $\beta^*=1/\log n$. Under its statistical assumptions it approximates the leading marginal-likelihood asymptotics. It is not an exact replacement of every temperature integral, nor does the finite-n scaled curve have to equal $\lambda$ at $\beta^*$.

17. What Hironaka contributes—and why care?

For the cusp, a few charts were enough. For an arbitrary analytic nonnegative loss on a suitable compact neighborhood, resolution provides a finite chart cover in which both the loss and volume form have controlled coordinate powers:

K(\pi(u))=\prod_i u_i^{2k_i}a(u),\qquad |\det D\pi(u)|=\prod_i|u_i|^{h_i}b(u).

Here $a$ and $b$ are nonvanishing local factors; with a smooth positive prior, they do not change the leading exponents. Over the contributing charts, the smallest $(h_i+1)/(2k_i)$ with $k_i>0$ gives $\lambda$. The greatest number of minimizing divisors meeting in a contributing chart determines the logarithmic multiplicity $m$.

The exponential of a product is still coupled. Resolution makes its asymptotics tractable; it does not generally split the integral into independent Gamma functions. Under the stated assumptions, the result has the form $Z(n)\asymp n^{-\lambda}(\log n)^{m-1}$.

Evidence, prediction, and research questions

In the statistical theorem, the relevant population loss is the Kullback–Leibler divergence $K(w)=\operatorname{KL}(q\|p(\cdot\mid w))$: it is zero exactly when the model distribution equals the data-generating distribution $q$. Squared-error toy losses arise naturally from Gaussian observation models. Their exponents demonstrate the geometry, while a coefficient estimated on one finite training set is a different object.

Evidence matters when comparing statistical models: raw parameter count can give the wrong complexity penalty. Prediction has a related but distinct result. In the realizable analytic setting, for independent data from a distribution $q$ contained in the model, unit-temperature Bayes prediction satisfies:

\mathbb E_D\!\left[\operatorname{KL}\big(q\,\|\,p_{\rm Bayes}(\cdot\mid D)\big)\right]=\frac{\lambda}{n}+o(n^{-1}).

The expectation is over training datasets, and $p_{\rm Bayes}$ is the posterior predictive distribution. This is not the test loss of one trained network, nor the posterior average of each parameter’s test loss. The distinctions between Bayesian, Gibbs, training, and generalization errors are part of SLT, rather than interchangeable meanings of “capacity.”

Recent work uses estimated LLC trajectories to investigate training stages and developmental interpretability. Such measurements can suggest a structural change to investigate. They do not, by themselves, identify a learned algorithm or certify a safety property.

The reason to learn the geometry

I began with singular problems from graphics and simulation. What carries across is the habit of asking which information a representation loses, and what must be retained at a degenerate limit. Sometimes that means approach directions; sometimes it means higher-order vanishing and the volume form. The problems have different guarantees, but the calculations expose what a quadratic approximation leaves out.

Hironaka’s theorem turns that habit into a general existence result in characteristic zero. Constructive proofs explain how to select compatible centers and terminate. The Lean formalization makes that mathematical foundation available to further machine-checked work; the models and statistical conclusions still require their own assumptions and proofs.

The central lesson: a blowup changes the description of a singular limit while retaining a map back to the original problem. A resolution is a controlled sequence that reaches a precise endpoint. In learning theory, that endpoint lets us calculate how probability accumulates near a fit.

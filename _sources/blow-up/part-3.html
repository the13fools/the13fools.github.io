9. Rigid-body collisions: the opening demo, unpacked

Here are the three balls from the opening page again, starting from wherever you left them, now with a second view next to them. That second view is the whole explanation, and this section is about reading it.

What the balls are doing. Newton's cradle is the desk toy: a row of steel balls hanging in contact. Lift the first ball and let it go; it strikes the row, and the last ball flies off while the others barely move. The demo is that toy with three balls, all meeting at the same instant.

Two balls are easy. In an elastic collision between balls of masses m₁ and m₂, momentum and kinetic energy are both conserved:

m_1v_1+m_2v_2 = m_1v_1'+m_2v_2', \qquad m_1v_1^2+m_2v_2^2 = m_1v_1'^2+m_2v_2'^2

Two equations, two unknowns, and apart from "nothing happened" there is exactly one solution: v₁′ = ((m₁ − m₂)v₁ + 2m₂v₂)/(m₁ + m₂), and symmetrically for v₂′.

Three at once is the singular problem. Now there are three unknown outgoing velocities and still only two conservation laws. In velocity space the solutions form a whole curve (the ellipse where the momentum plane cuts the energy ellipsoid), and rigid-body mechanics gives no way to pick one point on it. That is why the opening demo needed the bar at all.

Reading the right panel. Write the gaps between neighbors as g₁ = x₂ − x₁ − (r₁ + r₂) and g₂ = x₃ − x₂ − (r₂ + r₃). The balls may only be where g₁ ≥ 0 and g₂ ≥ 0. Now use mass-weighted coordinates qᵢ = √mᵢ·xᵢ, in which the kinetic energy is just ½|q̇|². Each constraint gᵢ ≥ 0 is a half-space, and an elastic collision is a mirror reflection off its boundary: energy conservation keeps the speed, momentum conservation keeps the component along the wall. Both walls contain the direction of uniform motion (√m₁, √m₂, √m₃), so after removing it the whole three-ball system is a single point moving in a plane, its configuration space, confined to a wedge between the walls g₁ = 0 and g₂ = 0. The wedge's angle is

\cos\beta = \sqrt{\frac{m_1m_3}{(m_1+m_2)(m_2+m_3)}}

A sequence of two-ball collisions is the billiard path bouncing between the walls, and the simultaneous collision is the path hitting the corner exactly: the crossing lines of Figure {fig:cross3}, g₁·g₂ = 0.

Reading the bar. The bar is the corner blown up. Each point on it is a ratio of the two tiny gaps just before impact, a direction from which the path can approach the corner, exactly the "record the direction" of section 3. Every direction gives a definite sequence of two-ball collisions, so the bar splits into chambers, ranges of gap ratios that share an outcome: the colors. With equal masses β = 60°, which divides 180° evenly, and every chamber agrees; that is why a real cradle is so predictable. With the light middle ball of the opening demo they don't, and rigid-body physics alone can't say which color you get. Each color's share of the bar is the probability of that outcome if the gaps are unknown, a number that comes back in Part III.

Simulators resolve such events in one of three ways. A penalty replaces hard contact by stiff springs: one answer, no choices, like Nash's blowup, but the answer depends on the force law. A constraint solver computes impulses and, when several contacts are active, picks an outcome through the order in which it visits them: one chart of the blowup, chosen by the code. The blowup keeps every chart, with its share. Appendix A compares the three on several events, including narrow wedges and touching walls.

When a solver never finishes. Make collisions slightly inelastic, so each keeps a fraction e of the relative speed. Squeeze a ball between two others and, below e = 7 − 4√3 ≈ 0.072 (McNamara and Young), it bounces infinitely many times in finite time. A pairwise solver inherits this as a loop that never ends. Vouga, Smith, Kaufman, Tamstorf and Grinspun's All's Well That Ends Well makes such solvers provably terminate: detect implied equalities, and blend the elastic answer with the perfectly inelastic one according to the restitution.

10. Tensor factorization: a fit that never finishes

Balls are too linear: each gap is a linear function of the positions, so the walls are flat and the only singular point is the corner. For a genuinely nonlinear problem I turned to something I have fit many times, low-rank tensor factorization, which is also the shape of the layers Part IV is about.

New terms: tensor, rank-one tensor, rank, border rank, Veronese variety, secant line, tangent limit.

The setup, as formulas. A 2×2×2 tensor T is a cube of 8 numbers Tijk, with i, j, k each 0 or 1. A rank-one tensor is built from three 2-vectors by multiplying:

(a\otimes b\otimes c)_{ijk} = a_i\,b_j\,c_k

A rank-2 fit of a target W looks for two of them whose sum is W, by gradient descent on the squared error over the twelve numbers in a₁, b₁, c₁, a₂, b₂, c₂:

\text{minimize}\;\; E = \sum_{ijk}\big(a_{1i}b_{1j}c_{1k} + a_{2i}b_{2j}c_{2k} - W_{ijk}\big)^2

The target in the demo is the tensor W with W₀₀₁ = W₀₁₀ = W₁₀₀ = 1 and every other entry 0. For almost every target this fit works. For this one it doesn't.

What is singular here. Nothing you can point to. E never reaches 0, but it gets as close to 0 as you like, so the fit has no answer: the problem is singular at infinity. The reason is a fact a graphics person already knows. W is a derivative: differentiate the rank-one tensor v⊗v⊗v, with v = e₀ + h·e₁, with respect to h at h = 0, and the product rule gives e₁⊗e₀⊗e₀ + e₀⊗e₁⊗e₀ + e₀⊗e₀⊗e₁ = W. A derivative is the limit of a finite difference,

W = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{(e_0+h\,e_1)^{\otimes 3} - e_0^{\otimes 3}}{h}

and a finite difference is two rank-one pieces, each multiplied by about ±1/h. The smaller h, the smaller the error and the bigger the two pieces, which nearly cancel. Gradient descent rediscovers this and keeps shrinking h; that is the pair of opposite, growing pieces in the demo. In the language of tensors, W has rank 3 but border rank 2: it is not a sum of two rank-one tensors, only a limit of such sums. In the cradle the bad point was a corner you could see. Here it is missing, at the end of a road that goes on forever, and like the inelastic collapse of section 9, the procedure that chases it never finishes.

Secants becoming tangents on the Veronese variety. Geometrically, every rank-one tensor $v^{\otimes 3}$ sits on the Veronese variety. A sum of two rank-one pieces sits on a secant line connecting two points on that variety. When the two points merge while their coefficients diverge like $\pm 1/\varepsilon$, the secant line tilts into a tangent vector:

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{(v+\varepsilon w)^{\otimes 3} - v^{\otimes 3}}{\varepsilon} = 3\,\operatorname{Sym}\big(v^{\otimes 2}\otimes w\big)

Border-rank tensors live on this tangential variety! In Part IV, we will analyze what happens when rank-two tensor components collide with free weights in singular learning theory.

Hironaka's blowup: give the limit its own coordinates. The finite difference is also the fix. Make the step h a parameter, and write the rank-2 model as

T(a,b,c,a',b',c',h) = \frac{(a+h\,a')\otimes(b+h\,b')\otimes(c+h\,c') - a\otimes b\otimes c}{h}

Multiplied out, the a⊗b⊗c terms cancel, and what is left is a polynomial in h and the vectors, perfectly well defined at h = 0, where it is the derivative a′⊗b⊗c + a⊗b′⊗c + a⊗b⊗c′. This is a blowup at infinity: it adds the limit points that the original twelve parameters could only approach, the way the cradle's bar added the directions at the corner. In the new coordinates the fit reaches W with bounded parameters, and h simply drifts to 0 (Appendix B runs both fits side by side). Stegeman and De Lathauwer use the same limit in practice, as a block-term decomposition that replaces two diverging CP components.

Nash's blowup: record the tangent. Nash's version works on the set of tensors that rank-2 fits can approach, a 7-dimensional surface in the 8-dimensional space of tensors, with W on its edge. It records the tangent plane of that surface at each point. At W the tangent plane is determined by a single rank-one tensor, the one the finite difference is taken around, e₀⊗e₀⊗e₀. So Nash's blowup records exactly the base point a⊗b⊗c of the formula above, and the two blowups land on the same coordinates. This agreement is special to tensors, and Appendix B explains it with a picture you can rotate.

How common this is. Brown, Kileel and Kolda's "The fascinating world of 2×2×2 tensors" maps it out: a random 2×2×2 tensor needs three rank-one pieces with probability 1 − π/4 ≈ 21%, and de Silva and Lim proved that every one of those has no best rank-2 approximation. A rank-2 fit of any of them runs off to infinity as in Figure {fig:tzfit}, though its error levels off above zero.

Compared with standard practice: a penalty on the factor norms (ridge regularization) is the penalty method of section 9, with the same trade-off: it always gives an answer, but the answer depends on the penalty weight. Constraining the factors to stay well conditioned is the constraint method. The blown-up chart is the third option: keep the limit as a legitimate answer, in new coordinates. And factorization has a gentler kind of singularity that matters even when everything converges: the redundant parameters. Those are the subject of Part IV.

11. Symmetric frame fields: where should the singularities go?

The third problem is the one I've spent the most time on: designing a cross field to guide a quad mesh. Every such field on a disk has to have singular points, and the whole question is where they go. Try it yourself first.

Why this example belongs here. A cross-field solver is an automatic singularity-placement machine: nobody tells it where the irregular vertices go, it decides. Each of its singular points is a point where the field has no direction, exactly the kind of point a blowup replaces by all its directions (section 3), and the competing methods below differ in the same way Hironaka and Nash do: some choose the singular points first and fill in the rest, others let them appear wherever an energy puts them. The question this section leads to, whether resolution machinery can do the placing itself, is taken up again in section 17.

Figure {fig:crossdrag}. A cross field on a disk, aligned with its boundary, with its singular points (the dots) wherever you drag them. The color is the cross's direction, so each singular point is a little color wheel. The bars show the field's Ginzburg–Landau energy (explained below) for your layout, for the best layout of four points, and for all four merged into one point. "Let the energy decide" rolls downhill from wherever you put them. "Merge" pushes the four into one, and the energy shoots up.

New terms: cross field, representation vector, winding number, Dirichlet energy, Ginzburg–Landau energy, vortex core, lift.

To cover a shape with quadrilaterals, a common first step is a cross field: at every point, four directions at right angles, aligned with the boundary and as smooth as possible inside. A cross at angle θ looks the same after a quarter turn, so represent it by the unit complex number u = e4iθ, its representation vector. The quarter-turn ambiguity is gone, and smoothness of crosses becomes ordinary smoothness of u. Being a unit vector is the polynomial equation |u|² = 1.

Why the field must have singular points. On a disk, aligning with the boundary forces u = e4iφ at the boundary point at angle φ, so u turns around the circle four times as you walk once around the edge: its winding number is 4. A continuous unit field inside cannot do that, for the same topological reason that a hairy ball can't be combed flat, so somewhere inside the field must break. Those break points are the singular points, and they become the irregular vertices of the quad mesh: winding +1 in u is a quarter turn of the cross, a valence-3 vertex.

Why a singular point costs infinite energy. "As smooth as possible" usually means the Dirichlet energy E(u) = ½∫|∇u|², the same energy whose minimizers are harmonic functions in every smoothing and interpolation problem in graphics. Near a point where u winds d times, u looks like eidφ in polar coordinates (r, φ), so |∇u| = d/r. The energy in the ring between radius r and 2r is then

\frac12\int_r^{2r}\Big(\frac{d}{\rho}\Big)^2\,2\pi\rho\,d\rho = \pi d^2\log 2

the same for every ring. Every halving of the distance to the singular point costs the same amount, and there are infinitely many halvings, so the energy is infinite. As posed, the problem has no solution: every unit field that matches the boundary has infinite energy. Some modeling choice has to enlarge the problem, and the methods below differ exactly in what they enlarge.

Ginzburg–Landau. The oldest fix comes from superconductivity. In Ginzburg and Landau's 1950 theory, u is a complex number describing the superconducting electrons, |u|² is their density, and in a magnetic field the material threads itself with vortices, points where |u| drops to zero and the phase of u winds around. For fields, the idea is to let u leave the unit circle and charge a penalty for doing so:

E_\varepsilon(u) = \frac12\int|\nabla u|^2 + \frac{1}{4\varepsilon^2}\int\big(1-|u|^2\big)^2

Now u may shrink to zero at a point, and a field of length zero has no direction, so winding around that point is no contradiction. Shrinking costs penalty, so it happens only in a core of radius about ε, the length scale set by the penalty (the superconductor's coherence length). Outside the core |u| ≈ 1 and the halvings argument applies, but only down to radius ε, which is about log₂(1/ε) halvings. So a core of winding d costs

E \approx \pi d^2\log\frac1\varepsilon + C_d

finite for every ε > 0, with Cd a constant from inside the core. As ε → 0 the cores shrink to points and the field approaches a unit field with point singularities; the log(1/ε) is the infinite cost above, cut off at the core.

Back to the demo: why the cores split, and where they go. The d² is the whole story of splitting: one core of winding 4 costs about 16·π log(1/ε), four cores of winding 1 cost about 4·π log(1/ε), and as ε shrinks the difference grows without bound. So a Ginzburg–Landau field never keeps a combined core; that is the orange bar in Figure {fig:crossdrag}. Once separated, the cores interact. Two cores of the same sign repel, with an energy that grows like −log of their distance, like charged particles in the plane, and the boundary condition pushes them back in. Bethuel, Brezis and Hélein worked out this leftover, finite part of the energy, the renormalized energy; on a disk it has a closed form, and it is exactly what the demo evaluates as you drag:

W(a_1,\dots,a_4) = -\pi\sum_{i\neq j}\log|a_i-a_j| \;-\; \pi\sum_{i,j}\log|1-\bar a_i a_j|

The first sum is the repulsion; the second is the boundary pushing back. Four points on a circle of radius ρ minimize it when ρ⁸ = 3/11, ρ ≈ 0.85: the familiar O-grid of a quad-meshed disk, and the layout "Let the energy decide" rolls into. The field drawn in the demo is the matching closed form u = Π (z − aᵢ)(1 − āᵢz)/|…|, which winds once around each aᵢ and points the right way on the boundary.

Viertel and Osting brought this to quad meshing, and Macq and coauthors use it to place singularities. In the language of section 6, Ginzburg–Landau is a penalty method: like soft springs for collisions, it never forms the singular point itself, it smooths it over at scale ε and lets the energy decide. Blowing up the center instead means drilling a small hole and letting the field wind around it: the cradle's move, replacing a bad point by its circle of directions, with the center chosen in advance. Macq and coauthors use exactly this construction to place singularities. It wins only once the hole is wider than about ε1/4, and it gives a single 4-fold singularity, which no quad mesh can have.

The Burer–Monteiro lift is newer to me, so here it is from the start. On a mesh, the field is one unit vector uᵢ ∈ ℝ² per vertex (the representation vector, so a cross is one vector), and smoothness is the sum over edges of |uᵢ − uⱼ|². That energy is quadratic; the only hard part is the constraint |uᵢ| = 1. This is a synchronization problem, and its standard convex relaxation replaces the vectors by their matrix of inner products, Xij = uᵢ·uⱼ, asking only that X be positive semidefinite with ones on the diagonal: a semidefinite program. Burer and Monteiro (2003) noticed that you can solve that program without ever forming X, by factoring X = VᵀV with V of only k rows. That is the same as giving each vertex a unit vector in ℝk instead of ℝ² and minimizing the same edge energy by gradient descent on spheres. For k large enough, about √(2n) for n vertices, every local minimum of the factored problem is a global minimum of the relaxation (Boumal, Voroninski and Bandeira 2016), and in practice much smaller k is enough. Pacheco-Tallaj, Couplet, Chien and Palmer (SIGGRAPH 2026) use this lift for field design. A sphere in three or more dimensions has no hole for the boundary loop to wind around, so the lifted field needs no singularity at all: near the trouble spot it simply leaves the plane, as liquid crystals do when their defects "escape into the third dimension". You get a field back by projecting onto the plane, and singular points appear wherever the projection vanishes.

On this disk the lift's best answer is striking. Its energy is 8π, finite, and the lifted field wraps once around a hemisphere: the map z ↦ z⁴, which projects back to one 4-fold core, the drilled hole's answer. Because the lift charges nothing extra for a combined core, it has no reason to split it. In practice the core does split, into four cores at radius about 0.22, but only because the grid perturbs z⁴ into roughly z⁴ − δ with δ ≈ 0.002, whose roots sit at |z| = δ1/4. That exponent is the Newton polygon of section 3.4 again, now saying how fast a degenerate singularity splits apart. Layouts near the giant core are nearly free in the lifted energy, a very flat valley. Section 13 comes back to what should decide among them.

In three dimensions, octahedral frame fields for hexahedral meshing (Ray and Sokolov; Palmer, Bommes and Solomon) have singular curves instead of points, and the same four choices reappear with higher stakes. The graph lift is the one I'd most like to compare directly; its singularities are part of the optimization's boundary condition rather than something placed by hand.

12. The three demos side by side, and the usual physics tools

Each demo in Part III had one bad point, and each was handled in the same three ways. A penalty smooths the bad point over and gives one answer that depends on a modeling constant. Picking a chart gives one answer that depends on a choice nobody wrote down. A blowup keeps every answer, organized by the direction you arrived from.

Applied mathematicians already use weighted blowups, one singularity at a time. McGehee's 1974 blowup of the collision in the Kepler problem and the "blowup method" of geometric singular perturbation theory (Dumortier and Roussarie; Krupa and Szmolyan) are section 3.4 done by hand, and similarity solutions for shocks are weighted blowups by another name. What Hironaka adds is a guarantee that works in every dimension, an algorithm that never has to guess where to blow up, a way to keep every outcome rather than pick one, and now a machine-checked proof.

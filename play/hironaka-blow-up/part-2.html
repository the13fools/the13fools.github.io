What is… a blowup? · Part II of IV

A cone, its directions, and the limits of Nash blowups

Replace a singular point by its directions, then explore the higher-dimensional limits of Nash blowups.

At the tip of a cone, many paths meet at one point. Blowing up the tip keeps track of their directions. We can see this operation explicitly on the real surface $X^2 + Y^2 = Z^2$: its strict transform is smooth, and a circle of directions sits above the old apex.

This example illustrates an ordinary point blowup, one of the operations used in resolution of singularities. It also helps separate two questions: what does a blowup do locally, and does a particular rule for choosing blowups always resolve every singularity?

6. Dimension belongs to the space

Key terms: ambient dimension, hypersurface, regular point, singular point.

A hypersurface $F(x,y,z,w)=0$ in four ambient coordinates is locally three-dimensional wherever $\nabla F\neq0$. The implicit function theorem gives a tangent 3-plane there. For a reduced hypersurface, vanishing of all first derivatives signals a singular point; it does not mean the tangent space has collapsed to dimension zero.

For example, $x^2+y^2-z^2-w^2=0$ has a singularity at the origin. Its $w=0$ slice is the two-dimensional cone in Figure 10. Neither the slice nor the three-dimensional hypersurface is a four-dimensional variety. The four-dimensional Nash counterexample below concerns the dimension of the variety itself.

7. Replacing the apex by directions

Key terms: direction chart, total transform, strict transform, exceptional locus.

Blow up the origin of real three-space. In the chart where the direction's $Z$ coordinate is nonzero, write

X=uv,\qquad Y=uw,\qquad Z=u.

Away from $u=0$, the new coordinates are $v=X/Z$ and $w=Y/Z$. The coordinate $u$ is signed; it is not Euclidean distance. Substitution gives the total transform:

X^2+Y^2-Z^2=u^2(v^2+w^2-1)=0.

The strict transform is the closure of the lifted cone away from the center. Removing the exceptional factor $u^2$ leaves

v^2+w^2=1,\qquad u\in\mathbb{R}.

This is a cylinder in $(v,w,u)$ coordinates. Its gradient $(2v,2w,0)$ never vanishes on the surface, so it is smooth. A convenient exact parametrization and its blowdown map are

(v,w,u)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,u)\quad\longmapsto\quad(X,Y,Z)=(u\cos\theta,u\sin\theta,u).

At $u=0$, all values of $\theta$ map to $(0,0,0)$. The apex has been replaced by a circle of directions, not by a hole of a prescribed physical radius. Away from that circle, the map is an isomorphism onto the cone away from its apex.

Which exceptional divisor? In the ambient blowup, the fiber over the origin is $\mathbb{RP}^2$, the space of lines through the origin. On the cone's strict transform, the exceptional locus is the projective conic $[X:Y:Z]$ satisfying $X^2+Y^2=Z^2$, whose real points form a circle. Every real point of this conic has $Z\neq0$, so this chart covers the entire real exceptional circle. Complex points require the other charts as well.

In the $X$-direction chart, the same substitution reads $X=a$, $Y=ab$, $Z=ac$, and the strict transform has equation $c^2-b^2=1$ with $a$ free. That is a hyperbola times a line. Different chart drawings describe the same resolution; their shapes and scales need not look alike.

8. What the Nash counterexamples prove

Key terms: Gauss graph, Nash blowup, normalization, recurring affine chart.

The Nash blowup records each smooth point together with its tangent $d$-plane, then takes the algebraic closure of that graph:

\operatorname{Nash}(X)=\overline{\{(p,T_pX):p\in X_{\mathrm{smooth}}\}}\subset X\times\operatorname{Gr}(d,N).

The normalized Nash blowup additionally normalizes the resulting variety. This distinction matters: the two operations have different known termination results.

For the cusp $x=\tau^2$, $y=\tau^3$, the recorded slope is $3\tau/2$, and the Nash lift is smooth after one step. But success on a cusp does not imply success on all varieties.

Castillo, Duarte, Leyton-Álvarez, and Liendo construct normal singular affine toric varieties of every dimension $d\geq4$ whose Nash and normalized Nash blowups, in characteristic zero, contain an open affine subset isomorphic to the original singular variety:

U\subset\operatorname{Nash}(X),\qquad U\cong X.

The same local singularity can therefore recur under iteration. This proves nontermination without asserting that the entire blown-up variety returns to its starting state in a three- or four-step orbit. The four-dimensional construction in Part I lives in seven ambient coordinates, so its Gauss map takes values in $\operatorname{Gr}(4,7)$.

The authors' November 2025 paper also gives a three-dimensional characteristic-zero counterexample for non-normalized Nash blowups. It does not settle the normalized three-dimensional question.

The three-dimensional obstruction, in its exponent lattice

The three-dimensional construction starts with the semigroup $S\subset\mathbb{Z}^3$ generated by six vectors $h_i$, the columns of

B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&-2&1&2\\0&1&0&-1&-1&-2\\0&0&1&2&1&1\end{pmatrix}.

Each vector is the exponent of a monomial. The associated three-dimensional variety $X(S)$ is the algebraic closure of the image of

\phi:(\mathbf{k}^*)^3\longrightarrow\mathbf{k}^6,\qquad(a,b,c)\longmapsto\left(a,b,c,\frac{c^2}{a^2b},\frac{ac}{b},\frac{a^2c}{b^2}\right).

Why there is a singularity to begin with. The missing point $u=(0,-1,1)$ has $3u=h_4+h_6\in S$, yet $u\notin S$. To check the latter, grade a vector by $L(x,y,z)=x+2y+3z$. All six generators have positive integer grade, and only $h_1$ and $h_6$ have grade one; neither equals $u$, whose grade is one. Thus the semigroup is not saturated, its toric variety is not normal, and it cannot be smooth.

Why two Nash blowups do not escape. The first selected chart has eight generators $g_i$; it even contains the old missing point as $g_2=h_5-h_1=u$. A chart of its Nash blowup has six generators $t_i$. Their relation to the original six is the exact integer identity

B_2=U_3B,\qquad U_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&4&2\\-2&-1&-1\\1&-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det U_3=1.

Since $U_3$ is invertible over the integer lattice, it identifies the full semigroups $T=U_3S$ and $S$, not just their real cones. That identifies the coordinate rings and hence the varieties $X(T)\cong X(S)$. The same singular affine chart is available again after two further iterations, and so on. Applying $U_3^{-1}$ in the explorer is only a relabeling, not another blowup.

The missing point matters: passing to a cone alone forgets which lattice points are absent, and normalizing fills such holes. This example concerns ordinary Nash blowups of a non-normal threefold; it does not disprove termination for normalized Nash blowups in dimension three.

Hironaka's theorem guarantees that suitable sequences of blowups along smooth centers resolve varieties in characteristic zero. Constructive proofs organize those choices using invariants that decrease. The Nash counterexamples show that a particular canonical operation can fail; they do not show that resolution requires an arbitrary choice or that every singularity has a visible “throat” to open.

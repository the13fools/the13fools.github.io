A companion to What is… a blowup? and Two ways to blow up

Resolving Newton's cradle

When a ball hits a row of touching balls, every contact closes at the same instant. Physics with hard contacts doesn't say what happens next. There are three ways to make it say something.

Bubble sortNumber the contacts. Repeatedly apply a two-ball collision at the first contact that is still closing. One answer, but it can depend on the numbering.
Hironaka: blow upPull the balls slightly apart, by tiny gaps in every possible ratio. Each ratio gives a definite sequence of two-ball collisions. Keep every outcome, and how much of the space of ratios gives it.
Nash-style: smooth itChoose no order at all. Replace hard contact with very stiff springs, so every contact acts at once, and take the limit. One answer, but it depends on the spring law.

What to try

Equal masses. Every point of the map has the same color, the bubble sort agrees in both numberings, and one ball flies out: the textbook cradle. With equal masses a collision just swaps two velocities, and any sequence of swaps ends with the velocities sorted, so every approach order lands in the same place. In the language of the main page, this is the good case where the contacts don't interfere, like a corner of 60° or 90°.

Change one mass. With a light middle, the map splits into chambers: regions of gap ratios with different outcomes. Each bubble-sort numbering lands in one chamber, and which one depends only on how you numbered the contacts. The blowup keeps them all, with their shares.

Look at the springs. The spring rows usually match no chamber. With three balls of masses 1, 3, 1 and both ends coming in, the two chambers are mirror images, and the springs give the symmetric answer that neither sequence produces. With equal masses, the springs spread the motion over several balls, and Hertz contact (exponent 3/2, the law for elastic spheres) spreads it less than linear springs. That spreading is real physics: the clean "one ball out" result relies on the balls not quite touching, a point made by Herrmann and Seitz (1982).

Look at the average. The share-weighted average of the chambers is order-independent, but it loses kinetic energy, so it is not itself an elastic collision. That is why the main page treats averaging as a modeling choice.

How this is a blowup

The trouble spot is the instant when all N − 1 contacts close together. Near it, only the ratios of the gaps matter, not their sizes, because nothing acts between collisions: double every gap and the whole motion happens at double the scale. So Hironaka's move is to replace that single instant with the set of all gap ratios [g₁ : … : g_{N−1}]. For three balls that set is a segment, for four a triangle. Every point of it has a definite outcome, and the map shows it. The bubble sort is one point's worth of that information, chosen by a numbering. The spring limit is the Nash-style move: no center, no choice of order, an answer built from the system's own smooth version. Like Nash's blowup, it is canonical. Unlike Hironaka's, nothing guarantees it agrees with any sequential story, and the force law is a choice in disguise.

For three balls, the main page's Figure 9 shows the same thing in one picture: after rescaling positions by √mass, the two contacts become two walls of a corner, and the two chambers are the two sides of the throw.

The cradle's polynomial

Three balls are the version you can graph. Remove the motion of the center of mass and rescale each position by √mass. What's left is a plane, and in it the two contacts are two walls through the origin meeting at an angle β with cos β = √(m₁m₃ / ((m₁ + m₂)(m₂ + m₃))). Every two-ball collision is a mirror reflection across one of those walls.

Reflect the walls in each other and you get more mirror lines, at angles 0, β, 2β, 3β, and so on. Multiply the equations of those lines together and you get the cradle's polynomial P. With equal masses, β = 60° and the lines close up after three: P = (x₁ − x₂)(x₂ − x₃)(x₃ − x₁). That is the Vandermonde polynomial, and its graph is a monkey saddle. Its three zero lines are the three ways two balls can trade places.

How to read Figure 2. The surface is the graph z = P over a disk, orange where P is positive and blue where it is negative. The floor shows its zero lines, the mirrors. The shaded violet wedge is where the balls are actually in order; its edges are the two contacts. A mirror that cuts through the wedge is drawn in red. The order of collisions stops mattering exactly when no red line appears.

Special masses

Try the presets. At 90°, 60°, 45°, 36°, and 30°, the angles 180°/k, the mirrors close up into k lines and none cuts the wedge. Then P is the harmonic polynomial r^k·sin(kθ), a saddle with k valleys, and every resolution of the impact agrees: bubble sort in either order, every chamber of the blowup, all the same. At √5, β = 72°: the lines still close up, after five, but one passes through the wedge, and the two collision orders disagree. For a generic mass, the lines never close at all, so no polynomial vanishes on every mirror.

These special cradles are exactly the finite reflection groups in the plane, the symmetry groups of regular polygons. Equal masses give the triangle's group; the √2 − 1 middle ball gives the square's. In the first figure you can find them by setting the middle mass and watching the chamber count drop to one.

For N equal balls, P is the Vandermonde polynomial ∏ (xᵢ − xⱼ) over all pairs, of degree N(N − 1)/2, in N − 1 variables once the center of mass is removed. Its zero set cuts space into N! regions, one for each ordering of the balls. The physical region is the one where the balls are in order, and every resolution simply sorts the velocities. With four balls the variables are three, so the zero set is six planes you can see; the triangle map in the first figure is a slice through the physical region.

P also connects back to the zeta function of the main page. For k lines through a point, one blowup separates them, and the same bookkeeping gives Z(s) = (2 + (2 − k)s) / ((1 + s)(2 + ks)). Its largest pole, −2/k, says how sharply P vanishes at the crossing: 2/3 for the monkey saddle of equal masses.

A swarm of perturbations

Here is Hironaka's method run as a particle swarm, in the spirit of ant colony optimization or Stein variational inference. Start every ball touching, at the instant of the simultaneous collision: the center. Then send out particles. Each particle is one small perturbation: every ball after the first gets one number, its gap to the ball before it. Run the collisions from there, one pair at a time, and color the particle by the outgoing velocities.

Does Hironaka pick the biggest region?

No. A blowup doesn't choose; it keeps every region and records how much of the space of directions each one fills. Choosing the biggest would be a mode-seeking rule, like taking the most likely answer instead of the whole distribution, and the "Keep only the biggest region" button shows what that throws away. Bubble sort doesn't pick the biggest region either. It picks whichever region its numbering happens to land in, whatever its size. And "the biggest region" is not even well defined yet: with a light middle on four balls, region C is biggest when each gap is uniform in [0, ε], but region A is biggest under the other two ways of perturbing.

Two features of the picture explain why there is no natural winner here.

  • Every region is a cone. Colors depend only on the direction of the perturbation, never its size, which is why shrinking ε changes nothing but the zoom. So every region's volume shrinks at exactly the same rate as ε goes to 0, and each keeps a fixed share. Those shares are what live on the blowup's exceptional set: the swarm's directions.
  • The shares depend on how you perturb. Switch between "uniform in [0, ε]", "half-Gaussian" and "every direction equally likely" and the percentages move, though the colors do not. Volume needs a measure, and the collision itself doesn't supply one.

Singular learning theory is the case where volume does pick a winner. There the regions are sets of near-optimal parameters, and different singularities make their volumes shrink at different rates, ε^λ with different λ. As data grows, the posterior concentrates on the region with the smallest λ, the one with the most volume near the optimum, and the free energy grows like λ·log n. In the cradle every region has the same exponent, so nothing takes over; only the constant shares differ.

The swarm also differs from ant colonies and Stein variational inference in one way: its particles never move toward better places, because there is no objective to climb. They only sample. A variational method would move particles to match a target distribution; here the target is just the perturbation distribution itself, and the interesting output is the coloring it reveals.

Watching the blowup

The swarm already contains the blowup; it just needs to be drawn the right way. Since an outcome depends only on the direction of a perturbation, give every particle a new coordinate, its direction, and lift it by that. The collision point, which had every direction at once, gets torn open into a whole set of directions: the exceptional set E. Particles no longer pile up at a point where colors meet. Each color gets its own part of E.

Method Step

Three balls. One blowup is enough. The corner of the gap plane becomes a vertical stalk, one point for each direction, running from "all slack between balls 1 and 2" at the bottom to "all slack between balls 2 and 3" at the top. The lifted swarm settles into horizontal bands, one per outcome, and the stalk is colored in the same bands. Where two colors met at the corner, they now meet only at one point of the stalk, at a definite height.

Four balls, step 1. The corner of gap space opens into a triangle of directions. That is the triangle map of Figure 1 and the slice of Figure 3. Its middle edge point and its interior are fine, but two of its corners are not. At the corner "2·3·4 touch at once", three balls are still colliding simultaneously, a smaller copy of the original problem. With one ball coming in from the left, every region boundary runs into that corner, so all the colors still meet at a point.

Four balls, step 2. Blow up those two corners. Each becomes a new edge, whose points are the directions of approach inside the three-ball cluster. The boundaries that crowded into the corner now hit the new edge at separate points. Nothing is singular anymore, and the triangle has become a pentagon.

That pentagon has a name: it is the associahedron. Its five corners are the five ways to bracket 1·2·3·4. For example ((12)3)4 means balls 1 and 2 close first, at the smallest scale, then 3 joins them, then 4. Its five edges are the single brackets (12), (23), (34), (123), and (234). With N balls the same construction gives the associahedron of dimension N − 2, the standard way to compactify configurations of points on a line. Each face is a nested order in which clusters of balls can form, and each outcome region is a union of pieces of it.

Nash, for comparison. Switch Figure 4 to Nash. Nash lifts the walls, the places where a contact is closed, by their tangent direction. The walls are flat, so one step pushes them apart, and the geometry is resolved. But the particles in between are not on any wall, so Nash leaves them where they were, and all the colors still meet at the corner. Over the collision point Nash puts one point per contact and nothing else. There is no room for directions of approach, so the impact law has to come from somewhere else; the right panel shows the answer from Hertz springs, which usually matches none of the swarm's regions.

Five balls: the blowup in three dimensions

With five balls there are four gaps, so the directions of approach form a solid tetrahedron. The same two rules apply. Wherever three or more neighboring balls can touch at once, blow up, worst first. There are two places where four balls touch (corners of the tetrahedron) and three where three touch (edges). Cutting them off turns the tetrahedron into the three-dimensional associahedron.

Five balls give 14 corners, the 14 ways to bracket five items (a Catalan number), and 9 faces, one for each cluster of neighboring balls: 4 pairs, 3 triples, and 2 quadruples. With N balls the solid has dimension N − 2 and Catalan(N − 1) corners. The blowup turns "everything collides at once" into a polytope whose every face is an honest, nested story of who meets whom first, and the outcome regions are pieces of that polytope.

How each method actually resolves a collision

All three methods start from the same place: every ball touching, one ball arriving, and physics that says nothing about what happens next. They differ in the question they ask to get an answer.

Bubble sort"In what order did I number the contacts?" It fires two-ball impulses at a single instant, always at the first contact still closing, until none is. The answer never depends on how the balls were arranged.
Hironaka"From which direction did the balls approach?" Any direction with all gaps open gives an ordinary sequence of two-ball collisions at different times, so physics has a definite answer. The blowup collects all those answers: one per direction.
Nash"Which contact am I on?" That separates the walls but says nothing about direction, so the impact needs a smooth law. Stiff springs let every contact push at once and give one answer, set by the spring law.

So "resolving" means something different for each. Bubble sort produces an answer by fiat: the numbering. Nash-style smoothing produces an answer by changing the physics, adding springs. Hironaka produces no single answer at all. It produces a function: give it the direction of approach and it returns a definite outcome, found by ordinary two-ball physics. After the blowups that function is well defined everywhere except on the boundaries between regions, which are themselves resolved by blowing up again.

Energy loss. Lower the restitution e in Figure 6 and the three methods part ways again. Hironaka's answer is still one ordinary sequence of collisions per direction. With equal masses, though, once e drops below 7 − 4√3 ≈ 0.072 (the threshold McNamara and Young found for three balls), the middle ball rattles back and forth infinitely many times in a finite time: inelastic collapse. The blowup still gives a limit, the balls ending up together, but no finite list of events reaches it. Bubble sort hits the same wall from the other side. With five equal balls and e near 0, each impulse only halves a relative velocity, so the loop runs forever. That is the Zeno example in Vouga et al., and their remedy is the fourth answer shown: blend the fully sticky answer, which for a row of balls is a mass-weighted isotonic regression of the velocities, with the elastic one, in proportion e.

To turn Hironaka's function into one number you need one more ingredient that the collision doesn't supply: either the actual tiny gaps of the real cradle, which pick a single direction, or a probability over directions, which turns the regions' shares into odds. That is the honest content of the blowup. The ambiguity was real, and the blowup shows exactly what extra information settles it.

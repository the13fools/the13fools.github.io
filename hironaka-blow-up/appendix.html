Appendix A. More on collisions

What the wedge is. In section 6 the three-ball system became one point moving in a plane: the plane of mass-weighted positions with the center of mass removed. The balls are allowed to be where neither gap is negative, and that region is a wedge whose two walls are "balls 1 and 2 touching" and "balls 2 and 3 touching". Collisions are mirror reflections off the walls, and the corner is the moment all three touch.

When the walls close up. Make the middle ball very light and the wedge narrows. A billiard ball entering a narrow wedge bounces about 180°/β times before it escapes, so a light ball trapped between two heavy ones rattles many times in a single "event". (The same geometry is behind Galperin's observation that a heavy ball pushing a light one into a wall produces a number of collisions that spells out digits of π.) In the limit the walls become parallel and touch. Picture one ball pressed between two walls: g₁ = x − a ≥ 0 and g₂ = a − x ≥ 0 together imply g₁ + g₂ = 0, an implied equality. A solver that handles one contact at a time flips the ball off one wall, then back off the other, forever.

Figure A1. The wedge with its angle under your control; the path is the whole three-ball system. Count the bounces as it narrows. At 0° the walls touch.

Three ways to resolve one event. Here is each method of section 6 in more detail.

  • Penalty. Replace hard contact by a stiff spring, F = k·δp for overlap δ (p = 3/2 is Hertz's law for spheres). No choices, one answer, like Nash's blowup; but the answer depends on the force law, which is a modeling decision in disguise.
  • Constraints and impulses. Keep contact hard and solve for impulses as a linear complementarity problem: each contact either separates or pushes. When several contacts are active the solution isn't unique, and the order in which a solver visits them picks one. Visiting pairs in a fixed order (Gauss–Seidel) is, for equal masses on a line, literally a bubble sort of the velocities. That is one chart of the blowup, chosen by the code.
  • Blowup. Keep every chart: all the outcomes, each with its share of the gap ratios. More information than any single solver run, at the price of needing an isolated singular point to work on.

The blowup is the only method that reports every outcome and how likely each is when the true gaps are unknown, as they always are in a real cradle. But it needs an isolated singular point. Where the trouble is a whole line with an equality hidden in it, All's Well's equality detection is the right tool.

B.2 A little tensor, slowly: Hironaka and Nash on one family

New terms: symmetric tensor, binary cubic, tangent developable, cuspidal edge.

Section 7 found the singular problem and wrote down both blowups. Here is a family of tensors small enough to draw, so that both kinds of blowup from Part I can be watched doing their thing in three dimensions.

Tensors you can draw. A 2×2×2 tensor is symmetric if its entries don't change when you permute the three indices, so T₀₀₁ = T₀₁₀ = T₁₀₀ and T₀₁₁ = T₁₀₁ = T₁₁₀. That leaves four numbers, and they are exactly the coefficients of a cubic polynomial:

T \;\longleftrightarrow\; f(x,y)=\sum_{ijk} T_{ijk}\,x_i x_j x_k = T_{000}x^3+3T_{001}x^2y+3T_{011}xy^2+T_{111}y^3

A symmetric rank-one tensor u⊗u⊗u becomes the cube of a linear form, (u₀x + u₁y)³, so decomposing T means writing a cubic as a sum of cubes, which is Sylvester's problem from the 1850s. Scale so that T₀₀₀ = 1 and set y = 1, and every such tensor becomes a monic cubic t³ + a·t² + b·t + c: a point (a, b, c) in ordinary 3D space.

Rank is read off the roots. Whether a 2×2×2 tensor needs two or three rank-one pieces is decided by the sign of Cayley's hyperdeterminant Δ, a quartic polynomial in its eight entries: two where Δ > 0, three where Δ < 0. For symmetric tensors Δ is, up to a factor −1/27, the discriminant of the cubic. (I checked this symbolically; it's a short computation.) So:

  • three distinct real roots: Δ < 0, three rank-one terms needed;
  • one real root and a complex pair: Δ > 0, two terms suffice;
  • a repeated root: Δ = 0, the boundary where fits misbehave;
  • a triple root, (t − r)³: rank one. As r varies these trace a curve, (a, b, c) = (−3r, 3r², −r³), the twisted cubic.

The boundary is made of tangent lines. A cubic with a double root is (t − r)²(t − s). Hold r fixed and vary s: the coefficients are linear in s, so the point moves along a straight line, and at s = r it passes through the rank-one tensor (t − r)³. The line is tangent to the twisted cubic there, because

(t-r)^2(t-s) = (t-r)^3 + \frac{s-r}{3}\,\frac{d}{dr}(t-r)^3

which is the finite difference of the demo once more: a rank-one tensor plus a multiple of its derivative, the limit of a difference of two nearby rank-one tensors divided by their distance. So the boundary surface is swept out by the tangent lines of the rank-one curve, a tangent developable. Graphics people know developables as surfaces that unroll flat, like bent paper. This one is smooth except along the twisted cubic itself, where the paper folds back on itself into a sharp crease, a cuspidal edge. Slice across the crease at r and, after a linear change of coordinates, the slice is the curve (e², 2e³), where e is how far the repeated root has moved from r: the cusp of Figure 3 (y² = 4x³, the same curve rescaled).

That is the payoff of drawing it: the rank-one tensors are a cusp, swept along a curve. Near a rank-one tensor, a small perturbation can land in rank 2, in rank 3, or on the boundary, and the boundary has a crease there.

Hironaka's way. The invariant of section 3.3 is (2, 3/2) all along the crease and smooth elsewhere, so the center is the whole twisted cubic, a smooth curve, which is an allowed center. Blowing up a curve in 3D blows up one point in every plane that crosses it. Locally the surface is the cusp times a line, so every cross-section goes through exactly the cusp column of Figure 5: one blowup leaves the slice smooth but tangent to the new exceptional surface, two more reach normal crossings. In tensor language, the first blowup records, for a tensor near rank one, the direction in which it leaves the nearest rank-one tensor.

Nash's way. Record the tangent plane at each smooth point. Along one of the straight lines (fixed r) the tangent plane never changes; that is what makes the surface developable. So the only thing Nash's lift records is r, that is, which rank-one tensor (t − r)³ this one is a limit around. Lift every point to (point, r): the surface becomes a smooth sheet parametrized by (r, s), the crease is gone, and it took one step, just like the Nash column of Figure 5 for the cusp. The new coordinates are the base point and the offset of the border-rank formula: Nash's blowup of the boundary is the blown-up chart of section 7.

B.3 All 2×2×2 tensors, and the parameter side

The same holds without symmetry. For a general boundary tensor T = a′⊗b⊗c + a⊗b′⊗c + a⊗b⊗c′ (a point on a tangent line of the rank-one tensors), the normal to the boundary, ∇Δ(T), written as a tensor, is again rank one: a⊥⊗b⊥⊗c⊥, where ⊥ rotates a 2-vector by 90°. I checked this numerically at random points. This is no accident. Classically, Δ = 0 is defined as the projective dual of the rank-one tensors: T is on it exactly when the hyperplane of tensors orthogonal to T is tangent to the rank-one tensors somewhere. Biduality then says the tangent hyperplane of the boundary at T is that point of tangency. So Nash's lift of the whole 7-dimensional boundary records (a, b, c) up to scale, the base of the border-rank formula, and because the rank-one tensors form a smooth family, the lifted space is smooth. One Nash step resolves it. Here, unlike the counterexample of section 4, Nash's choice-free recipe is exactly right, and for a structural reason.

Hironaka's algorithm would instead work down the list of degenerate tensors, because the order of Δ there is the first entry of its invariant. Expanding Δ around one example of each kind gives:

Hironaka starts at the zero tensor, where the order is 4; blowing it up just records T/|T|, the usual habit of normalizing a tensor before fitting it. Then come the order-2 families, the rank-one and the biseparable tensors, in an order the second entry of the invariant decides. Every center is a family of degenerate tensors with a name, and each blowup adds a coordinate measuring the departure from that family. It is more work than Nash's single step, but it would work just as well on a family with no duality to lean on.

The parameter side. Learning theory blows up the other space: not the tensors, but the factors. Fit the zero tensor with one rank-one term, so the loss is K = |a⊗b⊗c|² = |a|²|b|²|c|² with a, b, c in ℝ². Its zero set is three 4-dimensional planes (a = 0, b = 0 or c = 0) meeting at the origin. Blow up the origin of each factor's plane, a = ua·(cos θa, sin θa) and likewise for b and c: that is writing each factor as a length times a unit vector, the normalized CP form T = λ·â⊗b̂⊗ĉ every tensor library uses. Then

K = u_a^2\,u_b^2\,u_c^2, \qquad dw = |u_a u_b u_c|\,du\,d\theta

a monomial with a monomial Jacobian: normal crossings, in six dimensions. Three hyperplanes through one point were forbidden for curves in the plane in section 3.2, but in six dimensions three coordinate hyperplanes cross cleanly. Each u contributes (h + 1)/2k = (1 + 1)/2 = 1, all three tie, so λ = 1 with multiplicity m = 3. Those are the numbers quoted for this model in section 11, and the three-way tie is why its finite-n estimate sits well below 1.

B.4 Where rank-2 fits break, in a slice

Reading the hyperdeterminant slice. This figure answers "which targets will a rank-2 fit handle?", one step at a time.

  1. A tensor is a point in 8-dimensional space: a 2×2×2 tensor is just eight numbers. We can't draw 8 dimensions, so we draw a flat 2-dimensional slice through it, the way a CT scan shows one plane of a body.
  2. The slice. Choose a center tensor T₀ and two random directions T₁ and T₂ (random 2×2×2 tensors). The pixel at (x, y) is the tensor T₀ + x·T₁ + y·T₂. The center pixel is T₀ itself.
  3. The color. Compute Δ at each pixel. Blue, Δ > 0: two rank-one terms suffice, and a rank-2 fit converges to an exact answer. Orange, Δ < 0: three are needed, and a rank-2 fit diverges as in Figure 12, because no best rank-2 approximation exists. Black: the boundary Δ = 0, where the answer flips.
  4. What to look for at the center. Through W, the boundary is a single smooth curve: W is an ordinary boundary point, and tiny perturbations of it land on either side. Through the degenerate tensor, two boundary curves cross, like y² = x² in Figure 3. Through the zero tensor, several lines meet, since Δ(t·T) = t⁴Δ(T) makes the boundary a cone. These are the crossing, the smooth curve and the cone of Part I, now living inside tensor space.

Appendix C. How the thermometer's temperature is chosen: Watanabe's WBIC

What WBIC is. To compare models, Bayesian statistics uses the free energy F = −log Z, where Z = ∫ e−nLn(w) φ(w) dw is the probability the model gives the data, averaged over its parameters w with prior φ. Section 10's formula says F ≈ nLn(w*) + λ log n: a fit term plus a complexity term. Z is an integral over every parameter, hopeless to compute for a network. Watanabe's widely applicable Bayesian information criterion (WBIC, 2013) estimates F from samples instead: run the sampler at the single inverse temperature β* = 1/log n, and average the loss,

\mathrm{WBIC} = \mathbb{E}_{\beta^*}\!\left[\,nL_n\,\right], \qquad \beta^* = \frac{1}{\log n}

Watanabe proved that WBIC agrees with F up to terms that don't grow with n as fast as λ log n, even for singular models. The thermometer of Part III divides the same kind of reading by the temperature, which is why it uses the same β.

Why one temperature is enough, in three steps.

  1. Cool slowly. Let β run from 0, where the tempered posterior e−nβLnφ is just the prior and Z = 1, up to 1, the real posterior. As β increases, −log Zβ changes at a rate equal to the average loss at that temperature, Eβ[nLn]. So F is the total change: F = ∫₀¹ Eβ[nLn] dβ. Physicists call this thermodynamic integration.
  2. Equipartition. At inverse temperature β, the average loss above the minimum is about λ/β: the thermometer's reading λ times the temperature 1/β. That holds as long as β is not so small that the posterior spreads over the whole prior, roughly β > 1/n. So F ≈ ∫ λ/β dβ from 1/n to 1, which is λ log n.
  3. One reading. The curve λ/β, averaged over β from 1/n to 1, is about λ log n. It passes through its own average at exactly one point: λ/β* = λ log n, that is, β* = 1/log n. So the single reading Eβ*[nLn] equals the whole integral.

Figure C1. The three steps on the two-parameter losses of Figure 16, computed exactly. The horizontal axis is the inverse temperature β on a log scale, and the curve is β·Eβ[nK]. On these axes the shaded area under the curve is the free energy F, and step 2 says the curve is a flat plateau of height λ, about log n wide. How the figures are computed. Everything runs in your browser. Collisions are exact event-driven simulations; springs use small time steps. Cross fields solve the Ginzburg–Landau gradient flow on an 81 × 81 grid with ε = 0.06; the lifted field minimizes the same grid's smoothness energy over unit vectors in ℝ³ by block-coordinate updates. Tensor fits are plain gradient descent. The cross field you drag (Figure 13) and its energy are closed forms; a single vortex (Figure 14) is a radial Newton solve.

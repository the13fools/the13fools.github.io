What is… a blowup? · Part II of IV

3-Manifolds, Hironaka's radial excavation, and why Nash loops in 4D

Visualizing zero level sets in four dimensions as 3-manifolds, how Hironaka's blowup 1 excavates a pinched throat, and why Nash gets trapped in a 4-step periodic orbit.

Part I resolved singularities on plane curves. But Hironaka's theorem is monumental because it holds across all dimensions. In machine learning, neural network parameter spaces live in thousands or millions of dimensions—and the first fundamental obstruction to automatic resolution appears in four dimensions.

At the close of Part I, we noted that John Nash's choice-free tangent blowup fails in dimension 4 (Castillo, Duarte, Leyton-Álvarez, and Liendo, Annals of Mathematics 2026). How does a 4D singularity actually look, why does Nash get stuck, and what does Hironaka do in his very first step?

The answer is to visualize the zero level set as a 3-manifold. By taking 3D spatial slices through 4-space, we can watch both algorithms operate side by side.

6. Zero level sets in 4D as 3-manifolds

Key terms: 3-manifold, hypersurface, regular point, singular locus, 3D slice.

A single polynomial $F(x, y, z, w) = 0$ in four variables cuts out a 3-manifold in $\mathbb{R}^4$. Wherever the gradient

\nabla F = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial z}, \frac{\partial F}{\partial w}\right) \neq (0, 0, 0, 0)

is non-zero, the implicit function theorem guarantees local smoothness: zoomed in, the space is indistinguishable from flat $\mathbb{R}^3$, with a unique tangent 3-plane orthogonal to $\nabla F$. But where $\nabla F = 0$, the tangent space collapses into a singularity.

To inspect a 4D hypersurface, we slice it along coordinate planes (setting $w = 0$), yielding a 2D surface in $(x, y, z)$:

  • The A₁ Conifold: $x^2 + y^2 - z^2 - w^2 = 0$. At $w = 0$, this is the double cone $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$, meeting at a sharp pinch $(0, 0, 0, 0)$ with throat radius $r = 0$.
  • The A₃ Tacnode: $x^2 + y^2 = z^4 + w^4$. At $w = 0$, two parabolic sheets kiss with second-order contact ($r = z^2$).
  • The Fermat Cubic: $x^3 + y^3 + z^3 + w^3 = 0$. Slicing at $w = 0$ yields a fluted 3-lobed cone with 27 lines radiating from a triple point.
  • The Brieskorn Cusp: $x^2 + y^2 = z^3 + w^2$. Exhibits fractional scaling $r \propto |z|^{3/2}$, creating a 3D needle tip.

7. What Hironaka actually does in Blow-Up 1: Radial excavation

Key terms: radial excavation, exceptional divisor, strict transform.

How does blowing up a singular point turn a pinched cone into an open, smooth manifold? Take the quadratic cone $X^2 + Y^2 - Z^2 = 0$. At the origin $\nabla F = (0, 0, 0)$, and rays approach from every direction on the cone.

1. Express in directional coordinates: Set $X = u, Y = uv, Z = uw$, where $u$ is the radial distance from the origin and $(v, w) = (Y/X, Z/X)$ record the direction of approach.

2. Factor out the center: Substituting into the cone equation gives:

F(u, uv, uw) = u^2 + (uv)^2 - (uw)^2 = u^2\big(1 + v^2 - w^2\big) = 0

The factor $u^2 = 0$ is the exceptional divisor $E_1 \cong \mathbb{RP}^2$: the algebraic memory of the origin replaced by its projectivized sphere of directions.

3. The strict transform is smooth: Dividing out $u^2$ leaves the strict transform:

1 + v^2 - w^2 = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w^2 - v^2 = 1

This is a smooth one-sheeted hyperboloid! Its gradient $(0, -2v, 2w)$ never vanishes because $w^2 - v^2 = 1 \implies w \neq 0$.

Radial excavation: Hironaka's blowup 1 literally drills out the singular apex $r = 0$ and glues in a smooth throat of directions. In Figure {fig:manifold3d}, the waist jumps from $r = 0.00$ to $r = 0.92$: the pinch opens into an accessible, nonsingular channel.

8. The 4D failure of Nash: Why choice-free blowups loop forever

Key terms: Nash blowup, Gauss map, Grassmannian $\operatorname{Gr}(d, N)$, periodic limit cycle.

John Nash proposed a choice-free alternative: the Gauss map $\gamma: p \mapsto T_p X$ assigns to each smooth point its tangent $d$-plane in the Grassmannian $\operatorname{Gr}(d, N)$. The Nash blowup $\widetilde{X}_{\mathrm{Nash}}$ is the closure of its graph:

\widetilde{X}_{\mathrm{Nash}} = \overline{\big\{(p, T_p X) : p \in X_{\mathrm{smooth}}\big\}} \subset X \times \operatorname{Gr}(d, N)

For curves (1D), Nash resolves the cusp in a single step. For surfaces (2D), Spivakovsky (1990) proved that normalized Nash blowups always terminate. It was widely conjectured that Nash blowups always resolve singularities.

The 4D Counterexample: In 2026, Castillo, Duarte, Leyton-Álvarez, and Liendo proved this false in dimension four. Unlike Hironaka, Nash does not excavate the singular point: it retains original positions and merely appends limiting tangent planes. When the Gauss map folds over itself in $\operatorname{Gr}(3, 4)$, the pinch radius stays shut ($r = 0$). In the counterexample, an integer matrix $U \in \operatorname{GL}(4, \mathbb{Z})$ maps the exponent configuration of Step 3 identically back to Step 0:

M^3_0 \;\xrightarrow{\text{Nash}}\; M^3_1 \;\xrightarrow{\text{Nash}}\; M^3_2 \;\xrightarrow{\text{Nash}}\; M^3_3 \;\cong\; M^3_0 \;\xrightarrow{\text{Nash}}\; \cdots

Because Nash is completely deterministic and choice-free, it cannot change tactics: it is trapped in an infinite periodic cycle of length 4.

The takeaway: Choice-free methods like Nash's are appealing because they require no manual heuristics. But in four dimensions and higher, they can march in circles. Hironaka's algorithm succeeds because it insists on picking a center where the invariant is maximal, radially excavates the throat, and strictly decreases its invariant at every step.

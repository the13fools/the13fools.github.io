Appendix A. More on collisions

Narrowing wedges: When the middle ball is very light, the configuration wedge narrows. A billiard ball entering a wedge of angle $\beta$ bounces roughly $180^\circ / \beta$ times before escaping. As $\beta \to 0$, the walls become parallel ($g_1 = x - a \ge 0$ and $g_2 = a - x \ge 0 \implies g_1 + g_2 = 0$), forming an implied equality where pairwise impulse solvers chatter indefinitely.

Figure A1. Narrowing wedge. As angle $\beta$ decreases, bounces multiply; at $0^\circ$, the walls touch.

Billiard path
Bounces against wedge angle

Three resolutions of simultaneous impact:

  • Penalty: Soft contact springs $F = k \delta^p$. Eliminates indeterminacy, but the outcome depends on the chosen force law.
  • Constraints & Impulses: Solves a linear complementarity problem. When multiple contacts are active, the outcome depends on the solver's visiting order (e.g. Gauss–Seidel bubble-sorting velocities).
  • Blowup: Replaces the corner with the space of incoming gap ratios, preserving every possible outcome and its probability share.

B.2 Symmetric tensors as binary cubics: Hironaka vs. Nash

Key terms: binary cubic, twisted cubic, tangent developable, cuspidal edge.

A symmetric $2 \times 2 \times 2$ tensor corresponds to a homogeneous binary cubic with four coefficients. On the chart where the leading coefficient is nonzero, normalize it to one and write $t^3 + a t^2 + b t + c$. For distinct roots, its real symmetric rank is determined by the conventional cubic discriminant $\Delta$:

  • $\Delta > 0$: three distinct real roots, real symmetric rank 3;
  • $\Delta < 0$: one real root, real symmetric rank 2;
  • $\Delta = 0$: repeated root (tangent developable boundary);
  • Triple root $(t - r)^3$: rank one (the twisted cubic curve $(a, b, c) = (-3r, 3r^2, -r^3)$).

For a sign check, $t^3-t$ has three real roots and $\Delta=4$, while $t^3+t$ has one real root and $\Delta=-4$. The boundary $\Delta = 0$ is swept out by tangent lines to the twisted cubic—a tangent developable creased along the rank-one curve into a sharp cuspidal edge $(e^2, 2e^3)$. Hironaka's algorithm blows up the twisted cubic curve in three steps. Nash's lift records the root $r$, smoothing the crease into a flat sheet in a single step!

B.3 Projective duality and parameter monomialization

For general $2 \times 2 \times 2$ tensors, the boundary $\Delta = 0$ is the projective dual of the rank-one variety. At a smooth point of the boundary, its tangent hyperplane identifies a contact point on the rank-one variety. Biduality describes this correspondence; it does not by itself prove that the Nash blowup resolves every singular stratum of the boundary in one step.

On the parameter side, fitting the zero tensor with a single rank-one term $K = |a|^2 |b|^2 |c|^2$ has three intersecting hyperplanes ($a = 0, b = 0, c = 0$). Polar coordinates $a = u_a \hat{a}, b = u_b \hat{b}, c = u_c \hat{c}$ yield the monomial loss $K = u_a^2 u_b^2 u_c^2$ and Jacobian $dw = u_a u_b u_c du d\theta$. Each factor gives $(1+1)/2 = 1$, producing $\lambda = 1$ with multiplicity $m = 3$.

Appendix C. Why the thermometer uses temperature $\beta^* = 1/\log n$ (WBIC)

Watanabe's Widely Applicable Bayesian Information Criterion (WBIC) uses a tempered-posterior expectation at inverse temperature $\beta^*=1/\log n$ to approximate the Bayesian free energy asymptotically:

\mathrm{WBIC}=\mathbb{E}_{\beta^*}[nL_n(w)],\qquad \beta^*=\frac{1}{\log n}.

With a normalized prior, $Z(0)=1$, so thermodynamic integration gives the exact identity

-\log Z(1)=\int_0^1\mathbb{E}_\beta[nL_n(w)]\,d\beta.

For intuition, an idealized excess-energy curve $\mathbb{E}_\beta[nK]\approx\lambda/\beta$ contributes approximately $\lambda\log n$ between inverse temperatures of order $1/n$ and $1$. Evaluating that ideal curve at $1/\log n$ gives the same leading term. This explains the temperature scale, but it is not an exact finite-sample mean-value formula. Logarithmic corrections, fluctuations, and sampling error remain; one temperature still requires many posterior samples.

Figure C1. Thermodynamic integration and the WBIC plateau. In these toy losses, the curve $\beta \mathbb{E}_\beta[nK]$ approaches a plateau of height $\lambda$; the orange box illustrates the leading $\lambda\log n$ term. It is an asymptotic picture, not an exact integration rule.

How the figures are computed. Everything runs in your browser. Collisions are exact event-driven simulations; springs use small time steps. Cross fields solve the Ginzburg–Landau gradient flow on an 81 × 81 grid with ε = 0.06; the lifted field minimizes the same grid's smoothness energy over unit vectors in ℝ³ by block-coordinate updates. Tensor fits are plain gradient descent. The cross field you drag (Figure 15) and its energy are closed forms; a single vortex (Figure 16) is a radial Newton solve. Figures 19, 21, 22, 25 and C1 integrate exactly on grids; Figures 18 and 26 run Langevin chains live. The sampled points in Figure 24 come from offline Langevin sampling of the full posterior (500 to 1000 chains at n = 1000, Gaussian prior).

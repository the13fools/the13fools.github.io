What is… a blowup? · Part IV of IV

Singular learning theory

The local learning coefficient, two perfect fits, and how tensor collisions create normal-crossing divisors and logarithmic evidence.

The idea in one paragraph. Nudge a trained model's weights at random, retaining only perturbations that barely alter the loss. How much parameter room is available? Regular asymptotic theory describes a minimum by a quadratic bowl, whose sublevel volume contracts with exponent $d/2$. Neural network loss landscapes can instead contain degenerate minima and flat valleys of equivalent functions. The local learning coefficient $\lambda$ measures this room directly: $\lambda = d/2$ for a regular bowl, but $\lambda < d/2$ in the degenerate examples below. Under its analytic and statistical assumptions, Singular Learning Theory relates $\lambda$ to Bayesian evidence and excess generalization loss. We can estimate it with what amounts to a statistical thermometer.

13. Why singular models learn differently: two perfect fits

Consider a toy model with two parameters and two settings that fit the training data with zero error: one regular quadratic bowl ($K \propto r^2$) and one degenerate flat basin ($K \propto r^4$). Classical empirical risk cannot distinguish them. Which one does Bayesian inference select?

Figure 18. Posterior samples as sample size $n$ increases (blue = regular, orange = degenerate). Both achieve 0 training error. Yet as $n$ grows, the degenerate fit captures nearly all posterior mass: volume, not depth, decides the winner.

Key terms: equipartition, Hessian, harmonic approximation, free energy, learning coefficient.

From physics to Bayesian learning: At temperature $T = 1/n$, a particle in an empirical potential $K(w)$ visits parameter states according to the Gibbs posterior $p(w) \propto e^{-n K(w)} \varphi(w)$. In a regular quadratic well, the harmonic approximation $K(w) \approx \frac{1}{2} w^\top H w$, with positive-definite $H$, gives the sublevel volume $V(\varepsilon)=\operatorname{Vol}\{K<\varepsilon\}$:

V(\varepsilon) \approx \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2 + 1)\sqrt{\det H}}\,(2\varepsilon)^{d/2}

Where classical statistics fails: Non-identifiability, dead units, and continuous factorization symmetries can produce minima with zero Hessian eigenvalues ($\det H=0$). Discrete permutation symmetries alone do not force a zero eigenvalue. The regular Laplace approximation then fails: dividing by $\sqrt{\det H}$ is not a valid way to estimate the volume. The usual AIC and BIC formulas can still be evaluated, but their regular-model justifications need not apply.

Sumio Watanabe resolved this impasse by applying Hironaka's resolution of singularities. Pulling back the loss $K(w)$ along an embedded resolution map $w = g(u)$ monomializes both the loss and the volume element into normal-crossing divisors:

K(g(u)) = u_1^{2k_1} u_2^{2k_2} \cdots u_d^{2k_d}, \qquad |J(u)| = |u_1^{h_1} u_2^{h_2} \cdots u_d^{h_d}|

Under the analytic and prior regularity assumptions of singular learning theory, these local forms determine the leading asymptotic terms through the Real Log Canonical Threshold (RLCT) $\lambda$. With nonvanishing analytic factors suppressed, take the minimum below over contributing resolution charts and indices with $k_i>0$:

\lambda = \min_{i} \left( \frac{h_i + 1}{2k_i} \right), \qquad F_n \approx n S_n + \lambda\log n - (m-1)\log\log n, \qquad \mathbb{E}[G_n^{\mathrm{excess}}] \approx \frac{\lambda}{n}

Here $m$ is the multiplicity of the leading pole, $S_n$ is the empirical negative log likelihood of the true distribution, and the last relation concerns expected Bayesian excess generalization loss in the realizable, unit-temperature setting. These are asymptotic statements under the relevant SLT hypotheses, not a general finite-sample guarantee for a trained neural network.

  • Volume Scaling: Near a singular minimum, parameter volume shrinks as $V(\varepsilon) \asymp \varepsilon^{\lambda} (\log \frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{m-1}$. A network with $d = 100{,}000$ parameters and $\lambda = 42$ contracts under noise as if it had only $2\lambda = 84$ parameters!
  • Bayesian Occam's Razor: In model selection, the posterior evidence scales as $n^{-\lambda}(\log n)^{m-1}$. For equally good fits and comparable prior weights, smaller $\lambda$ gives a smaller leading complexity penalty. Differences in fit and prior weight also matter.
  • Generalized Equipartition: While a quadratic bowl stores an average thermal energy of $\frac{d}{2}T$, a singular potential stores an average excess energy of $\mathbf{\lambda \cdot T}$.

14. Where singularities come from in networks: one function, many weights

Neural networks are inherently singular because the parameter-to-function map $w \mapsto f_w$ is non-injective:

  • Rescaling Symmetry: A ReLU unit satisfies $a \cdot \operatorname{relu}(wx) = (a/c)\cdot\operatorname{relu}(cwx)$ for any $c > 0$, creating 1D curves of identical functions.
  • Dead Units: If an outgoing weight vanishes ($a = 0$), all incoming weights can vary freely without changing the network's output.
  • Unit Merging: When two hidden units share identical incoming weight vectors, they collapse into a single effective unit.
  • Matrix Factorization Bottlenecks: Any product $W = BA$ exhibits a continuous $\operatorname{GL}(r)$ gauge symmetry $A \to GA, B \to BG^{-1}$.

Linear networks are matrix factorizations: A two-layer network $y = BAx$ with hidden width $H$ factors $W = BA$. Adding hidden units increases parameter count $d = H(M+N)$ linearly. The Aoyagi–Watanabe formula depends on $M,N,H$ and the true matrix rank $r$. In the plotted family, it reaches $MN/2$ at sufficiently large width. This is half the dimension of the full matrix space; the rank-$r$ matrix stratum instead has dimension $r(M+N-r)$.

15. Measuring it: the local learning coefficient

Explicit resolution charts are usually impractical for modern neural networks. Lau and colleagues propose estimating the local learning coefficient (LLC) using a localized tempered posterior and Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD).

For the ideal local calculation, take a neighborhood $B$ on which $w^*$ minimizes $L_n$, and center the excess loss: $K_n(w)=L_n(w)-L_n(w^*)\geq0$. Define

Z_c(\beta)=\int_B\exp\!\left(-n\beta K_n(w)-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|w-w^*\|^2\right)dw,\qquad p_\beta(w)=\frac{\mathbf{1}_B(w)}{Z_c(\beta)}\exp\!\left(-n\beta K_n(w)-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|w-w^*\|^2\right).

Assume the nonnegative local loss admits the analytic resolution used above and the local weight is positive and smooth near the relevant minima. Its centered partition function then has leading scale $Z_c(\beta)\asymp(n\beta)^{-\lambda}(\log(n\beta))^{m-1}$ as $n\beta\to\infty$. Differentiation gives the exact expectation identity, followed by its leading asymptotic form:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}[-\log Z_c(\beta)]=n\mathbb{E}_\beta[K_n(w)]\approx\frac{\lambda}{\beta}-\frac{m-1}{\beta\log(n\beta)}.

Centering matters: an uncentered partition function includes $e^{-n\beta L_n(w^*)}$, and its free-energy derivative is $n\mathbb{E}_\beta[L_n(w)]$. Subtracting the baseline loss leads to the practical estimator

\hat\lambda(w^*)=n\beta\left(\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}L_n(w_s)-L_n(w^*)\right).

At inverse temperature $\beta^*=1/\log n$, this is a generalized equipartition measurement of local complexity. Finite chains introduce discretization and mixing error, and the logarithmic correction above need not be negligible. A quadratic spring provides soft localization in the demo; its strength changes which region is sampled. A finite SGLD estimate is not an exact LLC, and applying analytic SLT results to piecewise-analytic networks such as ReLU networks requires additional care.

16. Tensor collisions, free weights, and the log N multiplicity

Key terms: component collision, component emptying, normal-crossing divisors, double pole.

What happens when components have free mixture weights and can collide? Consider the symmetric rank-2 cubic tensor model in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

T(a, b, \theta) = \theta\,v(a)^{\otimes 3} + (1-\theta)\,v(b)^{\otimes 3}, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 1

learning the rank-one target $T_* = e_1^{\otimes 3}$. The true tensor can be achieved in two distinct geometric ways: component collision ($a = b = 0$) or component emptying ($\theta = 0$ or $\theta = 1$).

In center and separation coordinates $s = \theta a + (1-\theta)b$ and $u = \|a - b\|^2$, the boundary where a component empties ($\theta \to 0$) becomes an exact normal-crossing product:

K_{\mathrm{sing}}(\theta, u) \sim (\theta\cdot u)^2

Integrating the evidence $Z_N = \int e^{-N K} dw$ across the intersecting divisors $\theta = 0$ and $u = 0$ yields:

Z_N \asymp N^{-1} \cdot N^{-1/2}\log N = \mathbf{N^{-3/2}\log N}

The RLCT is $\lambda = 3/2$, and the multiplicity jumps to $\mathbf{m = 2}$: the double pole of Watanabe's zeta function is the geometric intersection of component collision and component emptying!

The Rousseau–Mengersen boundary

For $a, b \in \mathbb{R}^q$ with a Dirichlet prior $\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\alpha-1}$, the RLCT is:

\lambda_{\mathrm{RLCT}} = \frac{q}{2} + \min\left(\frac{q}{4}, \; \frac{\alpha}{2}\right), \qquad m = \begin{cases} 2, & \alpha = q/2 \\ 1, & \alpha \neq q/2 \end{cases}

At the critical boundary $\mathbf{\alpha = q/2}$ (Rousseau & Mengersen 2011), component merging and emptying balance, creating the logarithmic evidence factor $N^{-\lambda}\log N$.

17. Where I'd like to take this

Hironaka's resolution theorem supplies a geometric foundation for Singular Learning Theory: near singular minima, evidence and excess loss can depend on $\lambda$ rather than raw parameter count. For basins with equal minimum loss and comparable positive prior weight, smaller $\lambda$ gives a larger leading asymptotic evidence contribution, with a power-law advantage in $n$ up to logarithmic factors. These Bayesian results do not by themselves prove that gradient descent selects low-LLC basins or explain all neural-network generalization. They give a precise framework for investigating those questions.

With resolution of singularities now formally verified in Lean 4 without a single sorry, the deep algebraic machinery that underpins learning theory has become machine-checked bedrock—a powerful milestone for mathematical physics, geometry processing, and AI safety.

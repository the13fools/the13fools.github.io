What is… a blowup? · Part IV of IV

Singular learning theory: Volume, Networks, and the Thermometer

Why flatter valleys dominate Bayesian evidence, how blowups monomialize non-separable loss landscapes, and how a statistical thermometer measures neural network capacity.

In classical statistics, parameters are assumed to be regular and isolated. Every local minimum is approximated by a quadratic Taylor bowl, leading to familiar formulas like the Laplace approximation, AIC, and BIC—where every parameter claims an equal half-unit of complexity ($d/2$).

Modern machine learning shatters this assumption. Internal model symmetries and overparameterization fold the set of optimal parameters into continuous singular varieties. When multiple configurations achieve low training loss, how does learning choose between them? It integrates over parameter volume: the flatter, more degenerate valley possesses vastly more nearby room.

13. Count nearly correct parameters & Bayesian weight

Part III's tensor collision gave us a loss that is quadratic in the mean $s$ and quartic in the separation $d$. We now compare its local model $K_{\rm collision}=s^2+d^4$ alongside an ordinary quadratic loss $K_{\rm regular}=s^2+d^2$. Both have two parameters and the same perfect fit at $(0,0)$.

13.1 Parameter volume: room around the fit

Allow a small error tolerance: $K\le\varepsilon$. The regular fit gives a circular disk. The collision fit leaves a much longer band along the separation direction, because fourth powers stay small longer than squares.

Figure {fig:learningroom}. Press Demand 10× less error. Both regions shrink, but the collision region shrinks less. Read the fraction of the same parameter square that remains acceptable. The axes and shading scales are identical.

Regular: s² + d²

Collision: s² + d⁴

The learning coefficient is a shrinking rate

For the regular disk, each direction has width proportional to $\varepsilon^{1/2}$. Multiplying the widths gives an area scaling as $\varepsilon^{1/2} \cdot \varepsilon^{1/2} = \varepsilon^1$. Cutting allowable error tenfold shrinks the acceptable parameter area tenfold.

For the collision region, the mean direction has width $\varepsilon^{1/2}$, but the separation direction has width $\varepsilon^{1/4}$! Multiplying the widths gives an area scaling as $\varepsilon^{1/2} \cdot \varepsilon^{1/4} = \varepsilon^{3/4}$. Cutting allowable error tenfold shrinks the acceptable area by only $10^{3/4} \approx 5.6$ times. The collision retains nearly twice as much acceptable volume!

The power on $\varepsilon$ is the learning coefficient $\lambda$:

\operatorname{Area}(K \le \varepsilon) \asymp \varepsilon^\lambda, \qquad \lambda_{\rm regular} = 1, \qquad \lambda_{\rm collision} = \tfrac{3}{4}.

Smaller $\lambda$ means more parameter room per unit error. The model behaves as if it has $2\lambda = 1.5$ parameters rather than 2.

13.2 Bayesian evidence: adding up parameter weight

Now weight each parameter configuration by its likelihood on $n$ independent observations: $p(w) \propto e^{-n K(w)}$. The total integrated likelihood is the Bayesian marginal likelihood, or evidence $Z(n) = \int e^{-n K(w)} dw$.

Figure {fig:learningevidence}. Select n = 100, then n = 10,000. Bright means high relative likelihood, with the same 0–1 color scale in both panels. Both fits concentrate, but the collision keeps a wider bright region. The bar adds up that brightness.

Regular: s² + d²

Collision: s² + d⁴

Why the evidence scales as $n^{-\lambda}$

Integrating Gaussian bowls gives $Z(n) = \int e^{-n(s^2+d^2)} ds dd \asymp n^{-1/2} \cdot n^{-1/2} = n^{-1}$. But for the collision loss, substituting $u = n s^2$ and $v = n d^4$ decouples the integral:

Z(n) = \int e^{-n(s^2+d^4)} ds dd = \left(\int e^{-n s^2} ds\right)\left(\int e^{-n d^4} dd\right) \asymp n^{-1/2} \cdot n^{-1/4} = n^{-3/4}.

Look at the exponent: it is exactly $-\lambda$! In Bayesian model selection, the free energy $F = -\log Z(n)$ penalizes complexity as $\lambda \log n$ rather than the classical BIC penalty $\frac{d}{2} \log n$. Because $\lambda_{\rm collision} = 3/4 < 1$, Bayesian evidence strongly favors the collision fit over the regular fit.

The weighted blowup: how blowing up extracts $\lambda = 3/4$

Why did the exponent $3/4$ appear? Recall the weighted blowups from Part I, Section 3. The loss $s^2 + d^4$ has terms of degree 2 in $s$ and 4 in $d$. Assigning weights $(2, 1)$ puts both terms in degree 4. The chart substitution is:

s = r^2 v, \qquad d = r.

The pulled-back loss factors into a single monomial times a nonvanishing term: $K(r, v) = (r^2 v)^2 + r^4 = r^4(v^2 + 1)$. The Jacobian determinant of this coordinate transformation is $|J| = \frac{\partial(s,d)}{\partial(r,v)} = 2r^2$.

Now look at the volume integral pulled back to the blown-up chart:

Z(n) = \int e^{-n r^4(v^2 + 1)} \cdot (2r^2) dr dv.

Substitute $u = n r^4$, so $r = n^{-1/4} u^{1/4}$ and $dr = \frac{1}{4} n^{-1/4} u^{-3/4} du$. The factor $r^2 dr$ becomes:

r^2 dr = (n^{-1/4} u^{1/4})^2 \cdot \left(\tfrac{1}{4} n^{-1/4} u^{-3/4} du\right) = \tfrac{1}{4} n^{-3/4} u^{-1/4} du.

The powers of $n$ pull completely outside the integral: $n^{-(2+1)/4} = n^{-3/4}$! The leading pole of the zeta function $\int K(w)^{-z} dw$ sits at $z = \lambda = \frac{2+1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}$. The blowup monomialized the loss and the volume form simultaneously!

14. Where the blowup is genuinely needed: The Cusp Well & Cradle Contest

In $s^2 + d^4$, we could read off the scaling because the variables were already separated along coordinate axes. But real singular losses are curved and tangled. What happens when the flat direction is not aligned with any coordinate axis?

The canonical example is the Cusp Well $V = (y^2 - x^3)^2$. The zero-loss set is the Neil parabola $y^2 = x^3$—the exact cusp curve from Part I!

Figure {fig:cuspwell}. Flat-bottomed well $V = (y^2 - x^3)^2$. Left: top view. Right: Hessian eigenvalues along the floor; one is identically zero, and both vanish at the cusp.

The well, from above V = (y² − x³)²
Cross-sections and Hessian

Along the entire parabolic trough $y^2 = x^3$, the loss is zero. The Hessian matrix has one zero eigenvalue along the entire floor. But at the cusp tip $(0,0)$, both eigenvalues vanish. Classical Taylor expansion fails completely.

How do we compute the volume of this well? We cannot separate $x$ and $y$ by independent scalings. But recall Part I, Figure 8: the weighted blowup with weights $(2, 3)$ set $x = r^2, y = r^3 v$. Substituting into the cusp well:

V(r, v) = \big((r^3 v)^2 - (r^2)^3\big)^2 = \big(r^6 v^2 - r^6\big)^2 = r^{12} (v^2 - 1)^2.

The Jacobian determinant is $|J| = 3 r^4$. The evidence integral becomes:

Z(n) \asymp \int r^4 e^{-n r^{12}} dr \asymp n^{-(4+1)/12} = n^{-5/12}.

The learning coefficient of the cusp well is $\lambda = \frac{5}{12} \approx 0.417$. Even though the space is 2-dimensional ($d=2$), parameter volume contracts as if the model had only $2\lambda = 5/6 \approx 0.83$ parameters!

The Cusp vs. Cradle Contest

This reveals a profound contrast between classical physics and singular learning. In Newton's cradle (Part III, Figure {fig:cradle}), the blown-up gap-ratio bar decomposed into distinct chambers whose relative probability shares remained strictly frozen as tolerances shrank. In learning theory, however, a cusp tip dominates everything around it: as dataset size $n \to \infty$ or allowed error $\varepsilon \to 0$, the share of Bayesian posterior mass concentrating at the cusp tip climbs to 100%!

15. Where singularities come from in neural networks

Why do neural networks exhibit these singularities? Because the parameter-to-function map $w \mapsto f_w$ is non-injective: many different weight vectors implement the exact same input-output function.

15.1 One ReLU unit: rescaling and dead units

Consider a single rectified linear unit $f(x) = a \cdot \operatorname{relu}(wx)$. For any positive constant $c > 0$, scaling $w \mapsto c w$ and $a \mapsto a/c$ produces the identical function $f(x)$. The set of weights computing the same function forms a continuous hyperbola $a \cdot w = \text{const}$.

When the target function is zero ($c = 0$), the hyperbola pinches into the coordinate axes $a = 0$ (dead unit) and $w = 0$ (zero weight). The zero-loss set is a pair of crossing lines—the exact crossing singularity from Part I!

Figure {fig:reluunit}. One ReLU unit $f(x) = a\cdot\operatorname{relu}(wx)$ learning $c\cdot\operatorname{relu}(x)$. Rescaling curves form zero-loss valleys. When target $c = 0$, the valley pinches into crossing lines $a = 0$ and $w = 0$.

15.2 Linear networks: why overparameterization plateaus

Now consider a deep linear network $y = B A x$ mapping $\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^H \to \mathbb{R}^3$, trained on data from a true rank-one linear map. As hidden width $H$ grows from 1 to 5, the total number of weights scales from 6 to 30 ($d = 6H$). Classical theory predicts that effective capacity grows linearly: $d/2 = 3H \in \{3, 6, 9, 12, 15\}$.

Instead, internal matrix multiplication has a continuous gauge symmetry: for any invertible $G \in \operatorname{GL}(H)$, $(B G, G^{-1} A)$ computes the identical map. By resolving determinantal singularities, Aoyagi and Watanabe proved that the true learning coefficient $\lambda$ plateaus sharply at $\lambda = 4.5 = MN/2$, completely decoupled from the growing parameter count $d$!

15.3 Tensor factorizations and normal crossings

When multiple rank-one components collide, pulling back along polar coordinates monomializes the loss into intersecting normal-crossing divisors $\theta = 0$ and $u = 0$. The evidence scales with an extra logarithmic factor $N^{-3/2}\log N$, generated by the double pole ($m = 2$) of the zeta function.

16. Measuring it in practice: The LLC Thermometer

Explicit resolution charts are impossible to write down for deep neural networks with millions of weights. How can researchers measure $\lambda$ in practice? Sumio Watanabe and modern SLT researchers designed an empirical statistical thermometer based on Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD).

The centered partition function

Around an empirical minimizer $w^*$, define the centered excess loss $K_n(w) = L_n(w) - L_n(w^*) \ge 0$. Adding a quadratic localization spring $\frac{\gamma}{2}\|w - w^*\|^2$, the centered partition function is:

Z_c(\beta) = \int_B \exp\!\left(-n\beta K_n(w) - \frac{\gamma}{2}\|w - w^*\|^2\right) dw.

By Hironaka's theorem, resolving the singularities of $K_n(w)$ monomializes the loss, giving the asymptotic expansion $Z_c(\beta) \asymp (n\beta)^{-\lambda} (\log(n\beta))^{m-1}$. Differentiating the free energy with respect to inverse temperature $\beta$ yields an exact thermal identity:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\big[-\log Z_c(\beta)\big] = n\mathbb{E}_\beta\big[K_n(w)\big] \approx \frac{\lambda}{\beta} - \frac{m-1}{\beta\log(n\beta)}.

Multiplying by $\beta$ extracts the local learning coefficient directly:

\hat{\lambda}(w^*) = n\beta \left(\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S L_n(w_s) - L_n(w^*)\right).

Temperature selection and the WBIC plateau

What temperature should we choose? At high temperatures ($\beta \to 0$), the sampler explores arbitrary high-loss territory. At zero temperature ($\beta \to \infty$), it freezes at the point $w^*$. Watanabe proved that at the Widely Applicable Bayesian Information Criterion (WBIC) temperature $\beta^* = 1/\log n$, the thermal energy enters a wide plateau of height $\lambda$.

17. Why Hironaka? Lean 4, Developmental Interpretability, and Reflection

Hironaka’s universal guarantee. Why did Sumio Watanabe turn to Heisuke Hironaka's 1964 Fields Medal theorem? Because Hironaka proved that for any real-analytic model and any empirical dataset, there exists a sequence of blowups along smooth centers that monomializes both the loss and the volume form simultaneously:

K(\varphi(u)) = u_1^{2k_1} \cdots u_d^{2k_d}, \qquad |J_\varphi(u)| = u_1^{h_1-1} \cdots u_d^{h_d-1}.

In these blown-up coordinates, the tangled high-dimensional Bayesian integral factors into elementary Gamma functions! The leading pole defines the local learning coefficient $\lambda = \min_i (h_i / 2k_i)$ and its multiplicity $m$. Watanabe’s Singular Learning Theory proves that $\lambda$, not raw parameter count $d$, governs generalization:

  • Free energy (Bayesian evidence): $F(n) = -\log Z(n) \approx n L_n(w^*) + \lambda \log n - (m-1)\log\log n$.
  • Generalization error: $G(n) = \mathbb{E}[L(w)] - L_n(w^*) \approx \frac{\lambda}{n}$.

The 2026 milestone: formalizing Hironaka in Lean. Hironaka’s original proof was historically one of the most formidable in 20th-century mathematics—spanning over 200 pages of intricate local induction. In October 2026, researchers at Resolution formalized Hironaka’s theorem in Lean, providing the first machine-checked proof of the algebraic foundation underlying SLT.

For AI safety researchers, $\lambda$ provides an indispensable tool for developmental interpretability. By monitoring $\lambda$ during training, researchers can detect discrete phase transitions—the exact moments when a network drops into a more structured algorithmic valley, forming induction heads or learning modular algorithms.

A graphics researcher’s reflection. When I began this exploration, algebraic geometry felt completely foreign to my background in physics simulation, computer graphics, and numerical optimization. Yet across all these domains, the central lesson is identical. Whether simulating three billiard balls colliding at the same millisecond, unpinching a 3D cone into a smooth cylinder, or explaining why a neural network generalizes from finite data, classical calculus breaks down for the exact same reason: it assumes the world is locally quadratic, and nature produces singularities. And in every case, the solution is not to smooth over the breakdown with arbitrary penalties. The solution is a blowup: excavate the singularity, and keep track of incoming directions.

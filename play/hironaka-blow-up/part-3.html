9. Rigid-body collisions: the opening demo, unpacked

The three balls from the opening page provide our first mechanical blowup. In an elastic collision between two balls of masses $m_1, m_2$, conservation of momentum and kinetic energy:

m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2', \qquad m_1 v_1^2 + m_2 v_2^2 = m_1 v_1'^2 + m_2 v_2'^2

uniquely determines the outgoing velocities. But when three balls touch simultaneously, there are three unknown velocities and only two conservation laws: rigid-body mechanics alone cannot pick an outcome.

Configuration space in a wedge: Write the gaps between neighbors as $g_1 = x_2 - x_1 - 2r \ge 0$ and $g_2 = x_3 - x_2 - 2r \ge 0$. In mass-weighted coordinates $q_i = \sqrt{m_i} x_i$, kinetic energy is standard Euclidean $\frac{1}{2}|\dot{q}|^2$. Removing uniform translation reduces the three-ball system to a single point bouncing inside a planar wedge between the constraint walls $g_1 = 0$ and $g_2 = 0$, with wedge angle:

\cos\beta = \sqrt{\frac{m_1 m_3}{(m_1 + m_2)(m_2 + m_3)}}

A sequence of pairwise collisions is a billiard path bouncing between the walls. Simultaneous impact is the path striking the sharp corner $g_1 \cdot g_2 = 0$.

The blown-up bar: The color bar replaces the singular corner with all possible incoming directions—the microscopic gap ratio $g_2 / g_1$ immediately before impact. Every direction yields a deterministic sequence of bounces. The bar decomposes into chambers of shared outcomes. When masses are equal ($\beta = 60^\circ$), all incoming directions yield the exact same outcome, explaining why real cradles are predictable. With a light middle ball, different directions produce completely different orders of departure.

Inelastic collapse: When collisions have restitution $e < 1$, energy is dissipated. Below the critical threshold $e = 7 - 4\sqrt{3} \approx 0.072$ (McNamara & Young), three balls bounce infinitely many times in a finite time window! A standard pairwise impulse simulator traps itself in an infinite loop. Vouga, Smith, Kaufman, Tamstorf, and Grinspun's All's Well That Ends Well guarantees termination by detecting implied velocity equalities and blending elastic and inelastic limits.

10. Tensor factorization: a fit that never finishes

Key terms: rank-one tensor, border rank, Veronese variety, tangent limit.

A $2 \times 2 \times 2$ tensor $T$ consists of 8 entries $T_{ijk}$. A rank-one tensor is a product of three 2-vectors: $(a \otimes b \otimes c)_{ijk} = a_i b_j c_k$. Fitting a target $W$ with two rank-one components minimizes:

\text{minimize}\;\; E = \sum_{ijk}\big(a_{1i}b_{1j}c_{1k} + a_{2i}b_{2j}c_{2k} - W_{ijk}\big)^2

Consider the target $W$ with $W_{001} = W_{010} = W_{100} = 1$ and all other entries 0. When gradient descent attempts to fit $W$, the two rank-one pieces diverge to $\pm \infty$ while nearly canceling, and the error creeps asymptotically toward zero without ever arriving!

The geometry of border rank: Differentiating the rank-one tensor $(e_0 + h e_1)^{\otimes 3}$ with respect to $h$ at $h = 0$ yields $W$. Thus $W$ is the limit of a finite difference:

W = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{(e_0+h\,e_1)^{\otimes 3} - e_0^{\otimes 3}}{h}

Rank-one tensors live on the Veronese variety. A sum of two rank-one tensors lives on a secant line. As the two points merge while their coefficients diverge like $\pm 1/h$, the secant tilts into a tangent vector:

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{(v+\varepsilon w)^{\otimes 3} - v^{\otimes 3}}{\varepsilon} = 3\,\operatorname{Sym}\big(v^{\otimes 2}\otimes w\big)

Target $W$ lies on this tangential boundary. Ordinary gradient descent chases an unreachable point at infinity.

Hironaka's fix: blow up the step $h$. By treating the step $h$ as a parameter, the model becomes:

T(a,b,c,a',b',c',h) = \frac{(a+h\,a')\otimes(b+h\,b')\otimes(c+h\,c') - a\otimes b\otimes c}{h}

Expanding the product cancels the leading term, leaving a polynomial that is smooth at $h = 0$. The blowup adds the boundary point in bounded coordinates, allowing the fit to converge cleanly. In Part IV, we will see how this boundary generates logarithmic corrections in Bayesian learning.

11. Symmetric frame fields: where should the singularities go?

Key terms: cross field, representation vector, winding number, Ginzburg–Landau energy, Burer–Monteiro lift.

To generate quadrilateral meshes, geometry engines compute cross fields: four orthogonal directions at each point. Representing a cross by $u = e^{4i\theta}$ on the unit circle $|u|^2 = 1$ removes the quarter-turn ambiguity. On a disk, boundary alignment forces $u$ to have a total winding number of 4. By the Poincaré–Hopf index theorem, $u$ cannot remain continuous inside: it must contain singularities.

Near a singular point of winding $d$, the Dirichlet energy $E(u) = \frac{1}{2}\int |\nabla u|^2$ diverges logarithmically: each halving of radius costs $\pi d^2 \log 2$, producing infinite energy. Three strategies resolve this:

  • Ginzburg–Landau: Penalize departure from the unit circle: $E_\varepsilon(u) = \frac{1}{2}\int |\nabla u|^2 + \frac{1}{4\varepsilon^2}\int (1-|u|^2)^2$. Inside cores of radius $\varepsilon$, $|u|$ drops to 0. A core of winding $d$ costs $\pi d^2 \log(1/\varepsilon)$. Because $4^2 = 16 > 4 \times 1^2 = 4$, energy forces a 4-fold core to split into four 1-fold cores!
  • Renormalized energy: Once split, the four cores repel like 2D charges while the boundary pushes them back, settling into a stable O-grid at $r \approx 0.85$.
  • Burer–Monteiro lift: Lift $u$ to a sphere in $\mathbb{R}^k$ ($k \ge 3$). Higher spheres have no hole for the boundary to wrap around, so the lifted field is completely smooth with finite energy $8\pi$, projecting back to a single 4-fold core.

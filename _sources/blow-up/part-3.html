When two components become one

Crossing from physical space into parameter space: how model redundancy cancels first-order derivatives and blinds ordinary curvature.

9. Why redundant components change learning

In computer graphics, physics simulation, and classical geometry, singularities live in physical space: pencil strokes crossing on paper, billiard balls colliding in a corner, or the sharp tip of a cone. In each case, blowing up replaced the singular geometric point with incoming directions.

In machine learning and optimization, however, the geometry lives in parameter space. What produces a singularity in parameter space? Not an external physical boundary, but internal model redundancy.

Whenever an overparameterized model has more internal capacity than the data strictly requires, different parameter configurations compute the exact same function. In neural networks, neurons in a hidden layer can swap roles or merge together. In low-rank matrix and tensor factorizations, factors can collapse into identical modes. When two components in a model collide, something extraordinary happens to the loss landscape: ordinary curvature goes blind.

Here is the simplest, most explicit setting where this occurs. Our “data” consist of eight numbers: a $2\times2\times2$ symmetric tensor. The target tensor has entry $T^*_{000}=1$ and every other entry zero. We approximate it by summing two rank-one components, each defined by the vector $v(t)=(1,t)$ with tensor product $(v(t)^{\otimes3})_{ijk}=v_i(t)v_j(t)v_k(t)$:

T(a,b)=\tfrac12 v(a)^{\otimes3}+\tfrac12 v(b)^{\otimes3}.

The adjustable parameters are $a$ and $b$. At $(a,b)=(0,0)$, both components equal the target: the fit is exact ($K=0$). The loss $K$ is the sum of eight squared prediction errors, scaled by $1/3$ for convenience.

To see why pulling apart produces so little error, change coordinates to the mean $s=(a+b)/2$ and separation $d=(a-b)/2$. Moving together varies $s$; pulling apart varies $d$. Expanding the tensor entries reveals a fundamental asymmetry:

  • Entries with one index equal to 1: $\tfrac{1}{2}(a+b) = s$.
  • Entries with two indices equal to 1: $\tfrac{1}{2}(a^2+b^2) = s^2+d^2$.
  • The entry with three indices equal to 1: $\tfrac{1}{2}(a^3+b^3) = s^3+3sd^2$.

Look at what happens along the separation direction ($s=0$): the linear terms $+d$ and $-d$ cancel out to first order identically! The prediction shifts only at second order ($d^2$). And because loss is squared prediction error, the resulting loss grows only as $(d^2)^2 = d^4$!

Along the mean direction ($d=0$), error grows as $s^2$, so halving a displacement reduces loss by $2^2 = 4$. Along the separation direction, error grows as $d^4$, so halving a displacement reduces loss by $2^4 = 16$.

The exact loss and local scaling
K(s,d)=s^2+(s^2+d^2)^2+\tfrac13(s^3+3sd^2)^2.

Near the origin, this loss is tightly bounded above and below by positive constants times the simpler polynomial:

K_{\mathrm{local}}(s, d) \asymp s^2 + d^4.

Swapping the two components reflects $d \mapsto -d$. The flat direction is not an artifact of labeling; it arises because symmetric component summation annihilates odd-order derivatives at the collision point.

Why ordinary curvature goes blind

In classical statistics and machine learning, almost every standard tool—the Laplace approximation, Fisher information matrices, AIC, and BIC—approximates local minima by a quadratic Taylor bowl:

K(w) \approx K(w^*) + \tfrac{1}{2}(w - w^*)^T H (w - w^*), \qquad H = \nabla^2 K(w^*).

At our tensor collision, the gradient is zero. But look at the Hessian matrix evaluated at $(s, d) = (0, 0)$:

H = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial s^2} & \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial s \partial d} \\ \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial d \partial s} & \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial d^2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.

The second derivative along the separation axis is zero. The Hessian is degenerate. To quadratic order, classical calculus sees a flat valley and asserts that $d$ is an unidentifiable parameter with zero curvature. It is completely blind to the fact that loss grows as $d^4$.

This fourth-order valley is a cusp-like singularity in the loss landscape. It occurs whenever neural network units, mixture components, or tensor factors collide. And it raises a pivotal question: if a minimum is surrounded by a quartic valley rather than a quadratic bowl, how much extra parameter volume does it have—and how does a learning algorithm respond?

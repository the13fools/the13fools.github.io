What is… a blowup? · Part II of IV

A cone, its directions, and the limits of Nash blowups

Tracking directions above a singular apex, the exceptional circle, and where choice-free tangent blowups encounter higher-dimensional barriers.

At the apex of a cone, infinitely many distinct lines collide into a single pinch point. A blowup does not smooth out the pinch by rounding off corners; it untangles the collision by recording incoming directions. On the real quadric cone $X^2 + Y^2 = Z^2$, this algebraic surgery is completely explicit: its strict transform becomes a smooth cylinder, and an entire circle of directions sits above the old apex.

This resolves the local singularity. But it raises a deeper foundational question: can this process be made completely automatic and choice-free? John Nash conjectured that repeatedly attaching tangent spaces would resolve every algebraic variety. Ordinary Nash iteration resolves curves, and normalized Nash iteration resolves surfaces. These are different statements; the ordinary surface case remains open in general.

6. Hypersurfaces vs. varieties: keeping dimensions straight

Key terms: ambient dimension, hypersurface, intrinsic dimension, regular locus.

Before exploring higher-dimensional singularities, it is vital to keep two concepts distinct: the ambient dimension (the number of coordinates in the ambient Euclidean space) and the intrinsic dimension of the geometric variety.

A single polynomial equation $F(x,y,z,w)=0$ in four ambient coordinates defines a three-dimensional hypersurface. Wherever the gradient $\nabla F \neq 0$, the implicit function theorem guarantees a smooth tangent 3-plane. At the origin of the 4D conifold $x^2+y^2-z^2-w^2=0$, all first derivatives vanish, producing an isolated singular point. Taking a 3D spatial slice at $w=0$ yields the 2-dimensional cone $X^2+Y^2-Z^2=0$ shown in Figure {fig:manifold3d}.

When algebraic geometers speak of the recent Nash blowup breakthroughs in dimension 4 and dimension 3, they refer to the intrinsic dimension of the varieties themselves—which are not simple hypersurfaces, but higher-codimension varieties requiring ambient embeddings in $\mathbb{C}^7$ or $\mathbb{C}^6$.

7. Replacing the apex by directions

Key terms: direction chart, total transform, strict transform, exceptional locus.

To blow up the origin of real three-space $\mathbb{R}^3$, we introduce projective direction coordinates. In the affine chart where the $Z$-direction is nonzero, we make the substitution:

X = uv, \qquad Y = uw, \qquad Z = u.

Away from $u = 0$, the new coordinates simply record the slopes: $v = X/Z$ and $w = Y/Z$. The coordinate $u$ acts as a signed scaling factor along each line. Substituting into the cone equation gives the total transform:

X^2 + Y^2 - Z^2 = u^2(v^2 + w^2 - 1) = 0.

The total transform factors into two geometric pieces: the exceptional divisor $u^2 = 0$ (the fiber sitting over the origin) and the strict transform:

v^2 + w^2 = 1, \qquad u \in \mathbb{R}.

In $(v,w,u)$ space, this is a standard smooth circular cylinder! Its gradient $\nabla(v^2+w^2-1) = (2v, 2w, 0)$ never vanishes on the surface, meaning standard calculus is completely restored.

A natural parametrization of this cylinder is given by:

(v, w, u) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, u) \quad\longmapsto\quad (X, Y, Z) = (u\cos\theta, u\sin\theta, u).

At $u = 0$, every direction $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ maps down to $(0,0,0)$. The singular apex has been excavated and replaced by an entire circle of directions. Away from $u=0$, the blowdown map is a real-analytic diffeomorphism onto the cone.

Which exceptional divisor? In the ambient $\mathbb{R}^3$ blowup, the fiber over the origin is $\mathbb{RP}^2$ (the space of all lines through the origin). Restricting to the cone, the exceptional locus is the projective conic $[X:Y:Z]$ satisfying $X^2+Y^2=Z^2$, whose real points form a circle. Because every real line on this cone has $Z \neq 0$, this single chart covers the entire real exceptional circle.

8. What the Nash counterexamples prove

Key terms: Gauss graph, Nash blowup, normalization, recurring affine chart.

In 1968, John Nash proposed a canonical, choice-free alternative to Hironaka's algorithm. Instead of manually selecting centers of blowup, the Nash blowup assigns to each smooth point $p \in X_{\mathrm{smooth}}$ its tangent $d$-plane $T_p X$ in the Grassmannian $\operatorname{Gr}(d, N)$, and takes the closure of the resulting graph:

\operatorname{Nash}(X) = \overline{\big\{(p, T_p X) : p \in X_{\mathrm{smooth}}\big\}} \subset X \times \operatorname{Gr}(d, N).

The normalized Nash blowup additionally takes the integral closure (normalization) of the coordinate ring at each step. On curves (1D), a single Nash blowup resolves ordinary cusps immediately. On normal algebraic surfaces (2D), Mark Spivakovsky (1990) proved that normalized Nash blowups always terminate in a finite number of steps.

The Higher-Dimensional Breakthrough: In a milestone paper, Federico Castillo, Daniel Duarte, Maximiliano Leyton-Álvarez, and Alvaro Liendo (arXiv:2409.19767, Annals of Mathematics 2026) proved that Nash blowups fail in dimension 4 and higher. They construct normal singular affine toric varieties where an affine open chart $U$ of the Nash blowup is algebraically isomorphic to the starting variety:

U \subset \operatorname{Nash}(X), \qquad U \cong X.

Because Nash's process is completely canonical and deterministic, it is trapped: it repeats the exact same sequence of modifications indefinitely, never resolving the singularity. In a companion work (arXiv:2511.01772), the authors also established that non-normalized Nash blowups fail in dimension 3.

A zero-set view of the actual four-dimensional example

The four-dimensional Castillo et al. counterexample lives in seven ambient coordinates $(x_1, \dots, x_7)$, so its Gauss map takes values in $\operatorname{Gr}(4, 7)$. To visualize its geometry, take a real spatial section through its defining toric relations: fix $x_5 = 1$ and $x_2 = q$, using $(p, t, r) = (x_1, x_6, x_4)$ as coordinates. The resulting zero set is:

r \, t^2 = q^3.

For $q \neq 0$, the section is a smooth surface. But at $q = 0$, the equation collapses to $r t^2 = 0$, which is the union of two perpendicular planes: $r = 0$ and $t = 0$. Their intersection line lies directly inside the fourfold's singular locus.

The three-dimensional counterexample: see the singularity first

Three-dimensional does not mean a surface in ordinary 3D space. Here $X$ has three independent parameters but lives in six ambient coordinates. We can draw a two-dimensional section of its real points. The later lattice diagrams encode its coordinate algebra; they are a different kind of picture.

Fix $x_4=1$ and project to $(A,B,C)=(x_1,x_2,x_6)$. Direct substitution into the defining equations gives this exact real zero set:

A^6=B^3C^2.

Most of it is parametrized by $(A,B,C)=(su,s^2,u^3)$. Include the negative $B$-axis too: it consists of real solutions that this parametrization misses. For a point of $A^6=B^3C^2$, put $u=\sqrt[3]{C}$. The recovered point is $(x_1,\ldots,x_6)=(A,B,Bu,1,Au,C)$. All five equations vanish:

x_5^2=x_3x_6,\quad x_1x_5=x_2x_6,\quad x_1x_3=x_2x_5,
x_1^2x_2x_4=x_3^2,\qquad x_1^3x_4=x_3x_5.

For $B>0$, take $s=\pm\sqrt B$: the two branches are $C=\pm A^3/B^{3/2}$. For $B<0$, nonnegativity of $A^6$ forces $A=C=0$. This checks the extra negative axis. The real projection is not a replacement for the complex variety.

Now follow one chart through two Nash blowups

The result of Castillo, Duarte, Leyton-Álvarez, and Liendo concerns the ordinary, non-normalized Nash blowup. A selected open chart after two iterations is isomorphic to the starting singular threefold. It does not say that the entire blowup is unchanged, or settle normalized Nash resolution in dimension three.

At $(x_1,\ldots,x_6)=0$, all five defining equations have zero linear part. The tangent space there has dimension 6, while $X$ has dimension 3: this fixed point is singular. We will see a point of this same local type return after two selected Nash charts.

The three-dimensional construction begins with an affine semigroup $S \subset \mathbb{Z}^3$ generated by six vectors $h_i$, the columns of the matrix:

B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & -2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -1 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.

Each column vector represents the exponent of a monomial. The associated 3-fold variety $X(S)$ is the closure of the image of:

\phi: (\mathbf{k}^*)^3 \longrightarrow \mathbf{k}^6, \qquad (a, b, c) \longmapsto \left( a, b, c, \frac{c^2}{a^2 b}, \frac{ac}{b}, \frac{a^2 c}{b^2} \right).

How tangent planes enter this calculation. On the dense torus, the tangent-plane coordinates are Jacobian minors. Dividing by a chosen nonzero minor gives an affine Nash chart. Ratios of monomials subtract their exponent vectors: for example $x_5/x_1=\chi^{h_5-h_1}$. That is why the new lattice generators are differences, rather than arbitrarily chosen arrows.

Why there is a singularity to begin with: The lattice point $u = (0, -1, 1)$ is absent from the semigroup ($u \notin S$), yet its third multiple $3u = h_4 + h_6 = (0, -3, 3)$ is present in $S$! Using the grading functional $L(x,y,z) = x + 2y + 3z$, all generators have positive integer grades, and only $h_1$ and $h_6$ have grade 1 (neither of which equals $u$). Because $S$ has missing lattice points, the semigroup is not saturated: its coordinate ring is not integrally closed, and the variety is singular.

Why two Nash blowups cannot escape: The first Nash chart introduces eight generators $g_i$. A subsequent Nash blowup produces a chart with six generators $t_i$. Their relation to the original six generators $h_i$ is an exact integer linear transformation:

B_2 = U_3 B, \qquad U_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 & 2 \\ -2 & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det U_3 = 1.

Because $U_3 \in \operatorname{GL}(3, \mathbb{Z})$ is an unimodular integer matrix, it is an isomorphism of the integer lattice $\mathbb{Z}^3$. It identifies the full semigroup $T = U_3 S$ with $S$, proving that the resulting affine chart is algebraically isomorphic to the starting variety: $X(T) \cong X(S)$. Iterating Nash blowups simply reproduces this exact singular chart indefinitely.

An explicit resolution that does terminate

The Nash rule fails on this example, but resolution is still possible. Here is a computed toric resolution of this same threefold, using normalization and a regular fan refinement. It gives all four smooth charts and their maps to the original six coordinates. This is a resolution of the variety; it is not presented as the canonical sequence of ambient weighted centers produced by the general Abramovich–Temkin–Włodarczyk algorithm.

Now work in the dual lattice $N$, where a vector $n$ records the vanishing order $\langle h_i,n\rangle$ of the coordinate monomial $x_i$. Solving the six inequalities $\langle h_i,n\rangle\ge0$ gives the four primitive rays:

n_1=(0,0,1),\quad n_2=(0,1,2),\quad n_3=(1,0,1),\quad n_4=(1,2,2).

The polygon below is a flat cross-section of this cone, not the physical variety. First normalize $S$ to $\operatorname{cone}(S)\cap\mathbb Z^3$. Then triangulate along $\langle n_1,n_4\rangle$. One cone has determinant 1 in absolute value; the other has determinant 2. The latter contains the primitive lattice vector:

n_5=(1,1,2)=\tfrac12(n_1+n_3+n_4).

Subdivide that cone around $n_5$. The final cones are $124$, $135$, $145$, and $345$ (indices of the $n_i$); their determinants are $-1,+1,-1,+1$. Thus every chart is a smooth $\mathbb A^3$. This is the regular-fan resolution criterion, applied here with explicit integer arithmetic.

Compute the normalization, not just its name

On the original dense torus, adjoin $U=c/b$ and $V=c/a$, with exponents $u=(0,-1,1)$ and $v=(-1,0,1)$. Both are integral: $U^3=x_4x_6$ and $V^2=x_2x_4$. The saturated semigroup has generators $h_1,h_2,h_4,h_6,u,v$. In particular $x_3=x_2U$ and $x_5=x_1U$, so the original ring sits inside this six-generator normal ring.

To check completeness, split the exponent cone into $\langle h_1,h_2,h_4\rangle$ and $\langle h_1,h_4,h_6\rangle$. Their determinants are 2 and 3. The only nonzero lattice points in their half-open fundamental parallelepipeds are $v$, $u$, and $2u$. Every lattice point in either cone is one of these representatives plus a nonnegative integer sum of its ray generators. Thus adjoining $U,V$ generates the whole saturation.

Why these charts give a resolution

For a final cone with basis vectors $(n_i,n_j,n_k)$ and coordinates $(r,s,t)$, set $x_\ell=r^{\langle h_\ell,n_i\rangle}s^{\langle h_\ell,n_j\rangle}t^{\langle h_\ell,n_k\rangle}$. All exponents are nonnegative integers. Substitution satisfies the five original equations identically. Determinant $\pm1$ makes the torus map invertible with integer monomial exponents. The cones cover the original cone, with unchanged boundary faces, so the charts glue to a proper birational morphism from a smooth variety, unchanged over the original smooth locus.

In chart D, the map is particularly easy to inspect:

(x_1,x_2,x_3)=(rst,\ s^2t,\ rs^2t^2),
(x_4,x_5,x_6)=(t,\ r^2st^2,\ r^3t^2).

The new ray $n_5$ gives the scaling exponents $(1,1,2,1,2,2)$ of the six coordinates as $t\to0$. At $t=0$, the entire smooth coordinate plane $(r,s,0)$ maps to the old singular origin. Here the exceptional divisor is a surface, because the resolved space is three-dimensional. In a surface blowup, the analogous divisor is a curve.

The four charts describe a complex algebraic resolution. The earlier section's extra negative B-axis has no real lift to the normalization: V² = x₂x₄ = B < 0 there. Those points lift over ℂ, through non-real values of V. This is another reason a real surface picture cannot display the whole complex resolution.

How this relates to the ATW weighted algorithm

Abramovich, Temkin, and Włodarczyk construct a functorial embedded resolution using stack-theoretic weighted blowups. Their centers come from recursively computed coefficient ideals and maximal contact, not just the exponents of one polynomial in arbitrary coordinates. Smooth stack charts can have finite quotient identifications; passing to a smooth scheme requires further work such as destackification.

The calculation above instead exploits this example's toric presentation. It supplies a complete smooth model and its maps explicitly, but does not compute or assert the ATW center sequence in the six-dimensional ambient space. Nor does it assert termination of normalized Nash iteration: our subdivisions were chosen by the toric resolution procedure.

Why this proves failure. Nash blowup restricts to open subsets and respects isomorphisms. Once an open chart of the starting singular type returns after two steps, the same calculation applies inside that chart again. Thus every even iterate contains a singular open subset. The ordinary Nash iteration cannot become smooth. Hironaka's theorem uses a different, controlled sequence of smooth centers; it is not contradicted.

The Bridge: From Cones to Simulation and Parameter Space. Up to now, our singularities have lived in idealized geometric spaces: crossing pencil strokes, colliding billiard balls, or the pinched tip of a 3D cone. Hironaka's theorem guarantees that choosing permissible smooth centers resolves these pinches into smooth manifolds, while Nash's intrinsic lift can get stuck.

Next, we bring this machinery into problems I know from physics simulation and geometry processing: simultaneous rigid-body collisions, diverging border-rank tensor fitting, and symmetric frame fields. In each domain, classical formulas break down—and blowing up provides the clean, principled resolution.

What is… a blowup? · Part IV of IV

How much room does a good fit have?

Two experiments with the same two losses: count nearly correct parameters, then add up their Bayesian weight.

The tensor collision gave us a loss that is quadratic in the mean $s$ and quartic in the separation $d$. We now use its simpler local model $K_{\rm collision}=s^2+d^4$, alongside an ordinary quadratic loss $K_{\rm regular}=s^2+d^2$. Both have two parameters and the same perfect fit at $(0,0)$.

The simpler loss preserves the collision’s local scaling, but is not its exact tensor loss. All the pictures use the same parameter square $-1\le s,d\le1$. Nothing is being trained here: the losses are given explicitly so we can see what a statistical calculation counts.

10. Count the nearly correct parameters

Allow a little error: $K\le\varepsilon$. Every shaded point below is a parameter setting meeting that requirement. The regular fit gives a disk. The collision fit leaves a longer band in the separation direction, because fourth powers stay small longer than squares.

Figure {fig:learningroom}. Press Demand 10× less error. Both regions shrink, but the collision region shrinks less. Read the fraction of the same parameter square that remains acceptable. The axes and shading scales are identical.

Regular: s² + d²

Collision: s² + d⁴

The learning coefficient is a shrinking rate

For the disk, each direction has width proportional to $\varepsilon^{1/2}$. Multiply the widths: area is proportional to $\varepsilon^1$. For the collision, the mean still has width $\varepsilon^{1/2}$, but separation has width $\varepsilon^{1/4}$. Its area is proportional to $\varepsilon^{3/4}$.

\lambda_{\rm regular}=\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1,\qquad \lambda_{\rm collision}=\tfrac12+\tfrac14=\tfrac34.

This exponent is the local learning coefficient, also called the real log canonical threshold of the loss. Smaller $\lambda$ means that demanding a tighter fit removes parameter volume more slowly. In more general analytic models there can also be a logarithmic factor; neither of these two examples needs one.

11. Add up the weight, not just the best fit

For independent Gaussian measurements, multiplying likelihoods gives an exponential of a summed squared error. We isolate that concentration effect in a simple model: a parameter setting gets relative weight $e^{-nK}$. Here $n$ controls how severely errors are penalized, standing in for the amount of data. We compare two candidate models with equal prior probabilities, each with a uniform parameter prior on the same square. Their minimum losses agree, so the distinction comes entirely from the surrounding parameters.

The evidence $Z$ is the average of $e^{-nK}$ over that prior square. The posterior probability of each candidate is its evidence divided by the sum of both evidences. This is a controlled comparison of the two specified losses, rather than a simulation of a training dataset.

Figure {fig:learningevidence}. Select n = 100, then n = 10,000. Bright means high relative likelihood, with the same 0–1 color scale in both panels. Both fits concentrate, but the collision keeps a wider bright region. The bar adds up that brightness.

At large $n$, the regular evidence scales as $n^{-1}$ and the collision evidence as $n^{-3/4}$. Their ratio grows like $n^{1/4}$. This is why replacing both fits by Gaussian bowls gives the wrong answer. In regular models the coefficient is half the parameter count; singular learning theory replaces that count with a geometric exponent. See Watanabe’s account of the Bayesian information criterion.

These conclusions depend on the loss and the prior. They do not establish that gradient descent prefers this fit, or that a lower coefficient always means better predictions on new data.

12. Where the blowup enters

We could compute the exponent here because the two terms already separate. A blowup provides a systematic way to expose scaling when they do not. In the separation-dominated sector, use the weighted chart $s=r^2v$, $d=r$: mean is given weight 2 and separation weight 1.

s^2+d^4=r^4(v^2+1),\qquad |ds\,dd|=|r|^2\,|dr\,dv|.

The Jacobian matters as much as the transformed loss. On a bounded $v$-sector, substitute $r=n^{-1/4}\rho$: the volume factor $|r|^2dr$ contributes $n^{-3/4}$. The exceptional set $r=0$ records approach directions that all map to the original minimum. Other charts cover the remaining directions; this one chart is not a complete global resolution.

Check the full evidence without choosing charts

For these separable toy losses, define $I_p(n)=\int_{-1}^1 e^{-nx^p}\,dx$. Then $Z_{\rm regular}=I_2^2/4$ and $Z_{\rm collision}=I_2I_4/4$. The factor $1/4$ is the uniform prior density. Rescaling each one-dimensional integral gives $I_2\sim\sqrt\pi\,n^{-1/2}$ and $I_4\sim\tfrac12\Gamma(1/4)n^{-1/4}$. This independently checks both exponents. General resolved integrals need not factor into independent one-dimensional integrals.

13. Closing the loop: why AI safety formalized Hironaka

In our two-parameter collision ($s^2 + d^4$), the loss was clean and separable, allowing us to find the weighted blowup chart $s = r^2 v, d = r$ by inspection. But in modern neural networks with millions of parameters, the loss landscape is an immense, non-separable algebraic variety:

K(w) = \frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^n \|f(x_i; w) - y_i\|^2.

Permutation symmetries, dead ReLUs, low-rank matrix factorizations ($W = BA$), and multi-head attention interactions create complex, intersecting singular strata. You cannot guess blowup charts by hand. And because the Hessian matrix drops rank everywhere along these singular subvarieties, classical Taylor approximations and Laplace expansions fail completely.

Hironaka’s universal guarantee. This is why Heisuke Hironaka’s 1964 Fields Medal theorem is the master key. Hironaka proved that for any real-analytic model and any empirical dataset, there exists a sequence of blowups along permissible smooth centers that monomializes both the loss and the volume form:

K(\varphi(u)) = u_1^{2k_1} \cdots u_d^{2k_d}, \qquad |J_\varphi(u)| = u_1^{h_1-1} \cdots u_d^{h_d-1}.

In these blown-up coordinates, the tangled high-dimensional Bayesian integral factors into elementary one-dimensional Gamma functions! The leading pole of the resulting zeta function defines the local learning coefficient $\lambda = \min_i (h_i / 2k_i)$ and its multiplicity $m$. Sumio Watanabe’s Singular Learning Theory (SLT) proves that $\lambda$, not raw parameter count $d$, dictates how neural networks generalize:

  • Free energy (Bayesian evidence): $F(n) = -\log Z(n) \approx n L_n(w^*) + \lambda \log n - (m-1)\log\log n$.
  • Generalization error: $G(n) = \mathbb{E}[L(w)] - L_n(w^*) \approx \frac{\lambda}{n}$.

Why overparameterization doesn't overfit. Classical learning theory (AIC, BIC, VC dimension) assumes parameters are regular, predicting that model capacity scales with parameter count ($d/2$). If a network has 10 million weights, classical theory expects 5 million degrees of freedom. In neural networks, however, adding redundant weights does not create new predictive dimensions—it simply expands the volume of singular fibers where $\lambda$ plateaus! (As demonstrated live in Appendix F.3, expanding the width of a linear network leaves $\lambda = 4.5$ capped). Bayesian posteriors and noisy optimizers like SGD are naturally drawn to these singular valleys by sheer geometric entropy.

The 2026 milestone: formalizing Hironaka in Lean. Hironaka’s proof was historically one of the most formidable in 20th-century mathematics—spanning over 200 pages of intricate local induction. In October 2026, researchers at Resolution formalized Hironaka’s theorem in Lean, providing the first machine-checked proof of the algebraic foundation underlying SLT.

For AI safety researchers, $\lambda$ is an indispensable tool for developmental interpretability. By estimating $\lambda$ across training (using the statistical thermometer in Appendix F.2), researchers can detect discrete phase transitions—the exact moments when a network drops into a more structured algorithmic valley, forming induction heads or learning modular reasoning.

A graphics researcher’s reflection. When I began this exploration, algebraic geometry felt completely foreign to my background in physics simulation, computer graphics, and numerical optimization. Yet across all these domains, the central lesson is identical. Whether simulating three billiard balls colliding at the same millisecond, unpinching a 3D cone into a smooth cylinder, or explaining why a neural network generalizes from finite data, classical calculus breaks down for the exact same reason: it assumes the world is locally quadratic, and nature produces singularities. And in every case, the solution is not to smooth over the breakdown with arbitrary penalties. The solution is a blowup: excavate the singularity, and keep track of incoming directions.

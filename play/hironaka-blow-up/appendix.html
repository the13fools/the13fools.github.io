Appendix A. More on collisions

Narrowing wedges: When the middle ball is very light, the configuration wedge narrows. A billiard ball entering a wedge of angle $\beta$ bounces roughly $180^\circ / \beta$ times before escaping. As $\beta \to 0$, the walls become parallel ($g_1 = x - a \ge 0$ and $g_2 = a - x \ge 0 \implies g_1 + g_2 = 0$), forming an implied equality where pairwise impulse solvers chatter indefinitely.

Figure A1. Narrowing wedge. As angle $\beta$ decreases, bounces multiply; at $0^\circ$, the walls touch.

Billiard path
Bounces against wedge angle

Three resolutions of simultaneous impact:

  • Penalty: Soft contact springs $F = k \delta^p$. Specifies compliant contact dynamics; the outcome depends on the force law and damping.
  • Constraints & Impulses: Solves a linear complementarity problem. When multiple contacts are active, some pairwise sequential solvers depend on contact ordering (e.g. Gauss–Seidel bubble-sorting velocities).
  • Blowup: Replaces the corner with the space of incoming gap ratios, retaining the limiting outcomes for the stated pairwise model. Probability shares require a specified prior on gap ratios.

Appendix B. More on tensors

B.1 Projective duality and parameter monomialization

Background on tensor varieties and discriminants: Landsberg[16] and Gelfand–Kapranov–Zelevinsky.[17]

For general $2 \times 2 \times 2$ tensors, the boundary $\Delta = 0$ is the projective dual of the rank-one variety. At a smooth point of the boundary, its tangent hyperplane identifies a contact point on the rank-one variety. Biduality describes this correspondence; it does not by itself prove that the Nash blowup resolves every singular stratum of the boundary in one step.

On the parameter side, fitting the zero tensor with a single rank-one term $K = |a|^2 |b|^2 |c|^2$ has zero set equal to the union of the three codimension-two factor subspaces ($a = 0$, $b = 0$, or $c = 0$). Here $a,b,c\in\mathbb R^2$. Polar coordinates $a = u_a \hat{a}, b = u_b \hat{b}, c = u_c \hat{c}$ yield the monomial loss $K = u_a^2 u_b^2 u_c^2$ and Jacobian $dw = u_a u_b u_c du d\theta$. Each radial variable gives candidate $(1+1)/2=1$. Because the three minimizing divisors meet, the result is $\lambda=1$, $m=3$, rather than the sum of the three candidates. Assume compact factor domains with a prior positive near the all-zero factors.

Appendix C. Thermodynamic integration and WBIC

Let $\varphi$ be a normalized prior and $K\ge0$ a centered loss. Write $Z_c(\beta)=\int e^{-n\beta K(w)}\varphi(w)\,dw$. Since $Z_c(0)=1$, differentiation gives the exact identity:

-\log Z_c(1)=\int_0^1\mathbb E_\beta[nK]\,d\beta=\int_{-\infty}^0\beta\mathbb E_\beta[nK]\,d\log\beta.

For a regular quadratic loss, the scaled quantity $\beta\mathbb E_\beta[nK]$ approaches $d/2$ in the concentrated regime. In a singular toy loss it approaches $\lambda$, with finite-concentration and possibly logarithmic corrections. The unscaled expectation behaves roughly as $\lambda/\beta$; it does not plateau at $\lambda$.

For statistical evidence, replace $K$ with average negative log likelihood $L_n$ and retain the baseline term $nL_n(w^*)$. Watanabe’s WBIC uses $\mathbb E_{\beta^*}[nL_n]$ at $\beta^*=1/\log n$ to approximate the leading free-energy asymptotics under its stated assumptions. It is a finite-sample approximation, not an exact quadrature identity. The main chapter’s temperature experiment displays the centered toy quantities separately.

D.4 Priors and component emptying

Collision and emptying are different ways for an overfitted mixture to represent a smaller model. Rousseau and Mengersen’s result concerns posterior behavior in specified mixture models under identifiability and prior assumptions: the Dirichlet weight exponent can favor emptying or merging. It does not give a universal tensor RLCT formula merely from a vector dimension.

The explicit four-variable normal-crossing toy in Part IV has a computed $n^{-3/2}\log n$ evidence scale with a uniform prior. Changing that prior near a divisor can change the power or logarithm. State the model, domain, and prior before applying such an exponent elsewhere.

For the resolution algorithm: Bierstone, Grigoriev, Milman, and Włodarczyk, especially §3, describes marked ideals and recursive center selection. Abramovich, Temkin, and Włodarczyk gives the later weighted stack-theoretic approach (2019 preprint; 2024 journal publication).

For the obstruction: read the threefold paper alongside Part II’s semigroup charts. The precise theorem concerns ordinary Nash blowups.

For the statistical payoff: Watanabe’s WBIC paper develops marginal-likelihood asymptotics; Lau and colleagues explain local coefficient estimation and its practical limitations.

How the figures are computed. Everything runs in your browser. Collisions are exact event-driven simulations; springs use small time steps. Cross fields solve the Ginzburg–Landau gradient flow on an 81 × 81 grid with $\varepsilon = 0.06$; the lifted field minimizes the same grid's smoothness energy over unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ by block-coordinate updates. Tensor fits are plain gradient descent. The cross field you drag (Figure 23) and its energy are closed forms; a single vortex (Figure 24) is a radial Newton solve. The main learning figures use explicit tensor entries, exact sublevel-area formulas, and numerical one-dimensional evidence integrals. Figures 29, 35, D1, D2 and D3 use numerical grid integration; Figures 33 and 34 run Langevin chains live. The hollow rings and tensor-layer points in Figure 31 are stored finite-chain illustrations; they are not additional exact coefficient formulas. The normal-crossing figure integrates its explicitly stated toy model. The cusp concentration curve uses numerical integration of sublevel areas, and its heatmap changes coordinate ranges at large concentration.

Nash, Hironaka, and when blowing up stops

Two ways to blow up

Part of a series: What is… a blowup? (the essay) · Resolving Newton's cradle · Blowups in physics

A blowup untangles a shape by giving every point one extra number: a direction. Color each point by that direction, then lift each point up by it. Wherever two branches pass through the same spot with different colors, the lift pulls them apart.

The two classic blowups differ only in which direction they record.

  • Nash records each point's own tangent direction. No choices are needed. This is the tangent lift in the Petrov–Nabizadeh–Dodik–Solomon paper.
  • Hironaka first picks a bad point, the center, and records the direction from the center to each point. The center itself gets every direction at once.

1. Lift a curve

How to read Figure 1. The curve starts flat, colored by the direction its blowup records (see the color key). Drag the lift slider and every point rises to a height set by its color. Then look at the bad point in the middle: if the branches there have different colors, they separate. If they have the same color, they stay stuck together.

Crossing. Two branches pass through the center with slopes +1 and −1, so they get different colors and lift to different heights. Both blowups separate them in one step.

Cusp. One branch, with a sharp tip. Its color changes smoothly through the tip, so the lifted curve is smooth: the tip unfolds into an ordinary bend. Both blowups do this in one step.

Touching. The curves y = x² and y = −x² meet with the same slope, so at the center they have the same color under either blowup. One lift leaves them stuck together, now crossing instead of touching. Touching, step 2 shows that crossing in the new coordinates, where a second lift separates it, exactly like the Crossing preset.

Compare the two modes. Hironaka's coloring belongs to the plane, not the curve: every point is colored by its direction from the center, whatever curve it lies on, and only the center itself is torn open into the violet stalk. Nash's coloring comes from the curve, so it changes every point and needs no center.

2. How each blowup is actually computed

"Lifting" is a picture. The computation is a change of coordinates. Both blowups add one new coordinate, a ratio, and then look at the curve in the new coordinates. Figure 2 does this twice for the touching curves y = ±x², both ways.

How to read Figure 2. Each row is one method; each panel is the curve after one more blowup, drawn in that step's coordinates. Under each panel is the number of derivatives the two branches still share at the center. The red ring marks the center of the next blowup.

The two recipes have the same shape. Each one blows up an ideal, meaning it adds as new coordinates the ratios of a few chosen functions:

  • Hironaka uses the functions that vanish on the center. For the origin those are x and y, so the new coordinate is y/x. The center is a smooth point or curve that you choose.
  • Nash uses the derivatives of the equation. For one equation f, the new coordinate is the ratio f_x / f_y. That choice is forced by the shape, and where those derivatives vanish together can itself be badly singular.

Everything else follows from that difference. Choosing the center lets Hironaka's version measure its progress, while Nash's version can only hope.

3. What Hironaka proved

Everything so far has been examples. Here is the theorem they illustrate, first in plain words, then precisely.

In plain words. Any shape cut out by polynomial equations can be made smooth by a finite number of blowups. Each blowup replaces a smooth piece of the bad set with the set of all directions of approaching it. Nothing changes away from the bad set, and every function on the shape becomes, near each point, a product of coordinates times something nonzero.

Hironaka's theorem (1964), over the real or complex numbers, or any field of characteristic zero

Let X be cut out by polynomial or analytic equations, and let f be a function on a smooth space M containing it. There is a finite sequence of blowups, each along a smooth center inside the current bad set, ending in a smooth space M̃ with a map π: M̃ → M such that:

  1. Nothing changes where things were already fine: π is one-to-one away from the bad set.
  2. The shape becomes smooth: the strict transform X̃ of X is smooth.
  3. Everything crosses cleanly: the new pieces π added (the exceptional set E) are smooth and cross each other and X̃ like coordinate planes ("normal crossings").
  4. Every function becomes a monomial: near every point of M̃ there are coordinates u₁, …, u_d with f(π(u)) = u₁^k₁ ⋯ u_d^k_d · (nonzero).

The theorem does not promise uniqueness, a minimal number of steps, or any bound on them. In positive characteristic it is known only in low dimensions. Later proofs (Villamayor, Bierstone–Milman, Włodarczyk, Kollár) make the choice of each center canonical, using an invariant that drops with every blowup; that is the algorithm stepped through in Figure 4.

Item 4 is what singular learning theory uses. Apply it to the error function K and the volume element: after π, near every point, K = u^(2k) and the volume is |u^h| du, so the volume of near-optimal parameters can be computed coordinate by coordinate, and the learning coefficient is λ = min (h + 1)/(2k).

Now checked by a computer. In 2026 Resolution released a Lean 4 formalization of this theorem and its refinements by Kollár, Włodarczyk and Bierstone–Milman, for algebraic schemes and for real- and complex-analytic spaces, with every theorem proved (the announcement describes how it was produced). The form singular learning theory needs is Atiyah's local monomialization with Bierstone and Milman's Jacobian clause, stated there as exists_proper_analytic_monomialization: for f real-analytic on an open set U ⊆ ℝⁿ and not identically zero near any point, there is a proper analytic map π: M → U, an isomorphism where f ≠ 0, such that near every point of M both f∘π and the Jacobian determinant of π are monomials times functions that don't vanish. That is item 4 together with the |u^h| volume factor above. The cusp of this page is one of the library's worked examples, used to illustrate one of Kollár's warnings about restricting blowup sequences.

What carries over exactly is the move: the rule "what happens next" is undefined at one point, so replace that point by the directions of approaching it, where the rule is defined. What makes the cradle work is a known fact about elastic collisions, that a finite system of hard balls only ever has finitely many collisions. What would replace Hironaka's invariant is the size of the largest cluster colliding at once, which the companion page's later blowups reduce until only pairs are left.

4. The Kollár–Włodarczyk algorithm, step by step

Hironaka proved that good centers always exist. Włodarczyk (2005) and Kollár (2007) turned the proof into a short recipe that picks the center automatically. For a curve in the plane, one loop looks like this:

  1. Order. At each point, find the lowest power in the Taylor expansion of f. Call the largest value m. The points where the order equals m are the worst ones.
  2. Maximal contact. Take m − 1 derivatives of f until one has a nonzero linear term. Its zero set H is a smooth curve through all the worst points. It is chosen so that it keeps containing the worst points after later blowups.
  3. Coefficient ideal. Restrict f and its derivatives to H, with weights that make them comparable: C = Σⱼ (∂ʲf |H)^(m!/(m−j)). This is a problem one dimension lower.
  4. Invariant. Record the pair (m, order of C ÷ m!). Solve the lower-dimensional problem the same way; for a curve that just means picking the points where C is worst.
  5. Blow up the center, divide out the exceptional curve, and start over. The invariant is now smaller in dictionary order, comparing m first.

Once the order is 1, the curve is smooth. A second phase, run by the same loop with the exceptional curves folded into the invariant, blows up until the curve and the exceptional curves cross cleanly ("normal crossings"). At that point f is a monomial times a unit near every point, which is exactly the form singular learning theory needs.

How to read Figure 4. Step through the algorithm on two curves. The picture is the current chart: the curve in orange, exceptional curves in violet, the maximal contact curve H dashed green, and the next center ringed in red. The card lists each step of the recipe. The table at the bottom tracks, for each exceptional curve, how many times f and the area form vanish on it, and the resulting candidate for the learning coefficient.

Three things to notice. First, the center is never a guess: it is wherever the invariant is largest. Second, the invariant strictly drops, (2, 2) → (2, 1) → done, or (2, 3/2) → smooth → clean crossings, so the loop has to stop. Third, the shared-derivatives count from Figure 2 is the second entry of the invariant for the touching curves. The general algorithm is that count, made to work in every dimension.

The hard part of the general theory is step 2. Many curves H have maximal contact, and the answer must not depend on which one you pick. Włodarczyk's contribution was a "homogenization" trick that makes all valid choices give the same coefficient ideal up to a change of coordinates. Kollár's book streamlines the same idea. That independence is what lets local recipes glue into one global sequence of blowups.

A newer version by Abramovich, Temkin, and Włodarczyk (and independently McQuillan) blows up with weights. It reads the invariant as exponents and scales coordinates to match. For the touching curves the invariant (2, 4) says substitute x = u, y = u²v, giving f = u⁴(v² − 1) in one step. For the cusp, (2, 3) says x = u², y = u³v, giving f = u⁶(v² − 1), also in one step. The price is that the result can have mild "orbifold" points, like the identification of (u, v) with (−u, −v) in the cusp case.

5. Why Nash's version can fail

Nash's blowup has no invariant. It applies the same operation every time and hopes the shape improves. On curves it always does, and with an extra normalization step it does on surfaces too (Spivakovsky, 1990). Castillo, Duarte, Leyton-Álvarez, and Liendo showed that in dimension four it can go around in a circle.

How to read Figure 5. Each row is a process, each column one more blowup. For the curves, the number is the shared-derivative count from Figure 2, and green means done. For the four-dimensional shape X, the cell says what the worst piece of the result looks like.

What does "X again" mean concretely? X is a four-dimensional shape inside seven-dimensional space, cut out by six equations in coordinates x₁ to x₇. Nash's blowup adds ratios as new coordinates, just as Figure 2 added the slope p. In one piece of Nash's blowup of X, the coordinates are seven such ratios y₁ to y₇. Here are the equations they satisfy, next to X's:

Where the copy comes from

X is a toric variety: each coordinate is a monomial, recorded by an integer vector, here h₁ to h₇ in four dimensions, and each equation records a relation among the vectors. For example, x₇² = x₂x₄x₅ records 2h₇ = h₂ + h₄ + h₅. For toric varieties, Nash's blowup can be computed exactly from these vectors. By my count it has 11 pieces: 4 smooth and 7 still singular.

The piece indexed by {h₁, h₂, h₃, h₅} is generated by the seven vectors h₆ − h₅, h₄ − h₂, h₄ − h₅, h₁, h₆ − h₃, h₄ − h₃, h₇ − h₂, which are the ratios above. An integer matrix with determinant 1 carries h₁ to h₇ onto these seven, one for one. Because the matrix is linear, every relation among the h's holds among the new vectors, which is why the equations match.

Multiplying X by ordinary space carries the example into every dimension above four. In dimensions two and three, whether plain Nash blowups always finish is still open.

6. Both blowups on noisy data

Petrov et al. apply Nash's idea to point clouds, estimating each point's tangent from its neighbors. Hironaka's idea also works on point clouds, in three steps:

  1. Find the center. Look for where the data stops looking like one smooth curve. Here: fit one line to each group of clean points and intersect them.
  2. Lift every point by its direction from the center.
  3. Repeat at any point where the lifted branches still meet, which is the data version of the invariant not yet being zero.

How to read Figure 7. Two noisy lines cross. The floor holds the data, colored by the direction each blowup records; above it, each point is lifted by that direction. A red outline means the method itself can tell its direction is unreliable: indeterminate. A dark outline means the point ended up next to the other line after lifting, which the method can't see.

Things to try. At the default settings, Nash's red points sit in a blob of about one neighborhood radius around the crossing, and Hironaka's in a much smaller disc. Lower the angle to 15°: Nash's neighborhoods now see two nearly parallel lines that look like one thick line, so it stops flagging and becomes confidently wrong. Then switch to "center found from data" at low angle and high noise: finding the center fails in exactly the regime where Nash fails, because it relies on the same local fits.

So Hironaka on data moves the difficulty rather than removing it. Once the center is known, the lift is nearly exact and only a tiny region is indeterminate. Finding the center is the hard, global step.

7. Three dimensions

In 3D the extra coordinate of a surface point is the line through its normal vector, ignoring which way the arrow points. Line directions in 3D form a projective plane, a closed surface that cannot sit inside ordinary 3D space without passing through itself. That matters as soon as you try to show a lift with color, because color is three numbers.

Demiralp, Hughes, and Laidlaw ran into exactly this when coloring fiber directions in brain scans. The standard coloring (|x|, |y|, |z|) folds the sphere of directions so that every color is shared by four different lines. Their fix maps directions through Boy's surface, a way to place the projective plane in 3D that is smooth but crosses itself along one curve. Colors are then continuous and locally distinct, and only directions near that curve share a color with some far-away direction.

For blowups this is a picture of a real limit. The Nash lift of a surface lives in a space with five dimensions or more, and no three-number color can show it faithfully. Petrov et al. store the normal as the matrix nnᵀ, which represents line directions faithfully but uses six numbers per point instead of three. Hironaka's lift along a crossing curve needs only the direction around that curve, which is a circle of line directions, so the 2D color wheel works again.

Red outlines mark indeterminate points, as in Figure 7. In the Boy inset, red outlines mark directions whose color is also used by a direction at least 25° away. With (|x|, |y|, |z|), the dashed red circles are folds: every color is used four times.

A practical pipeline would combine them: use Nash's cheap local fits to find where the data is singular, which is exactly where Nash's red outlines cluster, then use Hironaka's blowups there, worst parts first, to separate it.

8. Simultaneous impacts as a blowup

Vouga, Smith, Kaufman, Tamstorf, and Grinspun's "All's Well That Ends Well" resolves rigid bodies colliding at several contacts at once. The standard loop repeatedly applies an impulse at whichever contact is still approaching, in some fixed order. For a row of equal balls (Newton's cradle) each impulse swaps two neighbors' velocities, so the loop literally bubble-sorts the velocities; their paper makes this point. Their contribution is a proof that the loop stops. But stopping is not the same as being well defined: in general the answer depends on the order.

Blowups suggest a different algorithm. A simultaneous impact is a singular point of the map from "state before" to "state after": nearby states that hit the contacts one at a time give definite answers, but different nearby states can give different answers. So blow it up. Replace the simultaneous contact with every way of approaching it, solve each approach one contact at a time, and keep the results.

Two of the paper's examples turn out to be the same picture. Their wedge example (a ball into a corner) is the left mode below. Newton's cradle with three balls is the right mode: once you subtract the motion of the center of mass and rescale positions by √mass, the two contacts become two walls, and the corner angle is set by the masses: cos β = 1/(1 + m₂) when the outer balls have mass 1.

How to read Figure 9. A ball flies straight at the corner. The thin paths are nearby throws, shifted slightly sideways, colored by which wall they hit first; those two groups are the blowup's two "directions of approach". The thick arrows show where each group ends up. In cradle mode the walls mean "balls 1 and 2 touch" and "balls 2 and 3 touch", and the strip above shows the actual ball velocities.

Try the cradle with equal masses: the corner is 60°, the two orders agree, and the outer balls simply swap velocities. That is why the equal-mass cradle looks so tidy and why bubble sort gives a unique answer there. A heavy middle ball opens the corner toward 90°, where the two contacts barely interact. A light middle ball closes it, and the ball rattles back and forth with more and more impulses. In the limit of a massless middle ball the corner closes completely. That is the paper's ball between two parallel walls: opposing normals, an "implicit equality", where the loop never stops and they project the offending direction out instead.

To play with more balls and arbitrary masses, and to compare all three resolutions side by side (bubble sort, the blowup's chambers, and stiff springs), open the companion demo Resolving Newton's cradle.

The good corners are exactly the angles 180°/k: 90°, 60°, 45°, and so on. There the reflections close up into a finite pattern and every order gives the same answer. Right angles are the impact version of normal crossings: the contacts don't interfere.

With k contacts, the "directions of approach" are all relative timings of the k contacts, and they split into regions by which contact goes first, up to k! of them. A blowup-based solver would:

  1. Detect the contacts that are simultaneous: the center.
  2. Enumerate the regions of approach directions, by which contact is hit first, then next.
  3. Resolve each region one contact at a time, which is well defined.
  4. Report all the outcomes, or average them with weights given by each region's share of random perturbations. An average of elastic outcomes generally loses energy, so averaging is a modeling choice, not a free lunch.
  5. Skip all of this when the contact directions meet at a good angle in the mass metric, as with equal-mass cradles, because then the order provably doesn't matter.

The output is independent of how contacts are numbered, which the bubble sort is not. The cost is the number of regions. The weights are a modeling choice too: if the start is spread evenly over the corner, each order's share is its share of the corner's angle (the chip in Figure 9), while symmetric sideways shifts of the throw give 50/50. Halm and Posa's set-valued impact model takes a closely related view: it treats the set of physically reasonable outcomes of a simultaneous impact as the answer, instead of picking one order. Framing that set as the image of a blowup is my own suggestion, not a claim from either paper.

9. A game, and one function for every resolution

Choosing blowups does look like reinforcement learning, and Hironaka made it a game in 1970. Player 1 picks a center to blow up. Player 2 picks which chart to continue in. Player 1 wins when the equation in the current chart is a monomial, and must win in every chart. So it is not a single agent exploring. It is a game against an adversary who always takes you down the hardest branch. Spivakovsky proved in 1983 that Player 1 always has a winning strategy, and Bérczi, Fan, and Zeng (2023) have trained RL agents to play it.

In that language, the Kollár–Włodarczyk invariant is a ranking function: a number that every move lowers, which proves the game ends. Nash's rule is a policy without one, and the 4D counterexample is a cycle in the game's state graph. In the plane the only centers are points, so the only branching is the chart; real choices about the center start in three dimensions.

Different winning plays give different resolutions. Which one does singular learning theory need? None in particular. It needs one number that they all agree on: how fast the set of near-zeros shrinks. Here is how any resolution computes it, using the cusp's resolution from Figure 3.

  1. Each curve upstairs gives a rate. Near a curve E, f looks like u^N, where N is how many times f vanishes on E, and the area element looks like |u|^(ν − 1) du, where ν = h + 1. Add up the area where |u^N| < ε and it shrinks like ε^(ν/N).
  2. The slowest rate wins. The total area where |f| < ε is a sum over the curves, so as ε → 0 it shrinks like ε to the smallest ν/N. For the cusp that is E₃, with ν/N = 5/6.
  3. Every resolution gives the same winner. Extra, unnecessary blowups add curves with new rates, but never a smaller one. The zeta function shows something stronger: their contributions cancel exactly.

The zeta function Z(s) is the bookkeeping that makes step 3 precise. It is the same object as Watanabe's ζ(z) = ∫ K(w)^z φ(w) dw in section 11. Every curve and every crossing point of the resolution adds one term, and Denef and Loeser proved that the total is the same for every resolution. Each curve's rate ν/N appears as a value of s where its term blows up, at s = −ν/N. Those are the "poles".

How to read Figure 10. Left: the resolved picture, drawn as in Figure 3. Each violet line is an exceptional curve, labeled with its N and ν; orange lines are the original curve; black dots are crossing points. Hover over a line, a dot, or a row of the table to see which term it adds. Below: the measured area where |f| < ε on a log-log plot, with one guide line per rate ν/N. The data follows the steepest line it can, the smallest rate. Try the extra blowups: the picture and the terms change, but the sum and the plot do not.

Here is why the extra blowup changes nothing. Blowing up the point where E₁ (factor a = 2 + 2s) meets E₃ (factor b = 5 + 6s) creates a curve with factor a + b = 7 + 8s and proposes a new pole at −7/8. But it sits between exactly two neighbors, so its own term has χ = 0, and the old crossing term splits exactly: 1/(ab) = 1/(a(a+b)) + 1/(b(a+b)). The candidate cancels. Real poles are the candidates that survive every possible resolution.

And yes, there is a literal polynomial. The Bernstein–Sato polynomial b(s) is the smallest polynomial with b(s)·f^s = P(f^(s+1)) for some differential operator P. For the cusp it is (s + 1)(s + 5/6)(s + 7/6). It can be computed with Gröbner bases in the algebra of differential operators, without choosing any blowups, and its largest root is again −5/6. The cusp also hides a knot. Its intersection with a tiny sphere around the origin is a trefoil, whose Alexander polynomial t² − t + 1 has roots e^(±iπ/3). Those are e^(2πi·5/6) and e^(2πi·7/6), the same numbers once more.

The plot is the bridge to learning. The area where |f| < ε shrinks like ε^(5/6) because the area function and the zeta function are two views of the same thing (a Mellin transform pair): the largest pole of Z, at −5/6, is the exponent of the area. For a model the error is K = f², which halves every rate, so the near-optimal set of parameters shrinks like ε^(5/12). That 5/12 is the learning coefficient λ, and section 11 explains why it controls Bayesian learning. One caveat: Z above is computed with complex geometry, while learning uses the real version. Its poles lie among the same candidates, and here the largest agrees, as the measured slope shows.

10. Playing the game in three dimensions

In the plane every center is a point, so the only branching is the adversary's chart. In three dimensions you can blow up a point or a curve, and the choices start to matter. To see them cleanly, take error functions that are sums of squares of monomials, such as K = x²y² + y²z² + z²x², which vanishes on the three coordinate axes. For these, a blowup only moves exponents around, so the whole game is arithmetic on exponent vectors. This is Hironaka's original "polyhedra game".

  1. You choose a center: the origin, or a coordinate axis on which every monomial vanishes.
  2. The adversary chooses a chart: one coordinate of the center. For the x-axis {y = z = 0} and chart y, substitute z = y·z′.
  3. Exponents update: the y-exponent of every monomial becomes its old y-exponent plus its z-exponent. Then factor out whatever monomial they all share. That part is already a clean product of coordinates.
  4. You win when one monomial is left, meaning that in this chart K is a monomial times a positive function.

What the table shows:

  • Always blowing up the origin never finishes. Whenever the bad set contains a curve, blowing up a point can never remove it: for the first example one chart hands you back the exact same board, and for others the boards just keep growing. Even x⁴ + y⁶ + z¹⁰, whose bad set is just the origin, grows a bad curve after one blowup. It is the same kind of failure as Nash's loop, except here the fix is known: blow up the curve.
  • Greedy is not optimal. On x⁶ + y⁴z⁴, curve-first needs 6 moves against the worst adversary, while exact search needs 5.
  • Order alone is not enough. Choosing the stratum of worst order, the first entry of the invariant, runs forever on x⁸ + y²z² + z⁶. The Kollár–Włodarczyk invariant has further entries precisely so that this cannot happen. I have not implemented their full invariant here.
  • The objective matters. On the last example, the order rule uses fewer total blowups than the strategy that minimizes moves against the worst adversary. "Shortest worst case" and "least total work" are different rewards, a familiar story in RL.
  • λ never depends on the strategy. Every strategy that finishes reports the same λ and m, just as Figure 10's zeta function didn't change. For x²y² + y²z² + z²x² you can check it by hand: the diagonal (t, t, t) first meets K's Newton polyhedron on the face x + y + z = 4 at t = 4/3, so λ = 1/t = 3/4.

That is the division of labor. Search, whether exact as here or learned as in Bérczi, Fan, and Zeng's RL agents, decides how cheaply you reach a resolution. Everything singular learning theory needs is the same at every winning end state.

11. Watanabe's singular learning theory, and how it compares

Sumio Watanabe's theory is where all of this machinery meets statistics. It answers one question: how does Bayesian learning behave when the model is singular, meaning many different parameter settings fit the data equally well?

The setting. A model p(x | w) with parameters w in d dimensions, data drawn from a true distribution q, and a prior φ(w). The error function is the Kullback–Leibler divergence K(w) = KL(q ‖ p(· | w)) ≥ 0, which is zero exactly at the parameters that fit perfectly. Two quantities measure learning. The free energy F_n is minus the log of the evidence, the integral of likelihood times prior; Bayesian model selection compares models by it. The generalization error G_n is how far the Bayesian prediction is from the truth.

The regular case. If K has a single zero where it looks like a quadratic bowl, Laplace's method gives the classical answer, the Bayesian information criterion: F_n ≈ n·S_n + (d/2)·log n, with S_n the empirical entropy. Each parameter costs ½ log n. Neural networks are nothing like this. Their zero sets are large and singular: whole families of weights compute the same function, so there is no bowl to approximate.

Watanabe's main theorem (2001; book 2009)

Suppose the truth is realizable by the model, K is analytic, the prior is positive near the zero set of K, and some technical conditions hold. Let −λ be the largest pole of the zeta function ζ(z) = ∫ K(w)^z φ(w) dw, and m its order. Then

F_n = n·S_n + λ·log n − (m − 1)·log log n + O_p(1),    E[G_n] = λ/n + o(1/n).

λ is the real log canonical threshold, also called the learning coefficient. For a regular model λ = d/2 and m = 1, recovering BIC. For singular models λ can be far smaller than d/2.

Where Hironaka enters. Watanabe applies the theorem of section 3 to K. After the resolution π, near every point K(π(u)) = u^(2k) and φ(π(u))·|π′(u)| = |u^h|·b(u). Three things follow.

  1. λ becomes computable. The zeta function splits into one-variable integrals, so its largest pole is −min (h + 1)/(2k), the rule used throughout this page.
  2. The randomness becomes tractable. In resolved coordinates the log-likelihood ratio takes a "standard form", n·u^(2k) − √n·u^k·ξ_n(u), where ξ_n converges to a Gaussian process. Without resolving, the fluctuations near a singular zero set have no simple limit. This step is the technical heart of the theory.
  3. The posterior integral factorizes. Each coordinate contributes ∫ e^(−n·u^(2k)) |u|^h du ∝ n^(−(h+1)/(2k)), and the slowest coordinate, the one with the smallest (h + 1)/(2k), sets the log n coefficient.

So how does Watanabe's theory compare with everything above?

  • Same object, new meaning. λ and m are exactly the zeta-function data of section 9, applied to K. Figure 10's slopes, halved because K = f², are Figure 12's middle panel. Watanabe adds what they mean statistically: λ is the price, in log n per data point, of the model's effective complexity, and λ/n is its expected generalization error.
  • Only existence is needed. Any resolution gives the same λ and m, so Watanabe needs Hironaka's theorem but not any particular algorithm. The choice of centers in section 10's game affects only how hard λ is to compute by hand. Nash's failure in section 5 is irrelevant.
  • Here, volume picks a winner. In Newton's cradle every outcome region was a cone, so all shrank at the same rate and none dominated. In learning, different singular regions shrink at different rates ε^λ. As data grows, the posterior concentrates on the region with the smallest λ, the most near-optimal volume, even against a region with slightly lower loss. That trade-off produces phase transitions in what a model learns, which the developmental-interpretability program studies in toy models and real networks.
  • In practice no one resolves a neural network. Its parameter space has millions of dimensions. Instead, λ is estimated: Watanabe's WBIC computes the free energy at a special temperature, and the local learning coefficient of Lau, Murfet, Wei and coauthors estimates λ near a trained network by sampling its loss landscape with stochastic-gradient Langevin dynamics. The algebraic geometry on this page is what guarantees those estimates measure a well-defined quantity.

It also explains a design choice. Resolution's Lean formalization follows Kollár and Włodarczyk rather than Hironaka's original proof, and their versions come with an invariant that provably drops at every step. Nash's idea survives as a practical tool on data, where one or two lifts separate crossing sheets. But "keep lifting until it's smooth" is not a promise anyone can make in the thousands of dimensions of a network's parameter space.

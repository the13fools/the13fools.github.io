Singular learning theory

The local learning coefficient: one number for how much room a trained network has around its weights, why that number governs learning, and how to measure it.

The idea in one paragraph. Take a trained model and nudge its weights at random, keeping only nudges that barely change the loss. How much room is there? For an ordinary minimum, a round bowl, the room is set by the number of parameters, since the loss climbs out of the bowl in every direction. The minima neural networks find have much more room, because many directions are flat or nearly flat. The local learning coefficient λ turns that room into one number: half the number of parameters for a round bowl, and less for a degenerate minimum. Singular learning theory says that this number, not the parameter count, controls how a model learns and generalizes. And it can be measured with what amounts to a thermometer.

Chains of noisy gradient descent start at the minimum and wander, and the plot tracks the average loss they carry, scaled by n. That average settles at λ: 1 for the round bowl, and less for each degenerate loss, although all four have the same minimum value and the same two parameters. Try the product (ab)²: the chains spread out along its two flat axes, and the reading settles near 0.4 (its λ is 1/2, approached only slowly). The rest of Part III unpacks that number in three steps, each opening with its own demo: why it governs Bayesian learning (section 10), where degenerate minima come from in networks (section 11), and how the thermometer works on a trained network (section 12).

10. Why singular models learn differently: two perfect fits

Here is singular learning theory in one demo. A model has two parameters and two settings that fit the data perfectly, both with zero loss. Near one of them the loss rises like r², the usual bowl. Near the other it rises like r⁴: flat at the bottom, the kind of minimum the Hessian calls degenerate. Which one does Bayesian inference believe?

Figure 17. The posterior after n data points, drawn as 900 samples over the two parameters; the background is the loss, darker where lower. Blue samples are near the regular fit, orange near the degenerate one, grey elsewhere. Slide n up. With little data the posterior is spread almost evenly. As data accumulates it gathers near the two perfect fits, but not equally: the degenerate fit pulls ahead, the regular fit's share peaks and then shrinks, and in the end the degenerate fit holds nearly everything, though both fits are equally perfect.

New terms: temperature, equipartition, Hessian, harmonic approximation, posterior, free energy, learning coefficient.

The bridge from physics is a marble in a bowl. At temperature T it jiggles, visiting position w with probability proportional to e−V(w)/T. For a round bowl, equipartition says the average potential energy is ½T per direction the marble can move in. The usual derivation, and the usual way simulators treat any minimum, is the harmonic approximation: near the bottom, V ≈ ½ wᵀHw with H the Hessian, the jiggle is Gaussian, and the integral of e−V/T is proportional to Td/2/√det H.

Now make the bottom of the well a curve: V = (y² − x³)². The marble can slide along the cusp y² = x³ at zero energy. On that floor one Hessian eigenvalue is zero, and at the cusp point both are. The harmonic approximation's 1/√det H is infinite exactly where the marble spends its time.

Figure 18. Left: the well from above, darker where V is lower. The small plots are cross-sections across the floor: far from the cusp they are parabolas, at the cusp a quartic. Right: the Hessian's eigenvalues along the floor; one is always zero, and the other drops to zero at the cusp.

Cross-sections and Hessian

Bayesian learning is this marble. A model with parameters w has a loss K(w) ≥ 0 measuring how far it is from the truth. After n data points, the posterior weights each w by e−nK(w) times a prior: the Boltzmann distribution with K as the potential and n as 1/T. The free energy −log ∫ e−nK measures how well the model as a whole explains the data, and governs which model Bayesian model selection prefers.

For a regular model, a round bowl in d dimensions, the free energy grows like (d/2)·log n: the BIC penalty. Neural networks are not round bowls. Whole families of weights compute the same function, so their minima are flat-bottomed wells like the one above. Watanabe's theorem says that in general

\text{free energy} \approx nS_n + \lambda\log n - (m-1)\log\log n, \qquad \text{expected generalization error} \approx \frac{\lambda}{n}

where λ, the learning coefficient, is a volume exponent: the volume of parameters with K < ε shrinks like ελ(log 1/ε)m−1. Resolution of singularities is what computes it. After resolving, K is a monomial u2k with volume element |uh|du, each coordinate contributes (h + 1)/2k, and λ is the smallest such number. It plays the role of d/2, and can be much smaller. In physics terms this is generalized equipartition: a singular well holds λ·T of potential energy instead of (d/2)·T. That is why Resolution formalized Hironaka.

The practical consequence is that the posterior prefers volume, not just depth. A sharp, slightly deeper well (λ = 1) loses to a slightly shallower valley with a flat direction (λ = 1/2) until n is large enough for the depth difference to dominate.

Where blowups come in. λ is a volume exponent, and computing it is exactly what resolving singularities is for. Take the product loss from the thermometer, K = (ab)², whose zero set is the crossing a = 0 or b = 0 from Part I. Blow up the origin: in the chart a = u, b = u·v the loss becomes K = u⁴v², and the area element da db becomes |u| du dv. Every factor is now a power of a single coordinate, so the volume where K < ε can be computed one coordinate at a time. The coordinate u appears as u⁴ in the loss and u¹ in the area, so it contributes (1 + 1)/4 = 1/2; v appears as v² with v⁰, and contributes (0 + 1)/2 = 1/2. λ is the smallest contribution, 1/2, and because two coordinates tie it carries multiplicity m = 2, which is the log log n term above. That is the recipe of section 3.5, run by hand: resolve, read off the monomials, take the smallest ratio. For the round bowl a² + b² the same recipe gives 1, half of two parameters.

The demo, in these terms. The regular fit has λ = 1, the round-bowl value d/2. The degenerate one has λ = 1/2: the set where r⁴ < ε is a disk of radius ε1/4, whose area shrinks like ε1/2. The posterior mass near each fit scales like n−λ, so the degenerate fit's share relative to the regular one grows like n1/2: the orange curve in Figure 17. Depth doesn't decide it, since both fits are perfect; volume does. And the Hessian can't see volume. At the degenerate fit the Hessian is zero, so the harmonic approximation's 1/√det H, which is how a Laplace approximation would weigh the two fits, is infinite at every n.

Who wins in general, and why the opening demo is different. In general the posterior ends up in the singular region with the smallest λ, the one with the most volume near its perfect fits. Compare the cradle demo on the opening page. Its chambers are regions too, but its walls are flat, so as you zoom into the corner every chamber shrinks at exactly the same rate. They all have the same exponent, and their shares of the bar never change, however small the gaps. A singular loss is the opposite. Near the cusp of Figure 18, the region where K is small shrinks like ε5/12 at the cusp point and like ε1/2 along the smooth floor, so the cusp point takes a larger and larger share. Training by gradient descent is a dynamics, not a posterior, and may behave more like the cradle, landing wherever its approach sends it. Which picture describes real training is, as far as I can tell, open.

11. Where singularities come from in networks: one function, many weights

The two fits of section 10 were planted by hand. In a real network the degenerate places come for free, from the many ways different weights compute the same function. The smallest network already shows it.

Figure 21. One ReLU unit, f(x) = a·relu(w·x), learning the target c·relu(x) from inputs spread evenly over [−1, 1]. Left: its two weights, w across and a up, with 700 samples from the posterior at n = 200 (background: posterior density; black: the weights that fit perfectly; grey: curves a·w = constant, along which the unit can be rescaled without changing its output). Middle: the functions computed by 40 of those samples, drawn over the target: hundreds of different weights, one function. Right: the loss along the diagonal a = w. Slide c to 0, the target "output nothing", and the curve of perfect fits pinches into two crossing lines: a = 0 (output switched off) and w = 0 (input switched off).

New terms: rescaling symmetry, dead unit, true parameter set.

A network's parameters map to functions, and the map is many-to-one in several systematic ways. Each way creates flat directions in the loss, and wherever two of them meet, the set of perfect parameters, the true parameter set, has a singular point.

  • Rescaling. A ReLU unit satisfies a·relu(w·x) = (a/c)·relu(c·w·x) for every c > 0, so each unit's weights come in curves of equivalent settings: valleys in the loss.
  • Dead units. If a unit's output weight is zero, its input weights do nothing; if its input weights never let it fire, its output weight does nothing. Whole planes of settings compute the same function.
  • Duplicate units. Two units with the same input weights matter only through the sum of their output weights.
  • Factorized layers. Any product of weight matrices, BA in a linear block, WQWKᵀ in attention, a low-rank adapter, has the redundancy A → GA, B → BG⁻¹ of section 7.

Where these families intersect, say a unit that is both dead and rescaled to nothing, the true parameter set crosses itself, exactly like the lines of Figure 3. The intuition to carry into the rest of Part III: the more ways a network can implement a function, the more parameter volume sits near it, and the smaller its learning coefficient. Singular points are where several implementations meet, which makes them both the most degenerate places in parameter space and, to a Bayesian, the most attractive. That is the precise version of "networks prefer simple functions".

In the demo, the rescaling curve is a valley with one flat direction (λ = 1/2 instead of 1), and at c = 0 the switched-off output and the switched-off input meet in a crossing (λ = 1/2 again, now with multiplicity m = 2). Along the diagonal the loss is a parabola for c > 0, which the Hessian sees, and grows like s⁴ at the crossing, which it doesn't.

Model problems

Four families of examples, each small enough to compute λ, and each a piece of a real architecture.

Two parameters. A round bowl a² + b² has λ = 1 = d/2. The product (ab)², a one-unit linear network y = b·a·x learning y = 0, has λ = 1/2 with m = 2: two lines crossing, as in Figure 3. The squared cusp (b² − a³)² has λ = 5/12. For losses like these the Newton polygon of section 3.4 gives λ without resolving anything: walk up the diagonal of the exponent plot until you enter the polygon at (t*, t*), and λ = 1/t*.

Linear networks are matrix factorizations. A two-layer linear network y = BAx with a hidden layer of width H writes its weight matrix as a product W = BA, which is the factorization of section 7 and, for symmetric matrices, the Burer–Monteiro lift of section 8. Its redundancy is large: A → GA, B → BG⁻¹ for any invertible G leaves the network unchanged. Aoyagi and Watanabe (2005) computed λ exactly for every input size M, output size N, width H and true rank r. Adding hidden units adds H(M + N) parameters each time, but λ stops growing at MN/2, the count for the matrix itself. Overparameterizing a factorization is nearly free in Watanabe's accounting, just as lifting was nearly free in section 8.

Grey: d/2, what the parameter count would predict. Rings: my own check, the posterior average n·⟨K⟩ sampled at n = 1000 with the expected finite-n correction (m − 1)/log n added back. Bilinear layers are tensor factorizations. The feed-forward blocks of most current transformers are gated linear units, (W₁x) ⊙ σ(W₂x). Drop the nonlinearity σ and each output becomes a sum over hidden units of c·(a·x)(b·x): a CP decomposition of a three-way tensor, the object of section 7. When the two factors read different inputs, y = Σ c·(a·x)(b·z), with two-dimensional x, z and y, it is exactly the 2×2×2 tensor of section 7. No closed formula for λ is known to me here, so the right panel shows sampled values. With a generic rank-2 target, the sampled value is about 4.07 at both H = 2 and H = 3: half the 8 dimensions of the tensor space, the same as fitting the 8 numbers directly, and the extra hidden unit adds nothing. With the zero tensor as target it rises slowly with width, from 0.75 at H = 1 (whose exact λ is 1, with m = 3, so a finite-n reading sits below it) to 2.1 at H = 3, far below d/2 = 9. The border-rank tensor W of Figure 12 is where the theory runs out: no finite parameters fit it exactly, so Watanabe's theorem doesn't apply as stated. Sampling shows the symptom. As n grows from 30 to 1000, the posterior's typical parameter size keeps growing (3.3 to 3.9) and n·⟨K⟩ drifts upward (4.7 to 4.9) instead of settling, the statistical version of the fit that never finishes.

ReLU networks. A ReLU neuron computes a·relu(x − b): zero left of its kink b, then a line of slope a. Take two of them learning a single relu(x) from inputs spread over [−1, 1], and freeze the first at a₁ = 1 − a₂, b₁ = 0. The loss is exactly K = a₂²·D(b₂), with D(b) = b²/2 + O(b³). Its zero set is two lines crossing: a₂ = 0, where the second neuron is dead, and b₂ = 0, where it has merged with the first. So λ = 1/2 with m = 2 instead of the regular value 1. (Watanabe's proofs assume analytic models, and ReLU networks are only piecewise linear, but the volume exponent still makes sense.)

12. Measuring it: the local learning coefficient

Sections 10 and 11 computed λ for models small enough to resolve by hand. Nobody can resolve a real network; what you can do is measure λ, with the thermometer from the top of this page. There it ran on two-parameter losses. Here it runs on trained networks, small enough that the exact answer is still known.

The setup. The data are 1000 pairs (x, y) with x random in ℝ³ and y = W*x plus noise, where the true map W* has rank 1: it only uses one direction of x. The model is a two-layer linear network y = B·A·x with a hidden layer of width H, so A is H × 3, B is 3 × H, and there are 6H parameters. Train it by gradient descent, then point the thermometer at the trained weights, for H = 1 to 5. Counting parameters predicts that the room around the minimum grows with every hidden unit (the grey line, 3H). But the trained network computes essentially the same function at every width, so the extra parameters are mostly redundant, and the exact learning coefficient (Aoyagi and Watanabe's formula, the solid line) levels off at 4.5. The question is whether the thermometer sees that.

Figure 24. The LLC of trained linear networks y = BAx against hidden width H. Grey: half the parameter count, what counting predicts. Solid line: the exact learning coefficient. Dots: the thermometer's estimate at the trained weights, filled in one width at a time. The buttons set the localization strength γ, a spring that keeps the chains near the trained weights. Too weak, and the chains wander into regions the minimum doesn't describe and read high; too strong, and they are held too tightly and read low. In between, the estimates follow the solid line rather than the grey one. Exactly solvable models like this one are how the instrument gets calibrated.

Localization γ
New terms: local learning coefficient (LLC), tempered posterior, stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD), localization.

What the thermometer reads. The local learning coefficient λ(w*) is the volume exponent of section 10, computed only in a small neighborhood of a trained weight vector w*: how fast the volume of nearly-as-good parameters around w* shrinks as "nearly" tightens. It is local because a network has many singular regions, and the question is about the one training found.

Why a thermometer measures it. Put a tempered posterior around w*, with weight e−nβLn(w), where Ln is the training loss and β an inverse temperature. Its normalizing integral Z behaves like (nβ)−λ for large n, the same volume law as before. Differentiate log Z with respect to β and you get the average excess loss:

n\big(\mathbb{E}_\beta[L_n(w)] - L_n(w^*)\big) \approx \frac{\lambda}{\beta} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\lambda = n\beta\big(\mathbb{E}_\beta[L_n(w)] - L_n(w^*)\big)

That is equipartition read backwards: measure the average energy above the minimum, divide by the temperature, and you get the number of effective quadratic directions, halved, which for a singular minimum is λ. Any temperature works in the limit. The standard choice is β = 1/log n, which comes from Watanabe's widely applicable Bayesian information criterion (WBIC): a way to estimate the free energy of section 10 from samples at a single temperature. Appendix C explains where 1/log n comes from, with a figure.

How it's computed (Lau, Furman, Wang, Murfet and Wei):

  1. Train to w*.
  2. Run chains of stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics starting at w*: w ← w − (ε/2)·(nβ·∇L(w) + γ·(w − w*)) + √ε·ξ, with ∇L computed on a minibatch and ξ standard Gaussian noise. It is training with a random kick, run at temperature 1/(nβ).
  3. The γ term, the localization, is a spring tying the chains to w*, so they measure this minimum and not a neighboring one.
  4. Average Ln along the chains after a burn-in, and apply the formula.

The knobs are the step size ε, the chain length, γ and β, and in practice you check that the loss along the chains has stopped drifting.

In Figure 16 the prior is the square and there is no data noise, so β = 1 and γ = 0: the chains sample the posterior of section 10 directly. In Figure 24, β = 1/log n and γ is the knob you can turn.

Why not just look at the Hessian? Many complexity measures count the large eigenvalues of the Hessian at w*, an "effective number of parameters". At the cusp of section 10 the Hessian is exactly zero, so that count says 0, yet λ = 5/12; for the product (ab)² the Hessian is also zero and λ = 1/2. The Hessian sees only the quadratic part of the loss. The LLC sees how the loss actually grows, quartic and higher terms included, which is exactly where singular models differ from regular ones.

What it's for. Three uses stand out to me.

  • A complexity measure that ignores redundancy. Two networks with the same parameter count can have very different λ, and λ, not the parameter count, sets the generalization error in Watanabe's theorem. Jesse Hoogland's "You're measuring model complexity wrong" makes this case.
  • Watching development. Hoogland and coauthors tracked the LLC through transformer training and found that it changes in distinct stages, which line up with the model acquiring identifiable behaviors (Loss landscape degeneracy and stagewise development in transformers). Moving between singular regions shows up as a change in λ.
  • Pointing at parts. Restricting the estimate to a subset of weights, or to particular data, gives refined versions that can say which components are doing something complex. For safety work, this is a way to notice when what a model has learned changes, from the geometry of its loss alone.

13. Where I'd like to take this

Can singularity placement be automatic? For frame fields I asked whether something like Hironaka's algorithm could decide where singularities go. Not on its own: Hironaka's algorithm is deliberately conservative. It never perturbs the data, and to it a 4-fold zero z⁴ is already a resolved monomial. Deciding where singularities go means choosing among perturbations. But the machinery supplies most of a loop:

  1. Lift so nothing is committed yet (Burer–Monteiro, or the graph in the circle bundle).
  2. Project and find the worst point with the invariant of section 3.3: a field's winding at a point is the order of the zero of its projection there.
  3. List the splittings of each degenerate point with the Newton polygon of its local expansion, with perturbation parameters as extra variables: z⁴ − δ splits at δ1/4.
  4. Choose by energy when the energy cares (Ginzburg–Landau's log always says split), and by volume when it doesn't, which is Watanabe's free energy.

Repeat until every singular point is simple, winding ±1/4. Those are exactly the meshable vertices, valence 3 and 5: the frame-field version of normal crossings. As far as I know nobody has written this down as an algorithm.

Next steps.

  • Blowups as a simulation tool, measured against the baselines. Resolve collisions by chambers and their shares, and compare with complementarity solvers and All's Well on the hard cases of section 6. Compare all four frame-field methods on the same domains, including the graph lift. Fit tensors in blown-up charts and compare with regularized CP and block-term decompositions.
  • Exact learning coefficients as ground truth. With monomialization formalized, the next step toward a formal singular learning theory is Watanabe's asymptotics. The examples here have known answers to check against: the cusp (5/12), the ReLU crossing (1/2, m = 2), linear networks (Aoyagi and Watanabe's formula). Bilinear layers are a natural next family, and border-rank targets show where the theorem's hypotheses run out.
  • Robustness and AI safety. The physics examples suggest two cautions. A modeling choice, penalty or blowup or lift, changed the answer for frame fields; in learning, temperature, weight decay or a smoother activation might likewise change which singularity wins. And natural procedures failed to terminate, in collisions and in tensor fits; estimators and training loops near singularities may share that failure mode. Both seem relevant to the program in "SLT for AI Safety".

Jesse Hoogland's "Neural networks generalize because of this one weird trick" and "Generalization: from thermodynamics to statistical physics" are good next reads; the second takes the physics framing of section 10 much further. I'd be glad to hear where this goes wrong.

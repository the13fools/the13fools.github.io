When two components become one

A tensor fit with two parameters, one perfect prediction, and a direction that ordinary curvature cannot see.

9. Why redundant components change learning

Suppose we fit data by adding two simple components. What happens when those components become identical? Counting the two adjustable parameters still gives two. But the prediction may stop responding to one combination of them. This is a small, explicit example of the degeneracy that motivates singular learning theory.

Our “data” are just eight numbers: a $2\times2\times2$ tensor. The target has entry $T_{000}=1$ and every other entry zero. A component is built from the two-number vector $v(t)=(1,t)$: multiply three entries to get $(v(t)^{\otimes3})_{ijk}=v_i(t)v_j(t)v_k(t)$. Our prediction is the average of two such components:

T(a,b)=\tfrac12 v(a)^{\otimes3}+\tfrac12 v(b)^{\otimes3}.

The adjustable parameters are $a$ and $b$. At $a=b=0$, both components equal the target, so the fit is exact. The loss $K$ is the sum of the eight squared prediction errors, divided by 3 for convenience.

Use the mean $s=(a+b)/2$ and separation $d=(a-b)/2$ as coordinates. Moving together changes $s$; pulling apart changes $d$. The three entries with exactly one index equal to 1 are all $s$. The three with two indices equal to 1 are all $s^2+d^2$. The last entry is $s^3+3sd^2$.

Along the separation direction $s=0$, the prediction changes only by $d^2$, so its squared error is $K=d^4$. Halving the separation reduces loss by a factor of 16. Along the mean direction, loss starts with $s^2$, so halving a small mean shift reduces loss by roughly a factor of 4. The Hessian detects the second direction as flat even though the prediction does eventually change.

The exact loss, and what this example does and does not represent
K(s,d)=s^2+(s^2+d^2)^2+\tfrac13(s^3+3sd^2)^2.

Near the origin this is bounded above and below by positive constants times $s^2+d^4$. Consequently it has the same local volume exponent as that simpler loss. Swapping the two components sends $d$ to $-d$; the flat direction comes from first-order cancellation, not merely from having two names for a component.

We fixed both component amplitudes and tied their three factor vectors together. This is a deliberately restricted two-parameter tensor family. Its coefficient is not the coefficient of the unrestricted rank-two tensor model.

Why this matters for learning

A parameter count sees two directions. A Gaussian approximation expects loss to rise quadratically in both. Here it rises quartically in one. That means more nearby parameter settings remain almost correct. Next we measure how much more room there is, then see why a Bayesian calculation notices it.

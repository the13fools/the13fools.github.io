The problem and approach

A clique is a set of vertices with an edge between every pair. The planted clique problem asks us to identify a small clique hidden in a random graph. In my main benchmark, planting preserves each vertex’s degree. Recovery therefore depends on structure beyond the number of neighbours.

I place one point per vertex on the sphere. Edges pull their endpoints together, triangles contract, and triples with no edges spread out. Between relaxation steps, I move the points toward patches with many small triangles. I use the resulting positions to rank the graph’s triangles.

For each proposed triangle, I can restrict the search to vertices adjacent to all three corners. If the triangle is planted, this smaller set contains the whole remaining clique. I evaluate the ranking by counting how many proposals it takes to reach the first planted triangle.

Related work

In the ordinary random-graph model, spectral methods and message passing have recovery guarantees when the clique is a sufficiently large multiple of √n, where n is the number of vertices. My experiments use cliques of comparable size in degree-matched graphs. Deshpande and Montanari give a message-passing guarantee for clique sizes of at least (1 + ε)√(n/e) for any fixed ε > 0.

The baseline is signed codegree, which compares the adjacency lists of two vertices. Agreement on an edge or a missing edge raises the score. Disagreement lowers it. Summing over all three pairs gives a triangle score computed directly from the graph.

What the experiments show

Four clusters also form on graphs without a planted clique. On the benchmark, the most enriched cluster tends to contain a larger share of the clique than its size would suggest. I test whether that concentration improves recovery against matched controls.

An early triangle in one selected run
On a graph of 2,000 vertices with a clique of 30, codegree with a bonus for compact triangles produced its first planted triangle at rank 21. Codegree alone reached its first at 359. From a second start on the same graph, the triangle that ranked 21st fell to rank 29,343.
A tie at the same recovery budget
On a separate panel, I compared codegree restricted to triangles inside the four clusters with codegree on the whole graph. Both recovered 12 of 14 cliques with the same decoder and 1,024 proposals per graph. A reliable gain from geometry remains an open question.

First-year plan

  • Test whether geometry improves the ranking

    Choose the checkpoint and triangle score on development graphs, then freeze both. On at least 40 fresh degree-matched graphs, compare how soon each ranking reaches its first planted triangle. Use signed codegree and positions shuffled within degree bands as controls, with the same proposal budget for each.

  • Explain when a cluster helps

    For one relaxation step followed by a cut, bound the chance of keeping a given share of the clique while shrinking the search space.

  • Analyze recovery from a seed

    Analyze the first few steps of a message-passing method that uses edges and triangles. Account for the correlations that arise when many triangles share the same edges. This aim builds on the seeded decoder benchmark.

The first-year outputs are a reproducible ranking benchmark and a mathematical analysis of the cut and the initial recovery updates.

Corner enrichment and matched decoder controls

The spherical quench concentrates the clique well above chance

Without labels and without a degree signal, the quench puts more of the clique into one corner than chance puts of a random k-set. Across 42 planted vs 6 null graphs (n = 1,000 and 2,000), planted enrichment has median 1.63× and reaches 2.99× (15 of 20 vertices in a 251-vertex corner at n = 1,000; and 27 of 40 in 504, 2.68×, at n = 2,000), while nulls stay at median 1.30× and never exceed 1.62× (Mann–Whitney p = 0.008; 22 of 42 planted graphs exceed the null maximum).

When decoded by an exact maximum-clique search per corner, this concentration yields direct recovery where naive baselines fail: 7 of 24 in K456 at n = 1,000 (κ 0.63–0.76) against 0 of 24 for random four-way partitions searched identically (sign test p = 0.016; 9 of 27 vs 1 of 27 when pooled with the earlier K450 pilot in the baseline table, p = 0.022), and 10 of 12 at n = 2,000 (κ 0.67–0.89) where baseline AMP scores 0/12.

Matched controls separate partitioning from geometry

Do quench corners give triangle-proposal decoders better proposals? Not yet, once the decoder’s budget and the graph-only ranker are matched. Two controls show this.

  • The 4× call-count confound (K458h vs K464): Running a pool-based low-rank AMP decoder separately on each of the four corners initially beat a single whole-graph centered-triangle pool 34/42 vs 25/42. Auditing the budgets revealed that each corner call ran 512 AMP completions (4 × 512 total vs 1 × 512). Substituting random four-way partitions with the same four calls immediately matched the corners at 34/42 = 34/42 (4 vs 4 discordant, p = 1.0); corners generated the same number of all-clique proposals as random partitions (48 vs 48) and fewer than the whole-graph centered-triangle pool (48 vs 142).
  • Corner truncation and soft reweighting vs whole-graph codegree (K458h3, K460 & K463): With a stronger decoder that ranks triangles by pairwise codegree and completes the top 1,024, keeping only intra-corner triangles tied whole-graph codegree at 12/14 = 12/14 on the hardest graphs. When the clique gathered inside one corner, recovery arrived earlier (e.g., proposal 548 → 184); when the top planted triangle straddled two corners, filtering discarded it (proposal 18 → 403, even on a graph with 17/26 clique vertices in one corner). Replacing the hard cut with a soft cosine bonus on triangle scores (K463) preserved cross-corner seeds but yielded no net gain (13/14 at β = 2, earlier on only 4 of 12 shared hits; 4/4 nulls clean). At budget 4,096, whole-graph codegree alone reached κ = 0.49 (5/6 at n = 2,000).

The geometric model motivated an algebraic comparison using signed edge and triangle channels. A heuristic state evolution predicts critical κ values of 1.000 for the unseeded pairwise channel, 0.779 for triangles alone, 0.647 for the hybrid, and 0.625 for the recursion summing all clique orders.

Limitations

  • Geometry does not yet beat the best graph-only search. Quench corners cluster the clique (p = 0.008) and beat random partitions under exact per-corner search (9/27 vs 1/27, p = 0.022), but under strong decoders they tie random 4-way partitions on equal-call AMP pools (34/42 = 34/42) and tie whole-graph codegree on the 14 hardest graphs (12/14 = 12/14).
  • Dense spherical concentration dilutes with n. At fixed κ, the clique’s share of global triangle density decays like n−3/2; at n = 4,000, κ = 0.79, single-solve quench concentration fell back to null, and at n = 4,000, κ = 0.60, codegree recovered 2/4 vs 1/4 for corners (while 1-seed (2+3)-body AMP recovered 3/4 in ~30 s and 2-seed edge AMP 4/4 in 2.6 s; at κ = 0.51, K459 scored 0/4 across all triangle arms while 2-seed edge AMP certified 4/4 in 8.2 s).
  • Finite-size regime. The experiments use k comparable to √n. For evaluating the smaller-size question k = n1/2−ε, one needs a protocol in which κ declines with n and a proof or sufficiently large scaling study.
  • Finite samples. Individual cells hold 4–11 graphs, so pooled tests (enrichment p = 0.008; exact corner search p = 0.022; K456 1-seed AMP 35/36 vs 17/36) carry the statistical weight rather than single points.

Method and analysis

Solver specification and phase analysis

Place every vertex at a point on the unit sphere S². Clique vertices belong to all the triangles internal to the planted set. The weighted forces test whether this local structure can produce a detectable geometric effect. The energy has two terms: an angular spring on every edge, and a weighted squared area for every triple of points, where the weight depends on how many of the triple’s three edges exist.

E(X) = ½ ks Σij ∈ E (θij − θ0)² + (μ / n) Σa<b<c f(mabc) · area(xa, xb, xc)²,   xv ∈ S²,   mabc = Aab + Abc + Aca

The benchmark runs minimize the quench energy alone from a uniform start for 15 cycles, splitting the cloud into four balanced clusters at tetrahedral angles (109.47°); the recorded movies in Replay prefix 6 gather cycles so initial cohesion is visible before the split. The example below follows one recorded gather-then-quench run (n = 2,000, k = 35, κ = 0.78); exact energies and solver moves follow immediately below.

  1. Zero-gap graph. 35 clique vertices among 2,000 (1.75%), with every vertex’s degree unchanged from the base G(n, ½) graph. Single-vertex degrees carry zero signal.
  2. Transport and relax on S². Edges pull pairs toward 10° and closed triangles contract while Sinkhorn transport moves mass toward patches dense in small triangles.
  3. Tetrahedral quench. Empty-triple repulsion splits the sphere into four balanced corners (~500 vertices each). This run split the clique 20 / 7 / 6 / 2—57% in one corner where chance gives 25%.
  4. Certified readout. Search or propose triangles inside each corner and complete on the full graph. Check every returned edge, then evaluate recovery against the plant. This run recovered all 35 planted vertices.

What a geometric corner buys—and what it costs

Landing 20 of 35 clique vertices inside a 504-vertex corner raises effective density from κ = 35/√2,000 = 0.78 to κcorner = 20/√504 = 0.89, making the subproblem substantially easier. The structural cost is hard truncation: any high-scoring triangle whose vertices straddle two corners is discarded. The matched controls measure both effects.

Two energies, used one after the other

The recorded runs minimize two different energies in sequence. Both share the edge springs and a squared-area term whose weight f(m) depends on how many of a triple’s three edges exist (m = 0, 1, 2, 3).

Stage 1 · gather (6 cycles). Pulls connected structure together.

\[ \begin{aligned} E_G &= \tfrac52\!\sum_{ij\in E}(\theta_{ij}-10^\circ)^2 + 0.5\!\!\sum_{ij\notin E}\! e^{-\theta_{ij}^2/2(30^\circ)^2} \\ &\quad + \tfrac{10}{n}\!\!\sum_{a<b<c}\! f_G(m_{abc})\,\mathrm{area}^2 + E_1 + E_2 \end{aligned} \]

fG = (−8, 0.1, 0.1, 4), so f(3)/|f(0)| = ½. E1 pushes a vertex adjacent to neither endpoint away from an edge’s midpoint; E2 penalizes a non-edge whose endpoints share many nearby neighbours (both 15° kernels). Transport targets a sharp density (σ 5°) with half the mass uniform.

Stage 2 · quench (15 cycles). Splits the cloud into corners.

\[ E_Q = \tfrac52\!\sum_{ij\in E}(\theta_{ij}-10^\circ)^2 + \tfrac{10}{n}\!\!\sum_{a<b<c}\! f_Q(m_{abc})\,\mathrm{area}^2 \]

fQ = (−16, 0.1, 0, 1), so f(3)/|f(0)| = 1/16: the push on empty triples dominates. No repulsion, E1 or E2. Transport is smoother (σ 15°) with a 0.2 uniform share. The benchmark runs use this stage alone, from a uniform start.

How the solver runs

The state is n unit vectors in ℝ³. A cycle starts with transport, followed by relaxation. The benchmark quench uses 15 cycles from a uniform start.

  1. Transport. Estimate the density of small graph triangles on an icosphere grid. In the quench, use a 45° locality scale, 15° smoothing, and a uniform mass share β = 0.2. Entropic optimal transport gives a barycentric target for each point. Add a sideways component of weight 0.3.
  2. Relax. Take 200 projected gradient steps on the energy. Normalize each update so that the largest tangent displacement is about 1°. The relative weights of springs and the four triangle families govern this step.

At the end, spherical k-means with four clusters gives the corners that the read-outs search.

Why the gather forms two circles and the quench a tetrahedron

Both shapes follow from one identity. Let \(m = \tfrac1n\sum_v x_v\) be the cloud’s mean and \(M = \tfrac1n\sum_v x_v x_v^{\top}\) its second-moment matrix, so \(\operatorname{tr} M = 1\). For any \(n\) unit vectors, \[ \sum_{a<b<c} \mathrm{area}(x_a,x_b,x_c)^2 \;=\; \frac{n^3}{8}\Big[\,1-\operatorname{tr}(M^2)-2\,m^{\top}(I-M)\,m\,\Big]. \] (Expand \((x_b-x_a)\times(x_c-x_a) =\) \(x_a\times x_b + x_b\times x_c + x_c\times x_a\) and sum.) The bracket is at most \(2/3\), with equality exactly when \(m = 0\) and \(M = I/3\): the cloud is centred and looks the same in every direction.

The negative empty-triple term favours large squared area. Replacing its graph mask by its mean gives the negative of the whole-cloud sum above. This suggests why the measured clouds stay close to isotropy. Both of the observed shapes satisfy that constraint.

  • Gather: two circles at polar angles \(\arccos(\pm 1/\sqrt3) \approx 54.7^\circ\) and \(125.3^\circ\), with points spread evenly around each. This is a one-dimensional set with \(M = I/3\). The recorded runs land on 54–55° and 125–126°.
  • Quench: the four vertices of a regular tetrahedron, the smallest point set on the sphere with \(m = 0\) and \(M = I/3\). The recorded corners sit at 109–110° apart, the tetrahedral angle.

In K466 the circles persisted when the transport grid was rotated and when transport was omitted. They also formed on a null graph. The identity permits both shapes. The effect of the extra gather terms on which shape is reached is an open question.

What this means for the clique. The rings and corners can form in the bulk on their own. I will measure whether the clique changes where vertices land within those shapes and whether the effect is visible in early triangle proposals.

What we understand, and what we don’t

  • The area² family is a global spread-or-collapse knob, with a sharp transition between r = 1.5 and 2. It does not single out the clique by itself.
  • The gathered clique is not a ground state. With every vertex free to move (K385), a state with the clique gathered does not have lower energy than ordinary restarts. Total energies differ by about 0.5%, and dense decoy sets sometimes end lower. So the concentration we measure is a property of the dynamics (relax, transport and the tetrahedral split) rather than of the minimizer. Any theory has to analyse the flow, not the energy landscape alone.
  • A corner assignment behaves like a degree vote. Tern’s analysis treats one exact best response with four fixed, balanced corners. Each vertex then joins the corner where it has the most neighbours. As n grows, the clique’s κ inside its best corner tends to half its whole-graph value, which is exactly the value an even split gives. One such step provably adds nothing in the limit. The concentration we observe must come from iterating the flow, or from finite size, and we cannot yet tell which.
  • Why four corners: the tetrahedron is the smallest isotropic support (box above). What sets the corner sizes, and whether a different energy would favour a corner holding most of the clique, is open.
  • A localized triangle term. For a uniformly shrinking triangle, squared area is fourth order in its diameter. A Gaussian reward in squared diameter changes the near-coincidence energy at second order. Its effect on the bulk phase and the proposal ranks can be tested separately.

Why a sphere?

The sphere gives a simple setting for tracking the shapes and relative angles of a low-dimensional embedding. Squared chord-triangle area is quadratic in the Gram entries of the unit vectors. This gives a rank-constrained Gram formulation and a starting point for investigating convex relaxations. The intermediate states are visible in the replay.

Why optimal transport?

Gradient steps move a point at most a degree at a time. In each cycle, entropic optimal transport (Sinkhorn) relocates every point toward the smoothed density of small complete triangles, mixed with a uniform share to prevent collapse—the one non-local move across the sphere. K466 and Petrel-05b show the global shapes form without it; Aim 3 isolates its net effect on baseline quench recovery.

Seed restriction and triangle sampling

Common-neighbour restriction in the ordinary model

Lemma 1 (restricting to a seed). Let \(G\) be \(G(n,\tfrac12)\) with a planted clique \(K\), \(|K| = k\). Fix \(S \subseteq K\) with \(|S| = s\), and let \(N(S)\) be the set of vertices outside \(S\) that are adjacent to every vertex of \(S\). Conditional on \(K\), the graph \(G[N(S)]\) is again a planted-clique instance: \[ N(S) = (K \setminus S) \,\cup\, U, \qquad \Pr[v \in U] = 2^{-s} \text{ independently for each } v \notin K, \] every edge of \(G[N(S)]\) not inside \(K \setminus S\) is an independent fair coin, and \(K \setminus S\) is a clique of size \(k-s\).

Proof. Whether \(v \notin K\) lies in \(U\) depends only on the \(s\) edges between \(v\) and \(S\). These are disjoint from the edges among \(V \setminus S\), and in \(G(n,\tfrac12)\) disjoint edge sets are independent. Hence the membership events are independent, each with probability \(2^{-s}\), and independent of every edge inside \(N(S)\). \(\square\)

Corollary 2 (the seed raises κ). \(|U| \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n-k,\,2^{-s})\) and \(n'=|N(S)|=k-s+|U|\). If \(2^s k=o(n)\) and \(s=o(k)\), concentration gives \(n'=2^{-s}n(1+o(1))\) with high probability, so \[ \kappa' \;=\; \frac{k-s}{\sqrt{n'}} \;=\; 2^{s/2}\,\kappa\,(1+o(1)). \]

Corollary 3 (completion from a fixed pure triangle). For \(s=3\) in the ordinary model, the sufficient ratio for AMP in the restricted graph is reached when \[ \kappa \;>\; \frac{1+\varepsilon}{\sqrt{8e}} \;\approx\; 0.214\,(1+\varepsilon). \] For a fixed planted triangle, the restricted graph satisfies Lemma 1, and AMP recovers \(K\setminus S\) with high probability in this regime. I use the rank \(T\) of the first pure triangle to evaluate the proposal sequence. In a uniformly random ordering of the graph triangles, \[ \Pr[\text{a random triangle of } G \text{ is pure}] \;\approx\; \frac{\binom{k}{3}}{\binom{n}{3}/8} \;\approx\; 8\kappa^{3} n^{-3/2}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[T] \approx \frac{n^{3/2}}{8\kappa^{3}}. \] At \(n = 2000\) and \(k = 30\), that is about 1 in 37,000. With \(s\) seeds instead of 3, \(s = \lceil 2\log_2\!\big(1/(\kappa\sqrt e)\big)\rceil + 1\) suffices for any \(\kappa>0\), for fixed small \(\kappa\). This gives the familiar seed-enumeration bound in that regime.

The fixed-seed reduction explains why proposing a pure triangle can be valuable. A seed selected by inspecting the graph induces extra conditioning, and degree-preserving swaps correlate the edges. Completion on those inputs requires its own guarantee. The experiments report first-pure rank and check decoder outputs against the planted set.

If a corner \(P\) holds \(s=\sigma k\) planted vertices, the fraction of its graph triangles that are pure is exactly \(\binom{s}{3}/\#\{\text{triangles in }G[P]\}\). In a quarter-sized corner with the background triangle density of \(G(n,\tfrac12)\), the leading approximation is \(64\sigma^3\) times the whole-graph pure fraction. A data-dependent corner can have a different triangle density, so I measure the denominator and the actual ranks. Its effective clique ratio is \(\kappa_{\mathrm{corner}}=\sigma k/\sqrt{|P|}\), which is \(2\sigma\kappa\) for an exactly balanced four-way partition.

Hyperplane rounding and the adaptive question

Aim 1 · What hyperplane rounding requires

The Goemans–Williamson separation probability gives a one-step retention bound for the clique.

Lemma 4 (hyperplane rounding keeps the clique together). Let \(x: V \to S^{d-1}\) be any embedding and let \(\bar\theta\) be the average angle \(\angle(x_u, x_v)\) over ordered pairs of distinct clique vertices. Cut with a uniformly random hyperplane through the origin, and let \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2\) be the fractions of the clique on the two sides. Then \[ \mathbb{E}\big[\max(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)\big] \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}\big[\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2\big] \;=\; \frac1k + \Big(1-\frac1k\Big)\Big(1-\frac{\bar\theta}{\pi}\Big). \]

Proof. Because \(\sigma_1+\sigma_2 = 1\), \(\max_i \sigma_i \ge \sum_i \sigma_i^2\). The sum \(\sum_i \sigma_i^2\) is the probability that two clique vertices drawn independently and uniformly, with replacement, land on the same side. They are the same vertex with probability \(1/k\). Otherwise, the Goemans–Williamson lemma says a random hyperplane separates \(x_u\) and \(x_v\) with probability \(\angle(x_u,x_v)/\pi\). Averaging over pairs gives the identity. \(\square\)

From expectation to a useful cut. Write \(B\) for the right-hand side of Lemma 4 and \(M=\max(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)\). Since \(M\le 1\), for \(a<B\) we have \(\Pr(M\ge a)\ge(B-a)/(1-a)\). If a cut leaves at most \((\tfrac12+\eta)n\) vertices on either side and \(M\ge a\), the richer side has \(\kappa'\ge a\sqrt{2/(1+2\eta)}\,\kappa\). With negligible imbalance, \(\bar\theta<52.7^\circ\) is a sufficient angular margin for choosing \(a\) to give a gain. One must also control how often the cut is balanced. A bound on the imbalance probability can be subtracted from the retention bound to control the joint event.

The adaptive question. An iterative guarantee would require a probability lower bound for that joint event conditional on the past cuts along a surviving branch. A constant conditional probability of a fixed gain would yield a path of \(O(\log n)\) cuts with inverse-polynomial probability. Turning such a path into recovery also requires a decoder guarantee for the selected subgraph. The first-year claim concerns one controlled step. The adaptive setting is a further research question.

Further analysis and bounds

Four analyses of the dynamics

Four analyses address why the flow concentrates at n ≤ 2,000, why the concentration dilutes at n = 4,000, and what limits triangle-based decoders. Analyses 2–4 are derivations written up in the agents’ notes. Analysis 1 is a heuristic scaling argument, not a proof.

1. Free-vertex 3-body spin-glass scaling (Θ(√n), not n2/5)

Holding background vertices fixed on S² suggests a gathered clique beats noise once k³ ≫ k√n + √k·n, i.e. k ≫ n2/5. Letting all 2n spherical coordinates relax changes this: dense mean-zero 3-body couplings act as a 3-spin glass whose noise ground state reaches Enoise ≈ −c n² (~c n per vertex). Pinning k clique vertices forfeits ~c k n of noise-lowering freedom, giving the global-minimum balance k³/48 ≳ c k n, i.e. k ≳ C√n, consistent with the n = 600 and n = 4,000 runs.

2. Walsh chaos of triangle density (97.5% edge noise)

Expanding raw local triangle excess in edge signs Xe = 2Ae − 1 gives 8R = Σe de Xe + Σt ht(XaXb + XbXc + XcXa + XaXbXc). Shared single-edge fluctuations account for (m−2)/(m−2+4/3) = 97.5% of local variance in a 54-vertex patch. Projecting out the edge term yields the unique Cauchy–Schwarz-optimal pure 3-clique field with triple weights (1, 0, −1, 2)—and its exact edge-null sphere potential f = (−1, −3, −5, 1).

3. Exact Sinkhorn splitting instability (18.89×)

On an ideal 4-partite tetrahedral configuration (109.47° corner spacing), both the graph energy and Sinkhorn substeps have an exact fixed point, yet zero-mean within-corner perturbations amplify under the shared barycentric transport Jacobian DF(x) = [2/(ε‖m‖)](I − FFᵀ) Covp(g). A continuum bound gives a splitting multiplier g ≥ (11/96)·(2/ε) ≈ 7.64, and it measures 18.89 on the icosphere mesh, which explains why unanchored masked transport destabilizes clean corners once local triangles disappear.

4. Uniform adaptive completion & codegree barriers

Using ε-net spectral bounds uniform over all graph-dependent subsets, any corner P of size m admits deterministic spectral + two-threshold completion on the same graph (even under degree-preserving switches) whenever s ≥ C√(m log(en/m)), and r = ⌈log₂(n/k²)⌉ + O(1) pure seed vertices suffice uniformly. Conversely, at k = n1/2−ε, centered codegree places zero planted pairs inside the top n1+δ edges (δ < 2ε) with high probability.

Research methods and collaboration

This program was conducted as an experiment in human-directed, multi-agent mathematical research inside one shared workspace, with independent logs, preregistered predictions, and cross-checked proofs: Wren ran the spherical flows, isotropy checks, and matched-control threshold panels; Lark proved the Walsh variance decomposition and Sinkhorn fixed-point instability; Tern proved the uniform adaptive completion and codegree-barrier lemmas; Heron formalized the multi-body motif-AMP covariance laws; and Petrel audited the two-ring spherical flows, built the zero-gap multi-body and edge-seeded AMP benchmarks, and ran the Walsh-clean sphere-flow ablation.

Research context and milestones

Related results and scaling regimes

Write κ = k/√n for a clique of size k in a graph with n vertices. A smaller constant κ and a clique of size n1/2−ε are different regimes. The experiments here probe the constant-κ regime.

  • Recovery in the ordinary model. Alon, Krivelevich and Sudakov use spectra and common-neighbour conditioning. Deshpande and Montanari give nearly linear-time message passing above κ = 1/√e. Kunisky and Mao use signed subgraph counts to recover above κ = 0.3320 in nω+o(1) time.
  • The graph-only comparison. Signed codegree measures agreement between two vertices’ adjacency rows, treating edges as +1 and absent edges as −1. Summing this score over the three pairs gives a strong way to rank triangles without an embedding.
  • The geometric question. Known barriers at n1/2−ε apply to specified classes such as low-degree tests and sum-of-squares relaxations. I ask what a low-dimensional nonlinear flow can add to those graph statistics on the degree-matched benchmark.
Detailed research aims

Aim 1 · Rounding for controlled spherical flows

I will first analyze one relaxation and rounding step on a fixed background and bound the joint probability of clique retention and bulk balance. Tern’s fixed-corner best-response result gives a reference where a single step has the same asymptotic clique ratio as an even split. The first-year goal is a quantitative statement for a specified flow and rounding rule. A bound that persists through adaptive cuts would support a longer result.

To study a possible obstruction, I will approximate rotation-invariant readouts, such as pair affinities and triangle scores, by graph polynomials. A lower bound would require approximation in a norm that controls the rounded proposal statistic under both graph models. Small-degree fits are diagnostics for formulating that statement.

Aim 2 · State evolution for dependent motif AMP

For a signed matrix S, the triangle channel uses Tijk = SijSjkSki. The four triangle signs on a tetrahedron multiply to one. This illustrates the dependencies a proof must handle when the channels reuse the graph. I will derive the covariance and memory correction through the first few updates in the ordinary model, then test how the degree-preserving switches and seed conditioning enter. The 0.647 and 0.371 values are targets from the heuristic equations.

Aim 3 · A controlled test of phase-dependent proposals

I will intervene on the relative spring and triangle weights and test a localized Gaussian reward for small graph triangles. I will record the phase, the triangle readout, and the geometry before and after transport and relaxation. The Walsh-clean and anchored variants in Petrel-05b reproduced tetrahedral shapes while providing weak separation from the null controls. They motivate testing the proposal ranks directly.

I will freeze a rule for choosing the checkpoint and the score on development graphs, then compare it on at least 40 fresh degree-matched graphs against signed codegree and within-degree position shuffles. I will first calibrate near κ = 0.67 and include a harder panel near κ = 0.45. The primary endpoints are first-pure rank and pure-triangle counts at fixed prefixes, with the same proposal budget and a miss recorded whenever the prefix contains zero pure triangles. Any claim of improvement will use paired, graph-level uncertainty. I will also measure how often the same fixed decoder converts those proposals into an exact recovery.

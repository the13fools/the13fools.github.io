What are we trying to change?

A polynomial zero set is a geometric space: all the points satisfying its equations. Many such spaces are smooth, but a crossing has no single local sheet and a cusp has no regular local parameter in its given algebraic coordinates. These are failures of smoothness at exact solutions of exact equations.

We want a smooth space $Y$ with a map $\pi:Y\to X$ back to the original zero set. At ordinary points, the map should be invertible. Over a singular point, several new points may map to the same old one. Those extra distinctions let calculus work upstairs while keeping an exact relationship to the original problem.

Rounding the picture or moving vertices would change the solutions. A resolution instead changes the space representing them. It is a geometric method for studying equations, but it is a different goal from geometrization theorems about classifying manifolds.

Two constructions, and one theorem

An ordinary blowup needs a chosen center. At a point center, attach the direction of the line from that point to each nearby point, then include the limiting records above the center. For a smooth higher-dimensional center, record the normal direction of approach. This is the construction from Part I. The exceptional set consists of the records that project into the center.

The Nash blowup needs the tangent planes of the variety. At each smooth point $p$ of a $d$-dimensional space, attach its tangent $d$-plane $T_pX$. Include all limits of these pairs as $p$ approaches the singular set. A tangent plane here means its linear direction, translated to the origin. A new point is “old position, together with a limiting tangent plane”. Nash also has exceptional fibers: several tangent-plane records can sit above one old point.

Hironaka’s theorem supplies a terminating strategy. There is no single competing operation called “the Hironaka blowup”. The classical approach chooses smooth centers and performs a sequence of ordinary blowups, checking the remaining singularities and exceptional boundary. Constructive proofs explain how to make those choices and guarantee that the process finishes in characteristic zero. Repeating Nash’s particular rule has no such guarantee in general.

Ordinary point blowup

Record the line from the chosen center to p. On a plane curve, its slope is $y/x$.

Nash blowup

Record the curve’s own tangent at p. Where it exists, its slope is $dy/dx$.

Both constructions retain extra geometric information; neither averages neighboring points as Laplacian smoothing does. On $y=x^2$, for example, the two recorded slopes are $x$ and $2x$. They are different rules even when their limiting slopes agree.

What is an exceptional divisor?

It is the whole set lying over the center we blew up. For a point center, its points are different direction records with the same old position. If $\pi$ is the map back and the center is $O$, this set is $E=\pi^{-1}(O)$: all new points sent to $O$. Away from the center, position determines the record uniquely. Above the center, many records share one image: this is where our point blowup ceases to be one-to-one. The formal definition is precisely this inverse image of the center.[35]

“Divisor” describes its dimension. In a smooth blowup chart, setting one coordinate to zero leaves one fewer independent coordinate. In Part I’s two-dimensional chart $(x,t)$, $x=0$ leaves the slope $t$ free: an exceptional line. In a three-dimensional chart $(v,w,u)$, $u=0$ leaves $v,w$ free: an exceptional plane. “Divisor” does not mean the set must be straight.

The blowup keeps the dimension of the surrounding space. It replaces the old point with a space of directions. A curve or surface inside that space uses only the directions along which its own points can approach the center. Figure 10 makes that distinction visible.

6. Hypersurfaces vs. varieties: keeping dimensions straight

Key terms: ambient dimension, hypersurface, intrinsic dimension, regular locus.

Before exploring higher-dimensional singularities, it is vital to keep two concepts distinct: the ambient dimension (the number of coordinates in the ambient Euclidean space) and the intrinsic dimension of the geometric variety.

One nonzero equation $F(x,y,z,w)=0$ defines a hypersurface of dimension 3 in ambient dimension 4 (over $\mathbb C$, these are complex dimensions). At points where $\nabla F\ne0$, the implicit function theorem gives a tangent 3-plane. The cone shown here has intrinsic real dimension 2 in $\mathbb R^3$. Its apex is singular. The counterexamples below have intrinsic dimensions 4 and 3, with embeddings in seven and six coordinates respectively.

A real section or projection is a way to draw part of such a space. It can reveal a pinch, but it cannot prove that a complex variety is smooth or that an iteration terminates. For those claims we need the coordinate algebra.

7. Replacing the apex by directions

Key terms: direction chart, total transform, strict transform, exceptional locus.

To blow up the origin of real three-space $\mathbb{R}^3$, we introduce projective direction coordinates. In the affine chart where the $Z$-direction is nonzero, we make the substitution:

X = uv, \qquad Y = uw, \qquad Z = u.

Away from $u = 0$, the new coordinates simply record the slopes: $v = X/Z$ and $w = Y/Z$. The coordinate $u$ acts as a signed scaling factor along each line. Substituting into the cone equation gives the total transform:

X^2 + Y^2 - Z^2 = u^2(v^2 + w^2 - 1) = 0.

The total transform factors into two geometric pieces: the ambient exceptional divisor $u = 0$ (the fiber sitting over the origin, with multiplicity 2 in the total transform) and the strict transform:

v^2 + w^2 = 1, \qquad u \in \mathbb{R}.

In $(v,w,u)$ space, this is a standard smooth circular cylinder! Its gradient $\nabla(v^2+w^2-1) = (2v, 2w, 0)$ never vanishes on the surface, meaning standard calculus is completely restored.

A natural parametrization of this cylinder is given by:

(v, w, u) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, u) \quad\longmapsto\quad (X, Y, Z) = (u\cos\theta, u\sin\theta, u).

At $u = 0$, every direction $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ maps down to $(0,0,0)$. These are distinct points of the new cylinder, each labelled by its own angle. Their common image is the old apex. Away from $u=0$, the blowdown map is a real-analytic diffeomorphism onto the cone.

Which exceptional divisor? In the ambient $\mathbb{R}^3$ blowup, the fiber over the origin is $\mathbb{RP}^2$ (the space of all lines through the origin). Restricting to the cone, the exceptional locus is the projective conic $[X:Y:Z]$ satisfying $X^2+Y^2=Z^2$, whose real points form a circle. Because every real line on this cone has $Z \neq 0$, this single chart covers the entire real exceptional circle.

8. Why Nash iteration can fail—and how resolution still succeeds

Key terms: Gauss graph, Nash blowup, normalization, recurring affine chart.

Nash’s construction starts on the smooth locus of an embedded $d$-dimensional variety. Attach its tangent $d$-plane, a point of the Grassmannian $\operatorname{Gr}(d,N)$, and take the closure:

\operatorname{Nash}(X) = \overline{\big\{(p, T_p X) : p \in X_{\mathrm{smooth}}\big\}} \subset X \times \operatorname{Gr}(d, N).

The construction is the closure of the tangent-space graph.[2]

The normalized Nash blowup takes one additional operation at each step: normalization, which adjoins integral algebraic functions missing from the coordinate ring. In characteristic zero, ordinary Nash iteration resolves curves; Spivakovsky proved termination for normalized surface iteration. Neither statement settles ordinary Nash iteration on all surfaces.

For the counterexample, follow one singular neighborhood through successive Nash steps. If the same singular local type returns, the rule can repeat forever. We will compare that failure with an explicit smooth model of the very same variety.

Related example: the fourfold and its real zero-level sections Figures 11–12

Castillo, Duarte, Leyton-Álvarez, and Liendo[12] exhibit a singular normal fourfold with an open chart of its Nash blowup isomorphic to the starting variety:

U \subset \operatorname{Nash}(X), \qquad U \cong X.

This is a precise obstruction to termination. Nash blowup commutes with restricting to open subsets and with isomorphisms. The same singular local type therefore survives inside every iterate. Their threefold example[11] returns after two ordinary Nash steps and starts from a non-normal variety. Normalized Nash iteration in dimension three remains open.

How exponent vectors encode the threefold and its tangent planes

The three-dimensional construction[11] begins with an affine semigroup $S \subset \mathbb{Z}^3$ generated by six vectors $h_i$, the columns of the matrix:

B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & -2 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -1 & -1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.

Each column vector represents the exponent of a monomial. The associated 3-fold variety $X(S)$ is the closure of the image of:

\phi: (\mathbf{k}^*)^3 \longrightarrow \mathbf{k}^6, \qquad (a, b, c) \longmapsto \left( a, b, c, \frac{c^2}{a^2 b}, \frac{ac}{b}, \frac{a^2 c}{b^2} \right).

How tangent planes enter this calculation. On the dense torus, the tangent-plane coordinates are Jacobian minors. Dividing by a chosen nonzero minor gives an affine Nash chart. Ratios of monomials subtract their exponent vectors: for example $x_5/x_1=\chi^{h_5-h_1}$. That is why the new lattice generators are differences, rather than arbitrarily chosen arrows.

Verify the missing function and the returning coordinate algebra

Why there is a singularity to begin with: The lattice point $u = (0, -1, 1)$ is absent from the semigroup ($u \notin S$), yet its third multiple $3u = h_4 + h_6 = (0, -3, 3)$ is present in $S$! Using the grading functional $L(x,y,z) = x + 2y + 3z$, all generators have positive integer grades, and only $h_1$ and $h_6$ have grade 1 (neither of which equals $u$). Because $S$ has missing lattice points, the semigroup is not saturated: its coordinate ring is not integrally closed, and the variety is singular.

Why two Nash blowups cannot escape: The first Nash chart introduces eight generators $g_i$. A subsequent Nash blowup produces a chart with six generators $t_i$. Their relation to the original six generators $h_i$ is an exact integer linear transformation:

B_2 = U_3 B, \qquad U_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 & 2 \\ -2 & -1 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det U_3 = 1.

Because $U_3 \in \operatorname{GL}(3, \mathbb{Z})$ is an unimodular integer matrix, it is an isomorphism of the integer lattice $\mathbb{Z}^3$. It identifies the full semigroup $T = U_3 S$ with $S$, proving that the resulting affine chart is algebraically isomorphic to the starting variety: $X(T) \cong X(S)$. Iterating Nash blowups simply reproduces this exact singular chart indefinitely.

An explicit resolution that does terminate

The Nash rule fails on this example, but resolution is still possible. Here is a computed toric resolution of this same threefold, using normalization and a regular fan refinement. It gives all four smooth charts and their maps to the original six coordinates. This is a resolution of the variety; it is not presented as the canonical sequence of ambient weighted centers produced by the general Abramovich–Temkin–Włodarczyk algorithm.

Verify all four charts: normalization and the fan calculation

Now work in the dual lattice $N$, where a vector $n$ records the vanishing order $\langle h_i,n\rangle$ of the coordinate monomial $x_i$. Solving the six inequalities $\langle h_i,n\rangle\ge0$ gives the four primitive rays:

n_1=(0,0,1),\quad n_2=(0,1,2),\quad n_3=(1,0,1),\quad n_4=(1,2,2).

The polygon below is a flat cross-section of this cone, not the physical variety. First normalize $S$ to $\operatorname{cone}(S)\cap\mathbb Z^3$. Then triangulate along $\langle n_1,n_4\rangle$. One cone has determinant 1 in absolute value; the other has determinant 2. The latter contains the primitive lattice vector:

n_5=(1,1,2)=\tfrac12(n_1+n_3+n_4).

Subdivide that cone around $n_5$. The final cones are $124$, $135$, $145$, and $345$ (indices of the $n_i$); their determinants are $-1,+1,-1,+1$. Thus every chart is a smooth $\mathbb A^3$. This is the regular-fan resolution criterion[13], applied here with explicit integer arithmetic.

Compute the normalization, not just its name

On the original dense torus, adjoin $U=c/b$ and $V=c/a$, with exponents $u=(0,-1,1)$ and $v=(-1,0,1)$. Both are integral: $U^3=x_4x_6$ and $V^2=x_2x_4$. The saturated semigroup has generators $h_1,h_2,h_4,h_6,u,v$. In particular $x_3=x_2U$ and $x_5=x_1U$, so the original ring sits inside this six-generator normal ring.

To check completeness, split the exponent cone into $\langle h_1,h_2,h_4\rangle$ and $\langle h_1,h_4,h_6\rangle$. Their determinants are 2 and 3. The only nonzero lattice points in their half-open fundamental parallelepipeds are $v$, $u$, and $2u$. Every lattice point in either cone is one of these representatives plus a nonnegative integer sum of its ray generators. Thus adjoining $U,V$ generates the whole saturation.

Why the chart cover is a resolution

For a final cone with basis vectors $(n_i,n_j,n_k)$ and coordinates $(r,s,t)$, set $x_\ell=r^{\langle h_\ell,n_i\rangle}s^{\langle h_\ell,n_j\rangle}t^{\langle h_\ell,n_k\rangle}$. All exponents are nonnegative integers. Substitution satisfies the five original equations identically. Determinant $\pm1$ makes the torus map invertible with integer monomial exponents. The cones cover the original cone, with unchanged boundary faces, so the charts glue to a proper birational morphism from a smooth variety, unchanged over the original smooth locus.

How this relates to the ATW weighted algorithm

Abramovich, Temkin, and Włodarczyk[9] construct a functorial embedded resolution using stack-theoretic weighted blowups. Their centers come from recursively computed coefficient ideals and maximal contact, not just the exponents of one polynomial in arbitrary coordinates. Smooth stack charts can have finite quotient identifications; passing to a smooth scheme requires further work such as destackification.

The calculation above instead exploits this example's toric presentation. It supplies a complete smooth model and its maps explicitly, but does not compute or assert the ATW center sequence in the six-dimensional ambient space. Nor does it assert termination of normalized Nash iteration: our subdivisions were chosen by the toric resolution procedure.

Why this proves failure. Nash blowup restricts to open subsets and respects isomorphisms. Once an open chart of the starting singular type returns after two steps, the same calculation applies inside that chart again. Thus every even iterate contains a singular open subset. The ordinary Nash iteration cannot become smooth. Hironaka's theorem uses a different, controlled sequence of smooth centers; it is not contradicted.

Why the other resolution succeeds. The Nash construction above is forced to reproduce the same semigroup. Our toric procedure instead normalizes it and subdivides its cone until each chart has an integer coordinate basis. Determinant $\pm1$ is a verifiable stopping test. General Hironaka-style algorithms use richer invariants to choose centers and prove termination without requiring a toric presentation.

A blowup can improve a singularity without finishing it; a canonical modification can even repeat it. Hironaka’s achievement is the existence of a controlled finite sequence that reaches the required endpoint.

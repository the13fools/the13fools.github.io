What is… a blowup? · Part III of IV

From a singular fit to a useful blowup

One tensor model, two blowup charts, and a reason to care about parameter volume.

Here is a direct use for the geometry: understanding a fit whose error grows quartically in one parameter direction. We will build one small model, locate its degeneracy, and use two blowup charts to expose the loss and volume powers. Part IV explains what those powers mean for statistics.

The singularity is in the map from parameters to predictions. The data themselves can be an ordinary array of numbers. This distinction matters: resolving that parameter geometry helps us analyze a fit; it does not change the observations or train the model for us.

A tensor fit with a hidden flat direction

Can two components change substantially while their combined prediction barely moves? We will fit a small array of measurements and compare two ways to perturb an exact fit. The cancellation will locate the singularity in parameter space.

Build a tensor from three vectors

A three-way tensor is an array $T_{ijk}$. Each index selects a different slot in a measurement: for example, person, test, and occasion. “Three-way” counts the indices; it does not make the array a surface in physical three-space.

A rank-one term predicts each entry by multiplying three vector entries: $A_{ijk}=u_i v_j w_k$. Fixing $k$ gives a matrix $uv^\mathsf T$ scaled by $w_k$. A CP decomposition adds several such terms to approximate the data. This is the outer-product construction in Rabanser, Shchur, and Günnemann, §§3.1 and 4.1[34].

Our toy has eight entries, with $i,j,k\in\{0,1\}$. We deliberately tie the three vectors in each term together, $v(t)=(1,t)$, and fix both weights at $1/2$. Only the two scalars $a,b$ can change:

A_{ijk}=\tfrac12v_i(a)v_j(a)v_k(a),\quad B_{ijk}=\tfrac12v_i(b)v_j(b)v_k(b),\quad T=A+B.

What is the fitting problem? Match the fixed data $T^*_{000}=1$, with all seven other entries zero. At $a=b=0$, each weighted term is $T^*/2$; their sum is exactly $T^*$. The loss is $K=\frac13\sum_{ijk}(T_{ijk}-T^*_{ijk})^2$. The factor $1/3$ makes the quartic coefficient below equal to one; it does not change the vanishing order. The target needs only one rank-one term, although our model allows two. At the exact fit those terms coincide in direction. This constrained two-parameter family isolates a component collision; it is smaller than a general CP model.

All eight entries in one table

To see why pulling apart produces so little error, change coordinates to the mean $s=(a+b)/2$ and separation $d=(a-b)/2$. Moving together varies $s$; pulling apart varies $d$. Expanding the tensor entries reveals a fundamental cancellation:

  • Entries with one index equal to 1: $\tfrac{1}{2}(a+b) = s$.
  • Entries with two indices equal to 1: $\tfrac{1}{2}(a^2+b^2) = s^2+d^2$.
  • The entry with three indices equal to 1: $\tfrac{1}{2}(a^3+b^3) = s^3+3sd^2$.

Look at what happens along the separation direction ($s=0$): the linear terms $+d$ and $-d$ cancel out identically to first order! The prediction shifts only at second order ($d^2$). And because loss is squared prediction error, the resulting loss grows only as $(d^2)^2 = d^4$!

K(s,d)=s^2+(s^2+d^2)^2+\tfrac13(s^3+3sd^2)^2 \asymp s^2 + d^4.

Now evaluate the Hessian matrix at $(s, d) = (0, 0)$:

H = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial s^2} & \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial s \partial d} \\ \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial d \partial s} & \frac{\partial^2 K}{\partial d^2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.

The Hessian correctly reports zero quadratic curvature in $d$, but it does not describe the quartic rise. Higher-order terms still matter. This is a restricted family: we fixed the amplitudes and tied together each component’s three factor vectors. Its local exponent is not the exponent of every rank-two tensor model.

Where the blowups enter

The troublesome local loss has the same leading scaling as $K_0=s^2+d^4$. It has an isolated zero, so there is no crossing curve to separate. The problem is that a quadratic approximation loses the $d$ direction. We want coordinates that expose the higher-order vanishing without losing track of the amount of parameter space.

Perform two ordinary point blowups, keeping the chart that contains the degenerate direction:

  1. First record a slope: set $s=dv_1$. The loss becomes $K_0=d^2(v_1^2+d^2)$. The exceptional line is $d=0$. After extracting $d^2$, the remaining factor still vanishes at $(d,v_1)=(0,0)$.
  2. Blow up that remaining point: set $d=r$, $v_1=rv$. Combining the substitutions gives $s=r^2v$, $d=r$, and $K_0=r^4(v^2+1)$. On a bounded real $v$-chart, the factor $v^2+1$ never vanishes. The difficult vanishing is now a single coordinate power.

The new exceptional line $r=0$ consists of different records $(0,v)$ that all map back to the perfect fit $(s,d)=(0,0)$. There is no new line of perfect fits in the old parameter space. The map is invertible for $r\ne0$ in this chart.

Area changes too. The Jacobian of $(r,v)\mapsto(s,d)=(r^2v,r)$ has absolute determinant $|r|^2$, so:

e^{-n(s^2+d^4)}\,ds\,dd =e^{-nr^4(v^2+1)}|r|^2\,dr\,dv.

Near the exceptional line, substantial weight lies at $r$ of order $n^{-1/4}$. A radial width $n^{-1/4}$ times the area factor $r^2$ gives $n^{-3/4}$. The other charts cover the remaining directions; the direct integral in Part IV checks the same exponent over the full square.

Does the actual tensor loss have the same behavior?

Yes. Under the same two blowups, its exact loss becomes:

K=r^4\!\left[v^2+(1+r^2v^2)^2+\tfrac13r^4v^2(3+r^2v^2)^2\right].

The bracket is positive and bounded away from zero near any bounded piece of $r=0$. The Jacobian remains $|r|^2$. Thus the higher-order terms preserve the local exponent. The mean–separation coordinate change from $(a,b)$ has a constant nonzero Jacobian and does not alter it.

This is the connection to Hironaka: blowups can turn complicated vanishing into coordinate powers, with corresponding powers in the volume form. Those powers make a hard integral calculable. The goal here is monomializing a loss, rather than smoothing a visible surface.

Optional: other meanings of “singularity”

The experiments below motivated the essay, but they ask different questions. A contact law needs limiting histories; a tensor approximation may lack an attainable optimum; a field can have a topological obstruction. Read these as separate case studies, rather than steps needed for the learning-theory argument.

Mechanical side trip: simultaneous impacts and inelastic collapse 2 interactive demos

Rigid-body collisions: the opening demo, unpacked

The three balls from Part I provide our first mechanical blowup. In an elastic collision between two balls of masses $m_1, m_2$, conservation of momentum and kinetic energy uniquely determines the outgoing velocities:

m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2 = m_1 v_1' + m_2 v_2', \qquad m_1 v_1^2 + m_2 v_2^2 = m_1 v_1'^2 + m_2 v_2'^2.

For an approaching pair, momentum and energy conservation together with the outgoing-contact condition select the usual elastic impact. With three simultaneous contacts, conservation alone leaves multiple outgoing possibilities. A contact model or a limiting procedure must provide additional information.

Configuration space in a wedge: Write the gaps between neighbors as $g_1 = x_2 - x_1 - 2r \ge 0$ and $g_2 = x_3 - x_2 - 2r \ge 0$. In mass-weighted coordinates $q_i = \sqrt{m_i} x_i$, kinetic energy is standard Euclidean $\frac{1}{2}|\dot{q}|^2$. Removing uniform translation reduces the three-ball system to a single point bouncing inside a planar wedge between the constraint walls $g_1 = 0$ and $g_2 = 0$, with wedge angle:

\cos\beta = \sqrt{\frac{m_1 m_3}{(m_1 + m_2)(m_2 + m_3)}}.

A sequence of pairwise collisions is a billiard path bouncing between the walls. Simultaneous impact is the corner $g_1=g_2=0$; the equation $g_1g_2=0$ describes the union of both walls.

What the bar keeps. Fix the incoming velocities and vary the ratio of two small positive gaps. The pairwise impact model gives intervals with common limiting outcomes. Equal masses give the same outgoing velocity pattern for this experiment; a light middle ball can give different patterns. The interval sizes become probabilities only after choosing a distribution of gap ratios. They are not probabilities supplied by a blowup theorem.

A different pathology: border rank and the fit that never finishes

Key terms: rank-one tensor, border rank, Veronese variety, tangent limit.

The collision above occurred at a finite point in parameter space. A completely different pathology occurs when a target tensor is approachable only by factors that diverge to infinity. Fitting a target $W$ with two rank-one components minimizes:

\text{minimize}\;\; E = \sum_{ijk}\big(a_{1i}b_{1j}c_{1k} + a_{2i}b_{2j}c_{2k} - W_{ijk}\big)^2.

For $W_{001}=W_{010}=W_{100}=1$ and all other entries zero, the tensor has rank 3 but border rank 2. There are rank-two approximations arbitrarily close to it. The stored gradient-descent trajectory illustrates growing, nearly canceling components; a finite trajectory does not prove divergence. The limiting construction makes the nonattainment explicit:

\frac{(e_0+h e_1)^{\otimes3}-e_0^{\otimes3}}{h}\longrightarrow W\quad(h\to0).

Symmetric tensors as binary cubics: two direction records

Key terms: binary cubic, twisted cubic, tangent developable, cuspidal edge.

A symmetric $2 \times 2 \times 2$ tensor corresponds to a homogeneous binary cubic with four coefficients. On the chart where the leading coefficient is nonzero, normalize it to one and write $t^3 + a t^2 + b t + c$. For distinct roots, its real symmetric rank is determined by the cubic discriminant $\Delta$:

  • $\Delta > 0$: three distinct real roots, real symmetric rank 3;
  • $\Delta < 0$: one real root, real symmetric rank 2;
  • $\Delta = 0$: repeated root (tangent developable boundary);
  • Triple root $(t - r)^3$: rank one (the twisted cubic curve $(a, b, c) = (-3r, 3r^2, -r^3)$).

The boundary $\Delta = 0$ is swept out by tangent lines to the twisted cubic—a tangent developable creased along the rank-one curve into a sharp cuspidal edge $(e^2, 2e^3)$. Transverse to the rank-one locus, the boundary has a cusp. The point-blowup calculation from Part I applies along that locus: the strict transform becomes smooth first, and the exceptional boundary requires further steps. Recording the repeated root $r$ gives the smooth Nash lift for this cuspidal boundary. This is a special example, not a termination argument for general tensors.

Cross fields: topology constrains what can be repaired

Key terms: cross field, 4-RoSy field, Poincaré–Hopf, Ginzburg–Landau vortex, Burer–Monteiro.

A quad mesh needs a cross field: four directions spaced by quarter turns at each regular point. Tangent boundary alignment on a disk forces total cross-field index $+1$. The representation $u=e^{4i\theta}$ has boundary degree 4. Four simple index-$+1/4$ defects realize that total; they can also merge into a higher-index defect. Topology fixes the total index, not a universal lower bound of four distinct points.

Energy breakdown

Core structure and energy: Standard numerical optimizers minimize the Ginzburg–Landau energy:

E_\varepsilon(u) = \int_D \left( \frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^2 + \frac{1}{4\varepsilon^2}(1 - |u|^2)^2 \right) dA.

Here $u$ is a complex representation of the cross, not the cross angle itself. Away from the cores, $|u|\approx1$; at a zero, its argument—and hence the cross direction—is undefined. For an isolated axisymmetric degree-$d$ vortex, the leading energy is $\pi d^2\log(1/\varepsilon)$. This favors splitting a degree-4 core into four degree-1 cores when the boundary and interactions permit it.

Three strategies for frame fields:

  • Ginzburg–Landau Penalty: Allows the field amplitude $|u|$ to soften to zero at the core. The penalty parameter $\varepsilon$ controls the core size.
  • Drill a hole (Blowup): Changes the domain to an annulus: the winding can live on the inner boundary. Shrinking a circular boundary while retaining its angle is related to a real oriented blowup. A finite drilled hole is not an algebraic resolution.
  • Lift to a sphere (Burer–Monteiro): Changes the target to unit vectors $v\in S^2$. The field can leave the equator; at a pole its planar projection is zero, so projecting back still leaves an undefined cross direction. This relaxation can improve optimization without removing the original topological obstruction.
